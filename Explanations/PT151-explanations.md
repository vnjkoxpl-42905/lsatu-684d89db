# Section 2 (Logical Reasoning)

## S2 · Q1 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Ullman: Plato argued that because of the harmful ways in which music can manipulate the emotions, societies need to put restrictions on the music their citizens hear. However, because musicians seek not to manipulate the emotions but to create beauty, this argument is misguided.

### Prompt
Ullman's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it fails to consider the possibility that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Plato's Evidence:** Music can manipulate the emotions in harmful ways.
- **Plato's Conclusion:** Societies need to put restrictions on the music their citizens hear.
- **Ullman's Evidence:** Musicians seek not to manipulate the emotions but to create beauty.
- **Ullman's Conclusion:** Plato's argument is misguided.
- **Evaluate:** The final sentence in the stimulus follows a common form:

  Premise Indicator - Premise - Comma - Conclusion

  The premise indicator "because" at the beginning of the second sentence is followed by a premise. That premise is followed by a comma, which is followed by the argument's conclusion.

  The pivot indicator "however" also helps us to identify the conclusion. A pivot indicator frequently marks the point where some initial claim or idea comes to an end and the actual argument begins. It also indicates that the actual argument is a rebuttal of the initial claim or idea.

  In this case, Ullman's argument is a rebuttal of Plato's argument.

  Plato's evidence describes something that music is capable of doing—it can manipulate emotions in harmful ways. Notice that Ullman's evidence is about what musicians intend to do: Ullman states that they don't seek to manipulate emotions.

  Ullman's argument is flawed because their evidence addresses a completely different issue than Plato's. Plato could easily say, "look, Ullman, it doesn't matter what musicians seek to do. I'm talking about what music actually does. My argument isn't misguided at all."
- **Goal:** We'll expect an answer to focus on the difference between Plato's evidence about what music is actually capable of doing and Ullman's objection, which focuses on musician's intentions. This question stem states that a correct answer should describe a possibility that the argument fails to consider, so the correct answer should be worded as an objection to Ullman's reasoning, something that would harm Ullman's argument. Beware of answers that would support Ullman's argument.

**Answer choices**
**A. what musicians intend their music to do and what it actually does are different** — Correct (96% of students)
This describes the significant gap between Plato's argument, which is based on what music actually does, and Ullman's rebuttal, which is based on what musicians intend their music to do.

**B. those with the power to censor music would not censor other forms of expression** — Bad Objection (0% of students)
Both Plato's argument and Ullman's rebuttal focus directly on music. "Other forms of expression" aren't relevant. This answer is trying to support Plato's conclusion while ignoring the evidence that Plato uses as support. It also ignores the actual reasoning that Ullman uses.

If you chose this answer, you might have had a general sense that Ullman is disagreeing with Plato without understanding the exact reasoning in one or both of their arguments. Take a look at our explanation and evaluation of the stimulus if this isn't totally clear yet.

**C. there are other, more convincing arguments for allowing the censorship of music** — Bad Objection (1% of students)
The fact that there are "other, more convincing arguments" doesn't weaken Ullman's argument. Like one of the other incorrect answers, this answer seems to offer support for Plato's conclusion while ignoring the actual reasoning that Ullman uses.

If you chose this answer, you might have had a general sense that Ullman is disagreeing with Plato without understanding the exact reasoning in one or both of their arguments. Take a look at our explanation and evaluation of the stimulus if this isn't totally clear yet.

**D. other forms of art have more potential to be harmful to society than music has** — Bad Objection (1% of students)
Even if other forms of art have more potential to be harmful, that doesn't impact Ullman's argument or Plato's. If anything, this answer choice looks like an attempt to weaken Plato's argument. That's not what we're being asked to do. We're looking for an answer that weakens Ullman's argument.

**E. artists who are trying to manipulate people's emotions to control them are not likely to admit it** — Bad Objection (3% of students)
This answer looks like it's trying to call Ullman's evidence into question, but it's not actually doing that. This answer would be tempting if Ullman's premise was, "the vast majority of musicians have stated that they seek to create beauty and not to manipulate emotions." But that's not what Ullman says.

## S2 · Q2 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Physician: A tax on saturated fat, which was intended to reduce consumption of unhealthy foods, has been repealed after having been in effect for only seven months. The tax was apparently having some undesirable and unintended consequences, encouraging people to travel to neighboring countries to purchase certain foods, for example. Nonetheless, the tax should not have been repealed so soon.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the physician's conclusion regarding the tax?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The tax on saturated fat (intended to reduce consumption of unhealthy foods) should not have been repealed so soon.
- **Evidence:** It was only around for seven months. Apparently, it was having some unintended consequences (people were just traveling to nearby countries to buy the taxed foods).
- **Evaluate:** This is a bit weird, because there isn't actually a premise, other than "it's only been around for seven months". The other fact presented is counterevidence — the fact that the plan is backfiring and driving people to buy their unhealthy food from a neighboring country would be a reason to get rid of the tax.

  So, we can't do much to predict the correct answer, other than say, "If it's only been in effect for seven months, then we shouldn't have repealed it so soon."
- **Goal:** Look for any answer providing a rule that would apply to this tax and would allow us to conclude something like "It was repealed too soon" / "it was premature to repeal it" / "we should have let it stay on the books for longer" / etc.

**Answer choices**
**A. A tax on unhealthy foods should be implemented only if it can be known with a high degree of certainty that it will actually improve people's health.** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
This is a principle about whether or not a tax should be implemented in the first place. We need a principle about whether a tax that was already implemented should be repealed so soon.

**B. It is not possible to adequately gauge the impact of a tax intended to affect people's health until the tax has been in effect for at least one year.** — Correct (95% of students)
Is this applicable to our tax? Was it a tax intended to affect people's health?

Yes, it was intended to reduce consumption of unhealthy foods. So this principle applies to our situation. What is it saying about our situation?

It says, "If the tax hasn't been in effect for at least one year, then it's impossible to adequately gauge its impact."

We were looking for

```
if only been in effect for seven months  →  shouldn't repeal it yet
```

This is basically giving us

```
if only been in effect for seven months  →  impossible to gauge its impact
```

So it will strengthen if we feel pretty good about this connection:

```
if impossible to gauge the impact of a new tax yet  →  should not repeal it yet
```

Sure, that seems like a fair common sense move to make. This type of correct answer is more like a correct answer on Strengthen, whereas it's very common for correct answers on Strengthen + Principle to feel more like Sufficient Assumption.

**C. Before any law intended to improve people's health is implemented, all foreseeable negative consequences should be carefully considered.** — Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
This is a principle about what to do before a tax is initially implemented. This principle would only allow us to scold the people who wrote the law, for what they may have failed to do while writing the law.

We need a principle that allows us to scold the people who repealed the law too soon.

**D. A law intended to improve people's health should be repealed if it is clear that most people are evading the law.** — Opposite of Goal (2% of students)
This is saying, "If most people are evading the law, we should repeal it".

First problem — we don't know if most (more than 50%) of people are evading the law.

Bigger problem — our goal here is to support the conclusion that they shouldn't have repealed it so soon. This is a rule that allows one to conclude that you should repeal something.

**E. A tax on unhealthy foods should be applied only to those foods that are widely believed to be the most unhealthy.** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
This is a principle about whether or not a tax like this should have been implemented in the first place. We need a principle about whether a tax that was already implemented should be repealed so soon.

## S2 · Q3 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Legislator: A foreign company is attempting to buy FerroMetal, a domestic iron-mining company. We should prohibit this sale. Since manufacturing is central to our economy, we need a dependable supply of iron ore. If we allow a foreign company to buy FerroMetal, we will have no grounds to stop foreign companies from buying other iron-mining companies. Soon foreigners will control most of the iron mining here, leaving our manufacturers at their mercy. The end result will be that our manufacturers will no longer be able to rely on a domestic supply of iron ore.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the reasoning of the legislator's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (should):** We should prohibit this sale (of our domestic iron-mining company FerroMetal to a foreign company).
- **Intermediate Conclusion:** We need a dependable supply of iron ore.
- **Evidence (since):** Manufacturing is central to our economy.

  If a foreign company buys FerroMetal, we will have no grounds to stop foreign companies from buying other iron-mining companies.

  Then foreigners will control most iron mining, leaving our manufacturers at their mercy.

  Finally, our manufacturers will no longer be able to rely on a domestic supply of iron ore.
- **Evaluate:** This argument seems to have a couple intermediate conclusions, as we have a "since X, Y" construction, as well as a "the end result will be" construction. Both of those constructions can be thought of as causal or reasoning, depending on the context.

  With so many ideas, it's hard to know where to begin or how to organize. It might be best to do a second read, claim-by-claim, to think about where it seems to go off the rails / where we'd want to object.

  The first two sentences give us the background and Conclusion, so we know that we will be pretending to inhabit the role of Opposing Counsel and make a case that "We should allow the sale of this domestic iron-mining company to a foreign company".

  Maybe we're lovers of the free market and think that private companies should be allowed to do whatever they wish without government intrusion.

  The 3rd sentence is saying, "since manufacturing is central, we need a reliable supply of iron ore". How does that connect to the conclusion? The author must be thinking that if we sell off a domestic iron mining company to a foreign company, it will threaten our ability to have a reliable supply of iron ore.

  We might already think of objections such as
  - what if we already have plenty of iron ore, or this company is really small and un-impactful, so selling off this company would have no big impact on our supply of iron ore?
  - why is it bad for a foreign company to control a company that mines some of our domestic iron ore? Will they not sell it to us? Won't it still be part of our supply of iron ore?

  The 4th sentence sounds like a really dubious idea in and of itself:
  If we let FerroMetal go to a foreign company, then we will have no grounds to stop other foreign companies from similar purchases?

  You won't? Why not? Why are you so convinced that as soon as you set this precedent, there's no way to put the gene back in the bottle?

  The 5th claim also sounds like a big jump:
  Soon foreigners will control most of the iron mining here.

  Really, soon, most will be under foreign control? Are their tons of foreign firms waiting on the sidelines to pounce as soon as we break the seal by selling FerroMetal?
- **Goal:** It's pretty hard to limit our focus here to any one specific objection. The author seems to make a lot of questionable moves that are vulnerable to objections.

  We might do best to remember our Anti-Conclusion perspective, "we don't need to prohibit this sale; it's okay to allow a foreign company to buy FerroMetal" and then deal with answers as they come, once we see what part of this argument they're focused on.

**Answer choices**
**A. The argument draws a conclusion that simply restates a premise presented in support of that conclusion.** — Wrong Flaw: not Circular (1% of students)
This answer describes the famous Circular reasoning flaw. The conclusion here was "we should prohibit this sale".

Was there also a premise that said "we should prohibit this sale"?

No, that idea wasn't expressed twice.

**B. The argument takes for granted that what is true of one particular industry is true of industry in general.** — Wrong Flaw: not Part to Whole (1% of students)
This argument describes the famous flaw Part vs. Whole.

Does the author provide a premise about one particular industry (iron-mining, in this case) and then give a conclusion about industry in general?

No, the conclusion is about the specific sale of FerroMetal within the particular industry of iron-mining.

**C. The argument defends a practice solely on the grounds that the practice is widely accepted.** — Bad Conclusion/Premise Match (0% of students)
Is the author's conclusion defending a practice? No it's suggesting we prohibit a practice.

Is the author's evidence (on the grounds that) saying that something is widely accepted? No, there are like six premises and none of them say that.

This type of move, "Because most people think X is true, X must be true" is similar to the famous Inappropriate Appeal to Opinion flaw.

**D. The argument presents a chain of possible consequences of a given event as if it were the only possible chain of consequences of that event.** — Correct (95% of students)
Does the author present a chain of possible consequences for a given event?

Yes, starting with the 4th sentence, our author says,
"If we allow the foreign company to buy FerroMetal, this given event will launch this chain of consequences ...
- no grounds to stop foreign companies from buying other companies
- soon, foreigners control most iron mining
- end result is that our manufacturers will no longer be able to rely on a domestic supply of iron ore."

Is our author presenting those consequences as if they are the only possible chain of consequences, like those consequences are certain to happen?

Yes, actually. the author never hedges his wording, as though these things might / could / probably will happen. He presents them with certainty.

We could call this flaw the Slippery Slope flaw, in which someone says, "no, we can't do that because it will start a cascade of things we don't want. We can't socialize medicine by having single-payer healthcare because that's a slippery slope towards socialism."

**E. The argument concludes that one event would cause a second event even though the second event would have to precede the first.** — Weak Conclusion Match / Bad Evidence Match (2% of students)
The main conclusion is not causal at all. It's normative: "we should prohibit this sale". So on a quick look, we should bail from this answer after we read that first half.

But sometimes Flaw answer choices will use the verb 'concludes', not in reference to the Main Conclusion but in reference to the author's thought process in getting to the main conclusion (even as a synonym for assumes).

The author is definitely assuming (mentally concluding) that selling FerroMetal to a foreign company would cause a series of events (we can't stop other foreign companies from buying iron companies / foreigners control most of iron mining / domestic manufacturers don't have reliable supply to depend on).

But it would be crazy for us to say about any of those events that they "would have to precede (come before) FerroMetal gets sold to a foreign company".

## S2 · Q4 — Principle-Conform

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Food company engineer: I stand by my decision to order the dumping of small amounts of chemicals into the local river even though there is some evidence that this material may pose health problems. I fish in the river myself and will continue to do so. Furthermore, I will have no problem if other food manufacturers do what our company does.

### Prompt
The engineer's reasoning most closely conforms to which one of the following principles?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** I stand by my decision to order the dumping of small amounts of chemicals into the local river, even though there's some evidence they may pose health problems.
- **Evidence:** I fish in the river myself and will continue to do so. And I have no problem if other food manufacturers do what our company does.
- **Evaluate:** The author is essentially saying, "it was okay for me to decide that our company would dump some potentially harmful chemicals into the local river".

  Why is it okay?
  1. Golden Rule — I won't object if other companies do the same thing
  2. Put My Money Where My Mouth Is — I'm not afraid of what these chemicals will do to the river, as evidenced by the fact that I love this river and use it and will continue to eat things that come out of it.
- **Goal:** Let's look for something like, "If you're okay with other people doing questionable action X, then it's okay for you to do it."

  Or something like, "If you're willing to deal with the potential negative consequences of questionable action X, then it's okay for you do do it."

**Answer choices**
**A. One is justified in performing an act if other people are also planning to perform that kind of act.** — Bad Trigger Match (2% of students)
This rule would look like this:

```
if other people are planning to do something like X  →  then it's okay to do X
```

The outcome matches the Conclusion well enough. But was there a premise saying that other people are planning to dump small amounts of chemicals into the local river?

No, it didn't say anyone was actually planning to do so. The evidence was just saying "IF someone decided to do so, the author wouldn't object".

**B. One should always choose to act in a way that will benefit the greatest number of people.** — Bad Trigger Match (0% of students)
This rule would look like this:

```
if action X would benefit the greatest number of people  →  then you should do X
```

The outcome doesn't match the Conclusion that well. Our author's conclusion is that "I stand by my decision", meaning "it's defensible / it's okay". This seems to go one step farther by saying "we should do it".

But the more glaring disconnect is the trigger. We definitely never heard that "dumping small amounts of chemicals into the local river" would benefit the greatest number of people.

**C. One is justified in performing an act if one is willing to submit oneself to the consequences of that action performed by oneself or others.** — Correct (98% of students)
This rule would look like this:

```
if you're willing to submit yourself to the consequences of doing X or having someone else do X  →  then it's okay to do X
```

The outcome matches the Conclusion well enough. Was the author saying that she is willing to submit herself to the consequences of dumping small amounts of chemicals into the local river?

Yes! She was saying that she fishes in the river and will continue to do so. If dumping chemicals in the river actually taints the river water or poisons the fish in there, then someone fishing in the river will potentially be exposed to that harm. And she says she will continue to fish in the river.

Was she saying she's willing to submit herself to the consequences of someone else dump small amounts of chemicals into the local river?

Yes, she said she'd have no problem if other food manufacturers do what this company is doing (dumping small amounts of chemicals).

**D. One should never perform an act until one has fully analyzed all the ways in which that act could impact others.** — Bad Outcome Match (0% of students)
This rule would look like this:

```
if you haven't fully analyzed all the ways an act could impact others  →  then it's not okay to do X
```

The author's conclusion is that is was okay to dump chemicals. This principle would only help someone conclude that they should not have performed an action yet.

**E. One has the right to perform an act as long as that act does not harm anyone else.** — Bad Trigger Match (0% of students)
This rule would look like this:

```
if action X isn't going to harm anyone else  →  then you have the right to do X
```

The outcome matches the Conclusion well enough. But was there a premise saying that dumping small amounts of chemicals into the local river will not harm anyone else?

No, it actually says that there's some evidence that this material may pose health problems.

## S2 · Q5 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Political strategist: Clearly, attacking an opposing candidate on philosophical grounds is generally more effective than attacking the details of the opponent's policy proposals. A philosophical attack links an opponent's policy proposals to an overarching ideological scheme, thereby telling a story and providing context. This makes the attack emotionally compelling.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption required by the political strategist's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (clearly):** Attacking an opposing candidate on philosophical grounds in generally more effective than attacking the details of the opponent's policy proposals.
- **Evidence:** A philosophical attack links an opponent's policy proposals to an overarching ideological scheme, thereby telling a story, providing context, and making the attack emotionally compelling.
- **Evaluate:** The conclusion is a comparison between two different strategies, and the author is ultimately trying to say that one strategy is generally more effective than the other.

  The stuff we hear about the supposedly better strategy is that it links an opponent's policies to an overarching ideology, thereby telling a story and providing context, and thereby making the attack emotionally compelling.

  Since there is a chain of causality or reasoning there, we can suspect that LSAT is testing us on where that chain ends.

  Ultimately, we're hearing that attacking on philosophical grounds is more effective because it's emotionally compelling.
- **Goal:** The most likely answer would be a missing link between "emotionally compelling" and "more effective". But the author is also assuming that "linking to an ideological scheme" is connected to being more effective, and that "telling a story and providing context" is connected to being more effective.

**Answer choices**
**A. The stories that people are most likely to remember are those that are emotionally compelling.** — Out of Scope: most likely to remember (3% of students)
This argument is never concerned with which stories are most likely to be remembered, so the author isn't assuming anything about that concept.

We want to see an answer talk about what would make one technique more effective than another.

**B. Political attacks that are emotionally compelling are generally more effective than those that are not.** — Correct (89% of students)
This links together where are Premises end with what our Conclusion is trying to say.

The premises tell us ultimately that a philosophical attack is emotionally compelling, and on this basis the author concludes that a philosophical attack is more effective than attacking policy details.

This answer reflects that Missing Link in the author's thinking.

If we negated this and said, "attacks that are emotionally compelling are generally not more effective", then we would badly weaken the argument.

**C. Political attacks that tell a story are able to provide more context than those that do not.** — Premise-Pretzel (2% of students)
It's always a trap answer when they take one single claim, like "thereby telling a story and providing context", and then try to make a strong relationship out of words in that claim.

The author never generalized about all political attacks that tell a story. We have no idea if she thinks they're better at providing context than those who do not.

Suppose we said, "My buddy Ted is dating this new girl Eileen, who is a law student and an avid jogger". This type of answer would say I'm assuming "People in law school are more likely to regularly jog than are people who aren't".

I'm not assuming anything like that. I just happened to use the words "law school" and "jog" in the same sentence!

**D. Voters are typically uninterested in the details of candidates' policy proposals.** — Out of Scope: uninterested / Too Strong: typically (2% of students)
The author never talked about whether more than 50% of voters are uninterested in policy details. She isn't talking about whether people are interested or not.

She's just saying that attacking your opponent on philosophical grounds is more effective than attacking your opponent's policy details. Even if people are interested in these policy details, attacking on philosophical grounds could be more effective because it's more emotionally compelling.

**E. Most candidates' policy proposals are grounded in an overarching ideological scheme.** — Too Strong: most (5% of students)
The word "most" is wrong on Necessary Assumption 98% of the time we see it. Does the author need to assume that more than 50% of policy proposals are grounded in an overarching ideological scheme?

Would the argument be badly weakened if only 49% of policy proposals are grounded in such a scheme?

Of course not, which is why the author doesn't need to assume that most are.

The author is advocating that politicians link an opponent's policy proposal to an overarching ideological scheme, but that doesn't imply that the opponent's policy proposal is grounded in such a scheme. It only assumes that at least some of the time, a politician would be able to link the opponent's proposals to an ideological scheme.

## S2 · Q6 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Michaela: I think doctors who complain about patients doing medical research on the Internet are being a little unfair. It seems only natural that a patient would want to know as much as possible about his or her condition.

Sam: It is not unfair. Doctors have undergone years of training. How can you maintain that a doctor's opinion is not worth more than something an untrained person comes up with after searching the Internet?

### Prompt
Sam's response indicates that he interpreted Michaela's remarks to mean that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Michaela's Conclusion:** It's a little unfair for doctors to complain about patients doing medical research on the internet.
- **Michaela's Evidence:** It seems natural that a patient would want to know as much as possible about their condition.
- **Sam's Conclusion:** It's not unfair for them to complain.
- **Sam's Evidence:** Doctors (unlike patients, presumably) have undergone years of training. How can you say that a doctor's opinion isn't worth more than an untrained Internet search?
- **Evaluate:** If I were Michaela, I would be immediately responding, "Whoa, there — I didn't say that! I definitely think a Doctor's opinion is worth more than an untrained person's internet search. I was just saying it's silly for doctors to be mad at patients for doing Internet searches, when it's a natural facet of human behavior to try to seek as much information as possible."
- **Goal:** Since Michaela never said "a doctor's opinion is just as valuable as the internet's opinion" and yet Sam is accusing her of having said that, we can say he misinterpreted her remarks to mean that.

  So let's look for something like "a doctor's opinion is not worth more than something an untrained person comes up with from an internet search", or an answer that would sound enough like that that Sam would think he heard that.

**Answer choices**
**A. health information found on the Internet is trustworthy** — Bad Match (2% of students)
We're looking for some rephrasing of "a doctor's opinion is not worth more than something an untrained person comes up with after searching the Internet".

Saying the information on the Internet is trustworthy would potentially support this claim, but it isn't itself this claim.

**B. the opinion of a patient who has done Internet research on his or her condition should have at least as much weight as the opinion of a doctor** — Correct (84% of students)
We're looking for some rephrasing of "a doctor's opinion is not worth more than something an untrained person comes up with after searching the Internet".

This seems to have a lot of the right language. Are they just flipping the syntax of the inequality? By that I mean, going from "Kevin is shorter than Paul" to "Paul is taller than Kevin", since they both represent the same inequality: Kevin < Paul

Yes. Sam heard Michaela saying "a doc's opinion is not worth more than patient's internet search". If a doc's opinion is "not worth more", then we would write that mathematically as "less than or equal to".

Doc's opinion ≤ Patient's web search

And this answer is using that same inequality, just starting with the patient as the subject, rather than the doctor. The patient's web search is greater than or equal to the doc's opinion, or as this answer says it, at least as much weight.

**C. the opinion of a patient's own doctor should not be given more weight than the opinions of doctors published on websites** — Weak Match (7% of students)
We're looking for some rephrasing of "a doctor's opinion is not worth more than something an untrained person comes up with after searching the Internet".

This is close, but it goes off the rails at the end. It compares "your doc's opinion" to "the opinions of doctors published on websites", but Sam's quote didn't make that comparison.

His was just "your doc's opinion" vs. "what a patient comes up with after searching on the Internet". That latter idea may or may not involve the opinions of doctors published on websites, so we can't swap out "what patients come up with after searching the web" for "the opinions of doctors published on websites".

**D. a doctor's explanation of a patient's symptoms should be taken more seriously than the patient's own view of his or her symptoms** — Weak Match (5% of students)
We're looking for some rephrasing of "a doctor's opinion is not worth more than something an untrained person comes up with after searching the Internet".

Instead of comparing "doctor's opinion" to "results of internet searching", this answer is comparing "doctor's explanation of symptoms" to "patient's own view".

**E. patients who do not research their conditions on the Internet give their doctors' opinions more consideration** — Bad Match (2% of students)
We're looking for some rephrasing of "a doctor's opinion is not worth more than something an untrained person comes up with after searching the Internet".

This is talking about patients who don't search the web for research on their conditions, so it can't match the quote we're looking for.

## S2 · Q7 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Principle: People should not feed wild animals because it makes them dependent on humans and less likely to survive on their own.

Situation: Bird lovers commonly feed wild birds to attract them to their yards and gardens.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if assumed, would most help to justify treating the human feeding of wild birds as an exception to the principle above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that...:** People should not feed wild animals because it makes them dependent on humans and less likely to survive on their own.
- **How can this be justified...:** Bird lovers commonly feed wild birds to attract them to their yards and gardens.
- **Evaluate / Goal:** We're looking for a reason to treat the bird lovers' feeding of wild birds as an exception. Is there a reason why these wild birds won't become dependent on humans and less likely to survive on their own? Maybe the bird lovers are only feeding birds that are already dependent on humans. This kind of answer could justify the bird lovers' actions even if we accept the principle.

**Answer choices**
**A. Congregating around human bird feeders makes wild birds more vulnerable to predators and diseases.** — Opposite (1% of students)
This supports the idea that humans endanger wild birds by feeding them.

**B. Some species of wild birds benefit humans by consuming large numbers of mosquitoes and other insect pests.** — Opposite (6% of students)
A benefit for humans doesn't justify making wild birds more dependent on humans or endangering their ability to survive. This gives us more reason to want the birds to survive in the wild and more reason to avoid harming them.

Since the conclusion is just a comparative claim between ProBit and KRV, where the other computers land is really not relevant.

- is diagnosing extroversion in neutral pictures of humans really parallel to diagnosing dominance in neutral pictures of chimps?

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Most old houses on 20th Ave contain more than one apartment.
- **Evidence:** All the apartments on 20th are in old houses, and there are twice as many apartments as there are old houses.
- **Evaluation:** This is very math-y, so using some numbers can maybe help us make sense of it. Let's start with "twice as many apartments as old houses". We'll say there are 10 apartments and 5 old houses.

  All 10 apartments are in those 5 old houses. That's an average of 2 apartments per house, so the author is reasoning that "most of these old houses" have more than one apartment.

  But does an average value have to be true of most data points within a group?

  Can a group of houses have an average of 2 apartments, even if most of those houses have less than 2 apartments?

  Sure. If you want the average value to be unlike most data points in the set, just make a couple data points be total outliers.

  Take any ten people off the street: their average wealth is probably about $50,000. Now add Jeff Bezos and Bill Gates to that group and you have a dozen people with an average wealth of about $10 billion / person. Is it true to say that most of the people in that group have more than $1 billion in wealth? No, only 2 of the 12 do. It's just that their influence skews the average very high.

  Similarly, we could allocate our 10 apartments over five old houses very unevenly:
  House 1: 1 apartment
  House 2: 1 apartment
  House 3: 1 apartment
  House 4: 3 apartments
  House 5: 4 apartments

  It would be true that they have an average of 2 apartments / house, even though most of the houses do not have more than one apartment (only 2 out of 5 have more than one).
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that calls out this mismatch between "an average" for a group and "something that is true of most members of the group". Or look for an answer raising the specific objection that the apartments might be allocated to the old houses in a very uneven way, such that there are a bunch of apartments found in a minority of the houses, with only 1 or 0 apartments found in the majority of the houses.

**Answer choices**
**A. overlooks the possibility that some of the buildings on 20th Avenue are not old houses** — Not an Objection (8% of students)
It doesn't affect this argument if some of the houses are not old. We only care about the old houses because that's where all the apartments are found and that's what the conclusion is about.

**B. draws a conclusion that simply restates one of the premises offered in support of the conclusion** — Conclusion ≠ Premise (4% of students)
The conclusion says that "most old houses have more than one apartment". Neither premise says that.

This answer describes the famous flaw Circular Reasoning, which is almost always wrong.

**C. fails to consider the possibility that some buildings on 20th Avenue may offer types of rental accommodation other than apartments** — Not an Objection (6% of students)
Just like (A), we don't care about the fact that there are also non-apartments on 20th avenue. We're just looking at apartments, which are all found in old houses, and analyzing whether there is more than one apartment in most of those old houses.

**D. confuses a condition whose presence would be sufficient to ensure the truth of the argument's conclusion with a condition whose presence is required in order for the conclusion to be true** — Bad Premise / Conclusion Match (26% of students)
Is there a condition whose presence would be sufficient to ensure that "most old houses on 20th have more than one apartment"?

No, there's no conditional logic in the argument that would deliver us the idea in the conclusion.

This answer describes the famous flaw of Necessary vs. Sufficient. There is one conditional statement in the argument:
"apartment on 20th → in an old house"

If the author had said, "All the apartments on 20th Avenue are in old houses. However, Bill does not live in an apartment on 20th Avenue. Thus Bill does not live in an old house", that would have been committing the error that (D) describes.

Our hypothetical example from before is getting at this idea.

10 apartments, 5 old houses:
House 1: 1 apartment
House 2: 1 apartment
House 3: 1 apartment
House 4: 3 apartments
House 5: 4 apartments

A significant number (2 out of 5) have 3 or more apartments, which allowed for the average to still be 2 apartments per house even though the conclusion was wrong: most of the old houses don't have more than one house.

## S2 · Q23 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A gram of the artificial sweetener aspartame is much sweeter than a gram of sugar. Soft drinks that are sweetened with sugar are, of course, sweet, so those sweetened with aspartame must be even sweeter. Thus people who regularly drink soft drinks sweetened with aspartame will develop a preference for extremely sweet products.

### Prompt
Which one of the following arguments exhibits flawed reasoning that is most similar to flawed reasoning in the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:**
```
1g of aspartame  >  1g of sugar   (Sweeter)
```
  Soda w/ sugar = sweet.
- **Intermediate Conclusion:**
```
soda w/ aspartame  >  soda w/ sugar   (Sweeter)
```
- **Conclusion:** Drinking sodas w/ aspartame will cause you to have an extreme preference for sweet things.
- **Evaluation:** Because the author draws an Intermediate Conclusion with "so" and then her ultimate conclusion with "Thus", we need to think through both moves here.

  If each gram of aspartame is sweeter than each gram of sugar, does that mean sodas with aspartame are sweeter than sodas with sugar?

  Not necessarily — who said that we're using an equal number of grams? Maybe sodas with aspartame use 3g of aspartame, but sodas with sugar use 20g of sugar.

  What about the next move — if we accepted that aspartame sodas are sweeter, do we have to accept that someone who drinks them will develop a preference for extremely sweetened things?

  Not necessarily — maybe they only like their sodas sweet. maybe the sweetness of their aspartame sodas actually turns them off from other sweetened stuff. maybe they don't even like the sweetness of the aspartame soda but they tolerate it because they have different reasons for wanting to drink that soda.
- **Goal:** Premise
  Ingredient 1 has more of property A, per unit, than does Ingredient 2.

  Intermediate Conclusion
  Something with ingredient 1 has more of property A than something with ingredient 2.

  Main Conclusion
  If you have something with ingredient 1, you'll develop a preference for lots of property A.

**Answer choices**
**A. People sometimes develop a preference for foods that they initially disliked. So if you dislike a new food, then you will eventually develop a preference for it.** — Bad Premise Match (5% of students)
There aren't any details in the premise about "ingredient 1 has more of property A, per unit, then ingredient 2", so I'd move on.

**B. Most people own more books than televisions. Moreover, it takes longer to read a book than to watch an episode of a television show. So most people must spend more time reading than they do watching television.** — Weak Logic Match (19% of students)
We could say that "Books have more Property A than TV Shows", if we thought of Property A as "time-consuming".

To mimic the author's move to Intermediate Conclusion, though, we'd need to say "Something containing books is more time-consuming than something containing TV shows".

If we had said, "Thus it would take someone longer to read all the books in their study than to watch all the shows in their Netflix queue", it would be replicating the flaw of, "Wait a sec — how many books are in the study vs. how many shows are in the Netflix queue?"

Instead, this is about how many books people own vs. how many televisions they own.

**C. Joe's piggy bank has only pennies in it, and Maria's has only nickels. Nickels are worth much more than pennies. It therefore follows that there is more money in Maria's piggy bank than in Joe's.** — Correct (53% of students)
This is a tough correct answer because it only deals with the author's flaw in drawing her subsidiary conclusion. It never gets into the final conclusion.

Nickels have more of property A (monetary value) than pennies do.
Thus, something with nickels (Maria's bank) has more property A than something with pennies (Joe's bank).

This is vulnerable to the same objection we had with the original argument:
how many grams of aspartame are in an aspartame soda vs. how many grams of sugar are in a sugary soda?
=
how many nickels are in Maria's bank vs. how many pennies are in Joe's bank?

**D. Stephanie likes hot summer weather much more than Katherine does. So the place where Stephanie grew up must have had more days of hot summer weather than the place where Katherine grew up.** — Inverts Premise And Conclusion (17% of students)
The original argument went from a Premise about exposure to sweetness to a Conclusion about developing a preference for sweetness.

This argument reverses that and goes from a Premise about preference to a Conclusion about exposure.

**E. Guillermo has a much shorter drive to work than Abdul does. So Guillermo's estimate of the average commute for workers in the country as a whole is likely to be lower than Abdul's estimate.** — Bad Conclusion Match (5% of students)
The conclusion is about making an estimate; it's hard to see how that would match either of the two conclusions.

We could say that Guillermo's commute has less of property X (drive time) than Abdul's commute does.

But then we would need a move that is saying "something with Guillermo's commute in it has more property X than something with Abdul's commute in it".

For example, "Thus, Guillermo spends more time commuting each week than Abdul does", to which we might object, "Wait a sec — how many days a week does Guillermo vs. Abdul go to work?"

## S3 · Q2 — Evaluate

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
In a study of honesty conducted in various retail stores, customers who paid in cash and received change were given an extra dollar with their change. Few people who received an extra dollar returned it. So, among those who received an extra dollar, most behaved dishonestly.

### Prompt
The answer to which one of the following questions would most help in evaluating the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** Customers who paid in cash at stores and received change were given an extra dollar with their change.

  Few people who received an extra dollar returned it.
- **Conclusion (so):** Among those who received an extra dollar, most behaved dishonestly.
- **Evaluate:** This argument attempts to explain a curious fact: when people received an extra dollar, why did so few of them return it?

  By claiming that most of these people behaved dishonestly, the argument assumes that nothing else caused these people to keep the extra dollar. There are actually a number of reasonable explanations that have nothing to do with dishonesty. The argument never addresses any of these alternative explanations. Based just on the evidence we're given, there's no way we can be sure that any of these people behaved dishonestly.

  This type of question isn't extremely common in LR, so it's important to clearly understand what the question is asking and what to expect in the answer choices. The answer choices are all questions that can be answered with a short, factual answer. These will often be questions that can be answered with "yes" or "no."

  We need to think about a possible answer to each question, and consider what effect the answer would have on the argument. For example, if the answer to one question is "yes" that might strengthen the argument; however if the answer to that same question is "no" that might weaken the argument.
- **Goal:** One answer choice will pose a question that has a noticeable impact on the argument. The answer choice which poses that question will be the correct answer choice. The other questions will have much less impact, and possibly even no impact.

**Answer choices**
**A. Did those who received an extra dollar count their change?** — Correct (97% of students)
If no one who received an extra dollar counted their money, we can't be sure that any of them knew that they received the extra money. And if they didn't know, there is no reason to believe that they were being dishonest.

If most or all of these people did count their money, it's more likely that they knew about the extra dollar. This doesn't prove that they were being dishonest, but it challenges the alternative explanation that they simply didn't know about the extra dollar.

**B. What percentage of the retail transactions studied were cash transactions?** — Irrelevant Comparison (0% of students)
The evidence is about cash transactions that were studied. It doesn't mention any non-cash transactions. But even if this was all part of a larger study that included non-cash transactions, this argument focuses solely on the cash transactions. If a massively larger number of non-cash transactions were also studied, that has no impact on this argument. We only care about the cash transactions.

If 100% of the transactions in the study were these cash transactions mentioned in the argument, that still has no impact on the evidence or how we might interpret the evidence.

**C. Would the people who returned the extra dollar describe themselves as honest?** — Irrelevant Distinction / Irrelevant Comparison (2% of students)
The conclusion focuses on the people who did not return the extra dollar and does not rely on any comparison with the people who did return the extra dollar. It doesn't matter if people who returned the extra dollar describe themselves as honest or not.

It can be easy to miss the fact that this answer is about people who did return the extra dollar. The LSAT might be trying to trick people who read this answer quickly.

**D. Did the people who returned the extra dollar suspect that it was given to them intentionally?** — Irrelevant Distinction / Irrelevant Comparison (1% of students)
It can be easy to miss the fact that this answer is about people who did return the extra dollar. The LSAT might be trying to trick people who read this answer quickly.

The conclusion focuses on the people who did not return the extra dollar and does not rely on any comparison with the people who did return the extra dollar.

If people who returned the extra dollar suspected that it was given to them intentionally, that doesn't impact the people who did not return the extra dollar.

If people who returned the extra dollar had no clue that it was given to them intentionally, that still doesn't impact the people who did not return the extra dollar.

**E. Does increasing the extra change to five dollars have an effect on people's behavior?** — Irrelevant Comparison (0% of students)
The argument's conclusion focuses on the people who were given an extra dollar and why they did not give that extra dollar back. It's strictly based on what happened when people were given that extra dollar. What happens when people are given a different amount of money is not relevant to this argument.

It's also worth noting that this answer doesn't even indicate what "effect" or how much of an "effect" an extra five dollars might have on people's behavior. The answer to this question could be, "yes, it has an effect on their behavior. The effect is that they go spend an extra $5 on something at some point." That has no impact on the argument.

## S3 · Q13 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A new screening test has been developed for syndrome Q. Research has shown that the test yields a positive for syndrome Q whenever the person tested has that syndrome. So, since Justine shows positive on the test, she must have syndrome Q.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the reasoning in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (so):** Justine must have syndrome Q.
- **Evidence (since):** Justine shows positive on this test.

  Whenever a person has syndrome Q, the test shows positive.
- **Evaluate:** Any time we're doing a Flaw questions and we see a conditional logic premise (whenever), we have a 90% or better suspicion that they're just testing their #1 famous flaw, Necessary vs. Sufficient.

  The sufficient indicator whenever (just like when, if, any, each, all, every) tells us to put the clause that follows on the left of the arrow, so we get this rule:

```
person tested has syndrome Q → test comes out positive
```

  We would not expect the author to either illegally reverse this rule or negate it.

  REVERSAL

  Bob tested positive. Thus, Bob was syndrome Q.

  NEGATION

  Bob doesn't have syndrome Q. Thus, the test will not come back positive.

  And we see that they did indeed argue via a reversal.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer calling out the illegal Necessary vs. Sufficient confusion.

  Or look for an answer making the same objection more conversationally: just because Justine tested positive doesn't guarantee she has Q. Tests sometimes provide false positives, where you think you're pregnant but you're not. You think you have covid, but you don't.

  This test apparently does not have false negatives. If you have Q, the test will say positive. But it's possible this test gives out false positives. You don't have Q, but the test says positive anyway.

**Answer choices**
**A. It confuses the claim that a subject will test positive when the syndrome is present with the claim that any subject who tests positive has the syndrome.** — Correct (90% of students)
This describes the Necessary vs. Sufficient error by comparing the conditional the author actually gave us with the conditional the author would have needed to give us in order to reach her conclusion.

She gave us, "when syndrome is present, test is positive"
Q is present → test is positive

She wanted a claim that says "any subject who tests positive has the syndrome"
test is positive → Q is present

After all, she tells us that Justine has tested positive. That doesn't trigger anything in the first rule, the one the author presented.

Another way to say all this is, "the author confuses a rule where testing positive is the Necessary condition with a rule where testing positive is the Sufficient condition".

But it triggers the 2nd rule and would allow the author to reach her conclusion

**B. It makes a general claim regarding the accuracy of the test for syndrome Q without providing adequate scientific justification for that claim.** — Out of Scope: general accuracy claim (3% of students)
There is no explicit claim made regarding the accuracy of the test, so this answer is just descriptively false.

The author is implicitly accepting the accuracy of the test, that's true. She does say that "research has shown ..." that the test yields no false negatives. So there also kind of is some scientific justification to her trust the accuracy of "if you have syndrome, then test will say positive".

**C. It fails to adequately distinguish between a person's not having syndrome Q and that person's not testing positive for syndrome Q.** — Scrambled Ideas (4% of students)
This has an overall form that could work if we inserted the right ideas into it:

This argument failed to distinguish between testing positive for Q and actually having syndrome Q (i.e. it failed to appreciate the possibility of false positives — a positive test doesn't mean for sure you have it).

This answer is saying the author failed to appreciate the possibility of false negatives — that you might not test positive but still actually have syndrome Q.

**D. It confuses a claim about the accuracy of a test for syndrome Q in an arbitrary group of individuals with a similar claim about the accuracy of the test for a single individual.** — Out of Scope: group vs. individual (2% of students)
There's no distinction anywhere in this argument between the test being accurate for groups but not for individuals, or vice versa.

This answer seems to be teasing some sort of Sampling or Part vs. Whole issue that wasn't present in the argument. Like, "even though that was true for this group, it won't necessarily be true for Justine". We can't match that up with this argument.

**E. It confuses the test's having no reliable results for the presence of syndrome Q with its having no reliable results for the absence of syndrome Q.** — Out of Scope: no reliable results (1% of students)
When a Flaw answer choice is saying the author confused X with Y, it's saying that X is what was talked about in the evidence but Y is what the author was apparently thinking in drawing her conclusion.

Neither the evidence nor the conclusion talked about "a test having no reliable results".

  New ones raise fish in visually stimulating environments, with non-dull routines.

- **Evaluate / Goal:** We're mainly looking at an assumption that "if the fish are bolder about exploring new environments and trying new food, then they are more likely to survive after their release".

**B. The quality of the environments into which hatchery-raised fish are released has little effect on the fish's survival rate.** — Too Strong: little effect (9% of students)
The author might believe that the quality of environment also has a significant effect on the fish's survival rate.

But even still, because of #1, an answer like that would still be wrong.

```
bolder about exploring new environments and trying new foods  →  more likely to survive after release
```

This answer is definitely relating "boldness about exploring" (timid in their foraging) to "survive after release" (die).

**D. Hatchery-raised fish that are released into the wild need to eat many different types of food to survive.** — Too Strong: need (29% of students)

The ones that live with both a cat and a dog are just meaningless when it comes to figuring out which way the overall number tilts (because by having both cats and dogs in the same house, those college grads won't tip the comparison in either direction).

Suppose students A, B, C, D live in cat-only houses.
E and F live in cat-and-dog houses.
G, H, I live in dog-only houses.

How many of those people live in a house with a cat? 6.
How many live in a house with a dog? 5.

The cat-and-dog house make no difference to the final comparison. We can tell that cat houses beat dog houses simply by comparing cat-only houses (A, B, C, D) to dog-only houses (G, H, I) to see that there is one more cat house than dog house.

**B. takes for granted that there are not significantly more households with a dog than ones with a cat** — Correct (49% of students)
Does the author need to assume this? Well, whenever we're looking at a Necessary Assumption answer (which is what "takes for granted / presumes / fails to establish" indicates), we know that it's tempting to see an idea being ruled-out with the word "not".

If we negate this idea, does it become an objection?

Does it hurt the argument to say, "There are significantly more dog houses than cat houses?"

Sure, it hurts. Let's say there are 100 houses with cats and 1000 houses with dogs.

47% of the 100 cat houses have college grads, so that's 47 college grads that live in a house with a cat.

38% of the 1000 dog houses have college grads, so that's 380 college grads that live in a house with a dog.

That would mean the conclusion is wrong. College grads are more likely to live in a dog house than in a cat house.

Since negating the answer choice turned it into an objection, we know that this really WAS something the author was assuming.

**C. fails to consider how many of the households have at least one person without a university degree** — No Impact (3% of students)
People without a degree are meaningless to the tally we care about for the conclusion:

how many college grads live with cats
vs.
how many college grads live with dogs

**D. fails to consider to what extent people with university degrees participate in decisions about whether their households have a cat or dog** — Out of Scope: why they have cats/dogs (6% of students)
This argument doesn't care about why these houses have a cat, dog, or both. This is only a statistical argument. The author isn't trying to come up with a causal story that accounts for why college grads may be more likely to live with one type of pet than another.

The math doesn't care whether the college grad picked the pet or whether one of her housemates did. If she lives in a house with a cat, she counts towards that tally.

**E. ignores the possibility that two things can be correlated without being causally connected** — Wrong Flaw (26% of students)
This describes the famous Causal flaw, in which the author's evidence presents a curious fact and the conclusion overconfidently posits a causal explanation for that fact.

This conclusion (as well as all the evidence) isn't causal. It's just mathematically descriptive.

## S4 · Q2 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A company decided to scan all of its salespersons' important work that existed only in paper form into a central computer database that could be easily accessed using portable computers, thereby saving salespersons the effort of lugging their paper files all over the country. The project was a dismal failure, however; salespersons rarely accessed the database and continued to rely on many paper files, which they had refused to turn over to the staff responsible for creating the database.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps to account for the failure described above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that...:** The salespersons' important work in the central computer database could be easily accessed using portable computers, thereby saving salespersons the effort of lugging their paper files all over the country.
- **How can it be that...:** Salespersons rarely accessed the database and continued to rely on many paper files, which they had refused to turn over to the staff responsible for creating the database.
- **Evaluate:** The stimulus compares the potential benefits of this project to the actual outcome. It seems like scanning the paper documents into a central database would make it easier for the salespersons to do their job. Why didn't they use the database? And why did they refuse to turn paper files over to the staff responsible for creating the database?

  The second question seems like the more important one. An answer to that question could very possibly answer the first question as well. The salespersons might have refused to turn over the files for any number of reasons, but we can imagine a couple of possibilities.

  Maybe there was some kind of office drama going on, and for some reason the salespersons didn't trust the people who were creating the database.

  Maybe the salespersons were worried about their work being automated. Or perhaps they thought that having their important paperwork in a database would make it easier for the company to replace them with other salespersons.

  Or maybe these files were so important to the salespersons that they just worried about the files being lost.
- **Goal:** Expect a correct answer to focus on the salesperson's refusal to turn over their important files. At the same time, we need to keep an open mind. It's very possible for an answer to suggest something that we haven't thought of. Beware of answers that ignore important aspects of the situation which need to be explained.

**Answer choices**
**A. Some of the salespersons gave huge paper files to the staff responsible for creating the database while other salespersons gave them much smaller files.** — No Impact / Cheats Paradox (2% of students)
Without more information, this doesn't explain why the salespersons rarely accessed the files. We would need to imagine quite a few details in order for this to have any impact. And it doesn't explain why salespersons refused to surrender their paper files.

**B. Most of the salespersons already had portable computers before the new database was created.** — Opposite (if anything) / Restates Paradox (1% of students)
If anything, this only deepens the mystery. If most of the salespersons were not already using personal computers before this project started, that might lead to an explanation for why they didn't use the database. Maybe they were technophobes who were reluctant to use new computers or just didn't want to change their way of doing things. The answer choice doesn't actually state any of this, so we could argue that this simply adds details without explaining the situation. But if this did have an impact on the story, it could very easily be the opposite of what we want.

**C. The papers that the salespersons found most important all contained personal information about employees of client companies, which the salespersons did not want in a central database.** — Correct (96% of students)
This isn't one of the exact explanations that we anticipated, but it's very similar. It provides a reason for the salesperson's refusal to turn files over to the people creating the database.

**D. All of the salespersons were required to attend a series of training sessions for the new database software even though many of them found the software easy to use even without training.** — Opposite (if anything) (1% of students)
If many of the salespersons found the software easy to use, that would seem to add to the list of reasons why we would expect them to use it. This is the opposite of what we want an answer to do. It doesn't explain why the salespersons didn't use the database.

**E. The number of staff required to create the database turned out to be larger than anticipated, and the company had to pay overtime wages to some of them.** — Cheats Paradox / Thin Ice (0% of students)
This answer is trying to trick us into imagining that it took much longer than expected to scan the files into the database, and that's why the salespersons rarely accessed the database. But this would be adding a lot of detail that isn't actually stated in this answer choice. This answer also doesn't explain why salespersons refused to hand over files.

## S4 · Q5 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Heavy tapestry fabrics are appropriate only for use in applications that will not need to be laundered frequently. These applications do not include any types of clothing—such as skirts or even jackets—but instead include swags and balloon valances, which are types of window treatments.

### Prompt
Which one of the following statements is most supported by the information above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:**
  - *conditional (only = right side)*
```
heavy tapestry appropriate  →  don't need to be laundered frequently
need to be laundered frequently  →  heavy tapestry not appropriate
```
  - *contrast*
  - Swags / balloon valances (which are types of window treatments) do not need to be laundered frequently.
  - All clothing does need to be laundered frequently.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we're given a conditional statement in an Inference stimulus, we ask ourselves two questions:
  1. are there any other conditionals present that I can chain this up with?
  2. are there any facts present to which I can apply this rule? (usually via the contrapositive)

  We got a conditional in the first sentence:
```
heavy tapestry fabric appropriate  →  doesn't need to be laundered frequently
```

  If we wanted to, we could turn the two claims in the second sentence into conditionals:

```
clothing  →  does need frequent laundering
swags or balloon valance  →  does not need freq. laundering
```

  Can we chain those together with the original? The overlapping concept is whether or not something needs to be laundered frequently. We often need to contrapose the rule in order to see how it chains or applies.

  When we look at the contrapositive,
```
Don't need to be laundered frequently  →  heavy tapestry not appropriate
```

  we can see that it could chain up with the Clothing conditional.

```
Clothing  →  does need frequent laundering  →  heavy tapestry inappropriate
```

**Answer choices**
**A. If a fabric is not a heavy tapestry fabric, then it is not appropriate for use in swags.** — Unsupported Trigger (2% of students)
The first sentence gives us a conditional that says,

If a fabric is a heavy tapestry,
then only appropriate for stuff that's infrequently laundered.

We weren't given any rule for what's true if a fabric is not a heavy tapestry, so there's no way we can derive an outcome about that.

**B. Heavy tapestry fabrics should not be used unless swags or balloon valances are being made.** — Bad Trigger Match (3% of students)
We have a rule that says,

```
needs to be laundered frequently  →  heavy tapestry fabrics should not be used
```

This answer is saying

```
it's not swags and not balloon valances  →  heavy tapestry fabrics should not be used
```

Can we say that, "If it's not a swag or a balloon valance, then it needs to be laundered frequently"?

No, we can't say that. There might be other things besides swags and balloon valances that don't need to be laundered frequently.

To put this another way, the paragraph has communicated to us that it's conceivably appropriate to use heavy tapestry fabrics if you're making swags or balloon valances. But they never told us that those are the only two cases in which heavy tapestry fabrics would be okay, and the answer is saying these are the only two cases.

**C. If heavy tapestry fabrics are appropriate for a particular application, then that application must be a window treatment.** — Unsupported Outcome (5% of students)
The first sentence gives us a conditional that says,

If a heavy tapestry fabric is appropriate,
then doesn't need to be frequently laundered.

In order for this answer to be derivable, we'd need to think that, "If it doesn't need to be frequently laundered, then it must be a window treatment". Is that something we know?

No, just like (B) this is too strong. We were told that window treatments don't need to be frequently laundered, but this answer is acting like window treatments are the only thing that doesn't need to be frequently laundered.

**D. If a fabric is appropriate for use in a skirt or jacket, then that fabric is not a heavy tapestry fabric.** — Correct (86% of students)
This is what we were predicting, although it's certainly worded in the weirdest way they could think of. We wanted "heavy tapestry is not appropriate for clothing (such as skirts or jackets).

By contrapositive, this is saying

```
Fabric is heavy tapestry  →  not appropriate for skirt or jacket
```

We know from the first sentence that

```
Fabric is heavy tapestry  →  not appropriate for stuff that needs to be laundered frequently
```

Can we say that skirts and jackets need to be laundered frequently? Yes, we were told that no type of clothing belongs to the category of "doesn't need to be laundered frequently", which means that all types of clothing belong to the category of "does need to be laundered frequently".

Again, we were able to build this chain:

```
Clothing (such as skirt or jacket)  →  does need frequent laundering  →  heavy tapestry not appropriate
```

By contrapositive,

```
heavy tapestry is appropriate  →  does not need freq. laundering  →  not clothing (such as skirt or jacket)
```

**E. Heavy tapestry fabrics are sometimes appropriate for use in types of clothing other than skirts and jackets.** — Contradicted (3% of students)
Since all clothing needs to be laundered frequently, heavy tapestry fabrics would be inappropriate for all clothing.

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
The brain area that enables one to distinguish the different sounds made by a piano tends to be larger in a highly skilled musician than in someone who has rarely, if ever, played a musical instrument. This shows that practicing on, and playing, a musical instrument actually alters brain structure.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** The brain area that enables one to distinguish the different sounds made by a piano tends to be larger in a highly skilled musician than in someone who has rarely, if ever, played a musical instrument.
- **Conclusion:** This shows that practicing on, and playing, a musical instrument actually alters brain structure.
- **Evaluate:** This is a classic example of a Causal Flaw. As the legendary Steve Irwin would say, "she's a beauty!"

  The evidence describes a correlation—two things that tend to happen together. A certain area of the brain tends to be larger in a highly skilled musician compared to someone who does not play a musical instrument.

  The conclusion then claims that one of these things causes the other. While a correlation is potentially evidence of a causal relationship, by itself a correlation doesn't prove that one thing causes the other. This argument is flawed because it fails to consider alternative possibilities.

  There could be any number of other factors which might cause this area of the brain to be larger in musicians. The actual causal relationship might be the reverse of what the conclusion claims: maybe when this area of the brain is larger than normal, that causes someone to become a skilled musician, or at least it's a partial cause.
- **Goal:** We'll expect an answer to point out how the argument concludes that a casual relationship exists based solely on evidence of a correlation. A correct answer might suggest that the argument has failed to consider an alternative possibility. Beware of answers that describe flaws which don't occur in this argument.

**Answer choices**
**A. The argument presumes, without providing justification, that what is true about the brain structures of highly skilled pianists is also true of the brain structures of other highly skilled musicians.** — Part vs. Whole (5% of students)
The evidence discusses an area of the brain that enables one to distinguish the different sounds made by a piano. It states that this area tends to be larger in highly skilled musicians. The argument never mentions highly skilled pianists. It doesn't use evidence specifically about pianists to support a conclusion about other highly skilled musicians.

**B. The argument fails to address the possibility that people who become highly skilled musicians do so, in part, because of the size of a certain area of their brains.** — Correct (83% of students)
This answer choice suggests that there might be a causal relationship between a certain area of the brain and being a highly skilled musician, but the relationship is the opposite of the one proposed by the argument's conclusion.

**C. The argument draws a conclusion about a broad range of phenomena from evidence concerning a much narrower range of phenomena.** — Sampling (8% of students)
This can be an appealing answer at first glance, because the conclusion is written in very general language. But the evidence is about the size of a certain brain area in highly skilled musicians, and there is no indication that the conclusion is about a broader range of phenomena. The conclusion isn't necessarily about people other than highly skilled musicians. And the "brain structure" mentioned in the conclusion might be the same area of the brain discussed in the evidence.

**D. The argument fails to address the possibility that a certain area of the brain is smaller in people who have listened to a lot of music but who have never learned to play a musical instrument than it is in people who have learned to play a musical instrument.** — Opposite (if anything) (2% of students)
If an argument "fails to address" a possibility, that possibility, whatever it is, should weaken the argument. Assuming that this answer choice is even talking about the same area of the brain as the argument, if this area is smaller in people who have never learned to play a musical instrument compared to people who have, that could potentially strengthen the argument.

This still wouldn't prove a causal relationship, but it's more evidence supporting the idea that there is some relationship between playing music and the size of this area of the brain, and it's not just a coincidence.

**E. The argument presumes, without providing justification, that highly skilled musicians practice more than other musicians.** — Irrelevant Comparison (2% of students)
The evidence compares highly skilled musicians to people who rarely, if ever, play a musical instrument. There isn't any comparison between highly skilled musicians and other musicians. The argument isn't assuming anything about a difference in the amount of practice done by these two different groups of musicians.

### Prompt

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (So):** The treatment is probably not actually effective.
- **Evidence:** All three studies showing the new treatment looked promising had critical methodological flaws.
- **Evaluate:** Parallel Flaw usually tests illegal moves with conditionals/quantifiers, or it tests one of the top 6 famous flaws:
  - Necessary vs. Sufficient (illegal conditional moves)
  - overconfident Causal conclusions
  - Part vs. Whole
  - Sampling
  - Ad Hominem
  - Unproven vs. Proven False

  Here, it's doing that last one. The fact that these studies had methodological flaws means they're not super trustworthy as evidence. We shouldn't lean on them as support for the idea that this new treatment is effective.

  The correct conclusion would be, "So, we should not judge the effectiveness of the treatment based on these studies".

  The author, meanwhile, is incorrectly volunteering some pessimism about the effectiveness of the treatment. Where is the author getting any evidence that the treatments aren't effective?

  The fact that these studies aren't good evidence in favor of X does not suddenly mean that they are good evidence against X. They just aren't good evidence.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that does something similar Unproven vs. Proven False move. There is an effort to prove X that is flawed or unsuccessful in some way. And the author concludes from this that X is probably wrong / false.

**Answer choices**
**A. since the judges in a baking contest did not have uniform criteria for selecting a winner, the cake that won is probably a bad one** — Correct (85% of students)
This replicates the idea of a certain experiment / study / trial having some sketchy methodology — there's no uniform criteria for selecting a winner? So how do the judges know how to score these cakes against each other?

And it replicates the conclusion that assumes the results of the experiment / study / trial are probably the opposite of what was found.

The three studies found that the treatment looked very effective, so the author concludes the treatment probably isn't effective.

The judges found that cake X was the best cake, so the author concludes that cake X was probably not the best.

This feels less like Unproven vs. Proven False and more like "shady methodology doesn't mean incorrect results". The latter is still a version of the former, but it would probably be easier to like this answer if we were searching for the latter rather than the former.

**B. since some people who fish seldom catch any fish, they probably have some reason for fishing other than catching fish** — Different Flaw (1% of students)
This is testing one of the semi-famous flaws: Intent vs. Result

It's assuming that because the people who fish ended up with the result of not catching fish, that their intent must not have been to catch fish.

**C. since some foods have very little nutritional value, people who include those foods in their diets are probably malnourished** — Different Flaw (7% of students)
This seems to commit the famous flaw of Part vs. Whole

It's assuming that because part of some people's diet (certain foods) have a trait (little nourishing value), that their overall diet has that same trait (little nourishing value).

**D. since all scarves are at least somewhat decorative, it is likely that when scarves were first adopted, they were purely decorative** — Different Flaw (1% of students)
This is sort of committing the Intent vs. Result flaw.

Somehow we've ended up in a world (result) with all scarves being at least somewhat decorative. The author is assuming that the intent of scarves must therefore have been purely decorative.

It's also making a move from "All A's are at least somewhat X" to "the first A's were purely X". There's not really a name for that flaw, but it doesn't resemble the original argument's move from "The thing that found X to be true had a flawed methodology" to "X is probably not true".

**E. since all members of the city council have a financial stake in the city's development, any development proposal they make is likely to be motivated purely by self-interest** — Different Flaw (7% of students)

It's acting like anyone with a biased interest in something will be motivated purely by that interest, not by legitimate, substantive things.

## S4 · Q12 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
If future improvements to computer simulations of automobile crashes enable computers to provide as much reliable information about the effectiveness of automobile safety features as is provided by actual test crashes, then manufacturers will use far fewer actual test crashes. For the costs of designing and running computer simulations are much lower than those of actual test crashes.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strongly supports the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If computer simulated crash tests become as reliable as actual test crashes, then manufacturers will use far fewer actual test crashes.
- **Evidence:** The cost of doing computer simulations is lower than paying for actual test crashes.
- **Evaluate:** At the heart of this question is a comparison between computer simulated crash tests vs. actual physical ones with cars and dummies and brick walls.

  This argument makes a Prediction, that if computer tests were as reliable as actual tests, then we'd see more computer tests and fewer actual tests based on a Comparison between the two types of tests:

  Computer tests are cheaper than actual tests.

  If you were a car manufacturer deciding whether or not to go with computer tests vs. actual tests, so far you know that they're equally reliable, but computer tests are cheaper. Is there anything else you'd like to find out?

  This is an Incomplete Comparison.
  We know the two options are tied when it comes to reliable information.
  Computer tests win when it comes to how costly.
  But do actual tests have any advantages? Faster turnaround time? Tax incentives? Do they provide some other kind of data that computer tests don't?
- **Goal:** We want to hear some other way in which computer tests are better than actual tests (or at least, not worse than actual tests). Or we'd like an answer that stressed that 'how costly is it?' is an important part of the decision making process for manufacturers deciding whether to go with computer tests or actual tests.

**Answer choices**
**A. Apart from information about safety features, actual test crashes provide very little information of importance to automobile manufacturers.** — Correct (57% of students)
This rules out an objection. One of the ways to argue against the author is to say,

"Sure, computer tests are less costly, but ... they only tell you about the effectiveness of auto safety features. When we do actual tests we get safety feature data, but we also get lots of other types of data that computer tests don't provide."

**B. It is highly likely that within the next 20 years computer simulations of automobile crashes will be able to provide a greater amount of reliable information about the effectiveness of automobile safety features than can be provided by actual test crashes.** — No Impact (21% of students)
This answer choice makes it seem like computer tests will one day surpass actual tests in amount reliable info, but this argument is specifically about the point at which they're providing equal reliable info.

This answer choice is also aimed at the Trigger of the conclusion. If a conclusion is phrased conditionally, as this one was, we're never worried about the Trigger. On a strengthen question, we're not trying to prove the trigger will happen. On a weaken question, we're not trying to prove the trigger won't happen. You accept the trigger (the "if" condition). It's a given, in this argument, that we're talking about a hypothetical world in which computer tests provide as much reliable info as actual tests.

**C. If computer simulations will soon be able to provide more information about the effectiveness of automobile safety features, automobile manufacturers will soon be able to produce safer cars.** — Untriggered Conditional (8% of students)
This is a conditional answer, but we have no reason to think the trigger gets triggered, so it doesn't go anywhere.

We're playing along with the conditional trigger of the conclusion — we're in a world where simulations provide as much info as actual tests, in regards to safety.

Is that a world that happens soon?
We don't know.

Is that a world where you get more info about the effectiveness of safety features?
No. Our hypothetical world just says that they provide as much info.

If we interpret this trigger as "providing more information than simulations used to provide", that's probably the case in our hypothetical world.

But since the outcome is saying that car makers could make safer cars, it sounds more like the first interpretation, where simulations provide more info than actual tests do.

After all, the second interpretation (where simulations provide as much info as actual tests, but provide more info than simulations previously did) wouldn't enhance what car makers already know about safety features from actual tests, so how could it possibly lead to safer cars?

So again, this answer goes nowhere because our hypothetical world doesn't establish the trigger of this answer, so we don't get to think about the impact of the outcome of this answer.

**D. The cost per automobile of testing and designing safety features is decreasing and will continue to decrease for the foreseeable future.** — No Impact / No Distinction (5% of students)
This gives a status quo idea "something that's been happening will continue to happen", and it doesn't differentiate between computer tests and actual tests at all.

**E. For years, the aviation industry has been successfully using computer simulations of airplane crashes to test the effectiveness of safety features of airplane designs.** — No Impact (10% of students)
If we were trying to be convinced that simulations could be as effective as actual crash tests at testing safety, then this answer would strengthen somewhat (even if it is about a different industry, it would provide some plausibility to the idea that simulations can be a successful way of testing safety).

But since we're dealing with a conditional conclusion, we're already playing along with the trigger idea of being in a world where simulations provide as much safety information as actual tests. So we don't need to be convinced that's true. We're assuming it's true, for the sake of argument, because we're analyzing a conditional conclusion.

When the conclusion is saying, "If X happens, then Y will happen", we can't strengthen the argument by trying to make it more likely that X will happen (nor can we weaken by making it seem like X won't happen). We have to just play along with the trigger, and judge whether the outcome would hold.

This answer doesn't do anything to enhance our belief that in a world where simulations are as effective as actual tests, they will do more simulations and fewer actual tests.

The reasoning in the legislator's argument is most vulnerable to the criticism that the argument

### Written (single style)

- **Intermediate Conclusion:** Her real reason for opposing must not be very persuasive.
- **Intermediate Conclusion:** She has not revealed her real reason.
- **Evidence:** She says the act would deter investment, but in the past she's voted for legislation that inhibited investment.
- **Evaluate:** When an author tries to dismiss an argument because the source of the argument either has a biased interest or because the source of the argument has conflicting past behavior, that's the famous flaw known as Ad Hominem.

  This author says, "because in the past you voted for something that inhibited investment, you can't really be worried about inhibiting investment".

  That's flawed thinking for a couple reasons
  - People are allowed to change. People can learn from their mistakes. Maybe this woman has learned that it was a mistake for her in the past to have voted for something that inhibited investment.

  - Deciding to vote for / against something is a complicated calculus. You might vote for something that inhibits investment in spite of the fact that it inhibits investment, because something else outweighs it.

  Of course not. Sometimes you accept a negative part of something because the good / necessary parts outweigh the negative.

  That famous flaw is happening in the move from Premise to Intermediate Conclusion. We have another famous flaw occurring in the move from Intermediate Conclusion to Main Conclusion.

  The author is acting like "since her reason for rejecting the act is bad / invalid / not real, then we should approve the act". But wait a sec — has the author presented any evidence in favor of approving the act?

  Just because the colleague failed to prove the act should be rejected doesn't mean that the author has proven that the act should be approved. This is the famous flaw Unproven vs. Proven False.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer calling out the Ad Hominem flaw, saying "just because she's previously supported something that did X doesn't mean she's lying when she says she doesn't like this new thing because it does X."

  Or look for an answer calling out the Unproven vs. Proven False flaw, saying "just because we don't have a good reason for rejecting something doesn't mean that we have a good reason for accepting/embracing something."

**B. fails to address the grounds on which the colleague claims the act should be rejected** — Correct (65% of students)

That's what this answer is complaining about. Our author addressed a tangential issue about the colleague, rather than substantively addressing the colleague's concern about whether this act would deter investment.

The author isn't assuming anything about whether the colleague is in the minority or majority position in the legislature. The argument wouldn't be affected one way or the other if she were one vs. the other.

```
X requires Y = X → Y
Significantly increasing efficiency  →  comp system requires employees to adopt new ways of working
```
  - *specific fact* - The Ministry's new computer system is built to fit its employees existing ways of working.
- **The Blank (so):** We need to draw a logical conclusion, so we think of this like a Most Supported inference question.
- **Evaluate:** On Inference, whenever we're given a conditional, we ask ourselves two things:
  1. Am I supposed to be chaining this up with some other conditional?
  2. Am I supposed to be applying this rule to a specific fact?

  The first sentence was conditional, but the second sentence is a specific fact, so we would more likely be doing #2.

  Typically, when we are tested on our ability to apply a conditional to a fact, we have to contrapose the conditional to understand how it applies (or we ask ourselves about what Overlapping Concept is in both the rule and the fact).

  The overlapping concept between the 1st and 2nd sentence is "whether a computer system requires employees to change their ways of working or permits them to continue their existing ways of working".

  When we contrapose the rule from the first sentence, we get:

```
If new computer system doesn't require employees to adopt new ways of working  →  won't signif increase efficiency
```
- **Goal:** We were told that the MoT's new computer system does not require employees to adopt new ways (it was custom built to fit their existing ways), so it triggers this conditional and yields this logical conclusion/inference:

  The Ministry of Transportation's new computer system will not significantly increase the ministry's efficiency.

  Let's look for something like that.

**Answer choices**
**A. the new computer system will not increase the efficiency of the Ministry of Transportation to any appreciable degree** — Correct (83% of students)
We were looking for this:
The Ministry of Transportation's new computer system will not significantly increase the ministry's efficiency.

So this seems like what we wanted (the output you get from applying the rule in the 1st sentence to the fact in the 2nd sentence).

Is "won't increase to any appreciable degree" the same as "will not significantly increase"?

Yes! A significant increase is an appreciable one.

Insignificant is synonymous with negligible. Some of us may have savings or checking accounts that increase by an insignificant/negligible amount of interest each year (a few dollars).

**B. it is likely that the new computer system will not function correctly when it is first installed** — Out of Scope: malfunction (1% of students)
Nothing in the passage presents any possibility of malfunctioning.

**C. the leaders of the Ministry of Transportation must not be concerned with the productivity of the ministry's employees** — Causal Speculation (5% of students)
This answer goes one thought farther than the derivable inference. The derivable inference is that, "This new system won't increase productivity/efficiency". This answer tries to speculate why we would use such a system.

Did the leaders choose to go with this new system because they're not concerned with efficiency? Because it was the only system they could afford? Because they needed to do a favor for the manufacturer of the system?

We have no idea. We can't speculate the backstory of why the ministry is installing a system that won't significantly boost efficiency (maybe they're already very efficient!)

**D. the new computer system will be worthwhile if it automates many processes that are currently performed manually** — Out of Scope: automating / Too Strong: will be (7% of students)
Nothing in the passage talks about automating jobs that used to be done manually, so we can't derive any language like that from the two sentences we have available.

This is a very strong idea, as well, which proves the system will be worthwhile, as long as it succeeds in automating many processes.

**E. it will be easy for employees of the Ministry of Transportation to learn to use the new computer system** — Too Strong: easy / One-Claim Support (4% of students)
Can we say that "because the system is built to fit the ministry's existing ways of working" that it will be easy to learn?

No. It's reasonable to speculate that it will be easier to learn this new system than if the system hadn't been custom built to fit their needs.

But no matter how good a computer system is, when you install a new computer system at work, there are usually hiccups and confusion and a learning curve for the employees, so calling it "easy" to learn seems pretty bold.

We should also be nervous to pick this answer because we'd be supporting it using only the 2nd claim.

On 99% or more of Inference questions (be they Must Be True, Most Supported, or Logical Completion), the correct answer involves combining 2 or more claims.

The fact that the secret was justifiably kept in the first place has nothing to do with this answer being right/wrong. It's just an extraneous detail.

Since concealing that a secret is being kept is a practice that might facilitate the abuse of power, we know that government officials shouldn't do it unless there is a compelling reason to do so.

**D. Government officials who conceal information without a compelling reason are thereby guilty of an abuse of power.** — Unsupported Relationship (16% of students)
We don't have any rule that allows us to say, "you are guilty of abuse of power."

That would look like this:
```
_____________  →  abused power
```

Our conditionals look like this:
```
[practice might facilitate abuse] AND [no compelling reason]  →  shouldn't do it
govt official conceals from public that they're keeping a secret  →  opens up possibility for abuse
```

**E. Government officials should keep information secret only if doing so does not make it easier for those officials to abuse their power.** — Too Strong (5% of students)
This rule (when we contrapose it says),

```
if keeping info secret does make it easier for officials to abuse power  →  should not keep info secret
```

That's too strong an idea, when the paragraph is not saying they should never keep secrets; it allows for the fact that you can keep a secret if there's a compelling reason to do so.

## S4 · Q20 — Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
According to a theory embraced by some contemporary musicians, music is simply a series of sounds, bereft of meaning. But these musicians, because they understand that their theory is radically nonconformist, encourage audience acceptance by prefacing their performances with explanations of their intentions. Thus, even their own music fails to conform to their theory.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if assumed, enables the argument's conclusion to be properly drawn?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (thus):** The music of these contemporary musicians fails to conform to their theory
  (i.e. the music of these contemporary musicians does have meaning)
- **Evidence:** These musicians encourage audience acceptance by prefacing their performances with explanations of their intentions (since they realize their approach to music is very weird).
- **Evaluate:** "Bereft of" = lacking, devoid of. If we don't know that, we'll struggle to understand what failing to conform with their theory means, although we could make do with thinking, "Okay, we need to prove that these musicians' music is not bereft of meaning."

  Where does the author think he establishes that their music has meaning?

  The closest semantic cousin would be explanation of their intentions. These experimental musicians who are about to play a bunch of weird noises, since to them, 'music is just a series of sounds with no meaning', first tell there audiences a little disclaimer:

  Hey, everyone. Thanks for coming out. We're going to be doing stuff that sounds [smugly] a little different than what you're used to tonight. We believe that music has no meaning and is just a series of sounds, so we want you to listen to what we're doing and accept it via that framework.

  Our author is basically saying, "telling people that your music has no meaning is actually imbuing your music with a meaning".
- **Goal:** Look for a linking answer saying "if you're encouraging audiences to accept your music, by explaining to them your intentions, then your music has meaning".

  But we'll take anything that functionally allows us to prove that the music of these musicians does have meaning.

  And if an answer isn't giving us a mechanism to prove that "their music has meaning", then it's functionally worthless to us.

**Answer choices**
**A. The human ability to think symbolically and to invest anything with meaning makes it very difficult to create music that has no meaning.** — Too Weak (7% of students)
This makes it seem like the contemporary musicians are probably wrong to believe that music is just a series of sounds with no meaning.

But it doesn't have any power to prove that their music has meaning. It says it's very difficult (but not impossible) to make music without meaning, so it's leaving a gap where the music of these contemporary musicians might still be meaningless.

**B. It will be possible for musicians to create music that means nothing only when listeners are able to accept such a theory of music.** — Unrelated to Goal (6% of students)
Only when (just like "only if"), introduces necessary ideas. So this says,

```
possible to create music that means nothing  →  listeners can accept this music theory
```

We're trying to prove that "their music has meaning", so we want to see that idea on the right of the arrow.

It looks like we'd have to contrapose this to get the part about 'meaning' onto the right side. The contrapositive looks like this:

```
listeners can't accept this music theory  →  impossible to create [music that means nothing]
```

What we need is a rule that gives us a right side that looks more like this:
```
such and such is true  →  music does have meaning
```

Since this rule has no way to prove that music does have meaning, it's useless to us.

**C. The fact that music is distinguishable from a random series of sounds only when it has meaning makes music with meaning more appealing to audiences than music without meaning.** — Out of Scope: more appealing / No Impact (6% of students)
This is not giving us a rule that allows us to prove that certain music does have meaning, so it's useless to us.

It should also be a red flag on Sufficient Assumption when you see answer choices bring in new ideas, like more appealing to audiences.

**D. Music that opposes current popular conceptions of music is less likely to be enjoyed by audiences than is music that accords with such conceptions.** — Out of Scope: less likely enjoyed / No Impact (3% of students)
This is not giving us a rule that allows us to prove that certain music does have meaning, so it's useless to us.

It should also be a red flag on Sufficient Assumption when you see answer choices bring in new ideas, like less likely to be enjoyed by audiences.

**E. Musicians whose music has no meaning do not preface their performances with explanations of their intentions.** — Correct (78% of students)
In classic form, they've given us the predicted missing link in its contrapositive form.

```
musician whose music has no meaning  →  don't preface a performance w/ explanation of intention
```

So,

```
preface performance w/ explanation of intention  →  not a musician whose music has no meaning
```

These contemporary musicians preface their performances with an explanation of their intentions, so according to this rule, they are either not musicians, or they're musicians whose music does have meaning.

We know they're musicians, so this rule is telling us that their music does have meaning.

Thus, we've derived the conclusion.

## S4 · Q21 — Method

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Evolution does not always optimize survival of an organism. Male moose evolved giant antlers as a way of fighting other males or mates, giving those with the largest antlers an evolutionary advantage. But those antlers also make it harder to escape predators, since they can easily get tangled in trees. All male moose would be better off with antlers half the current size: they would all be less vulnerable to predators, and those with the largest antlers would maintain their relative advantage.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is a technique of reasoning used in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Evolution does not always optimize survival of an organism.
- **Evidence:** Male moose evolved giant antlers as a way to determine the dominant males, for whom the giant antlers provide a survival benefit.

  But those giant antlers make it harder to escape predators, since the antlers can easily get tangled in trees.

  All male moose would be better off with antlers half the current size (for the sake of escaping predators), assuming they could preserve whatever relative size/status advantage they have over their peers.
- **Evaluate:** On Method questions, the correct answer usually pulls from this list of concepts:

  presents Analogy
  rules out alternate possibilities
  provides (counter)example of general claim
  discusses implications of logic
  calls out dubious assumption
  raises overlooked consideration / distinction
  defines a term
  suggests alternate explanation

  This one seemed to start with a very general claim about evolution and then illustrate it by discussing the case of the male moose.
- **Goal:** Let's look for something like "providing an example to illustrate a generalization".

**Answer choices**
**A. citing an example to cast doubt on a competing argument** — Out of Scope: competing argument (18% of students)
We love the first three words! But, not so much the rest. There was no competing argument. That author isn't fighting anyone else's position. The author is citing an example to support a generalization.

**B. employing an analogy in order to dispute a generalization** — Out of Scope: analogy (6% of students)
The example of the male moose is meant to support the generalization in the first sentence.

We could imagine an unspoken generalization, "Evolution always optimizes the survival of an organism", and yes the argument does employ a counterexample to dispute that generalization. But it doesn't employ an analogy.

Disputing with analogy
Jane is not going to love the Performing Arts school that accepted her, even though it's her dream school. After all, Jose got into his top choice law school and ended up hating it.

Disputing with counterexample
Getting into one's dream school does not always make one happy. After all, Jose got into his top choice law school and ended up hating it.

Notice that we're using Jose identically in both cases. What makes the first case an analogy, and the second case a counterexample is the difference between the initial claims.

Analogy = 2 specific stories that both embody the same (unspoken) general idea
Example = 1 specific story that embodies an explicitly stated general idea

**C. challenging a general claim by presenting a counterexample** — Correct (62% of students)
We have to look at the conclusion in the first sentence and think, "Yes, he's challenging a general claim, because it would be a general claim to say evolution always optimizes survival of an organism, and he's challenge that."

Is the evidence a counterexample, a case in which "evolution happened, but it didn't optimize the survival of an organism?"

Sure the moose is a case where evolution caused the giant antlers and the giant antlers actually impede the moose's survival somewhat.

**D. disputing the relevance of an example thought to support an opposing view** — Out of Scope: opposing view / dispute (6% of students)
The author is presenting what she thinks is a very relevant example (or counterexample, technically). There isn't an opposing view named that the author is fighting.

While the main conclusion (first sentence) is arguing against a general claim, that claim has been attributed to anyone, so we can't call it anyone's view.

That invisible view has not presented a supposed example, and the author does not attack the relevance of that supposed example.

**E. undermining a claim by showing that it is self-contradictory** — Too Strong: self-contradictory (8% of students)
The idea of contradictory (aka, inconsistent / incompatible) is wrong over 90% of the time we see it. Self-contradictions are incredibly rare.

The author is undermining the claim "evolution always optimizes survival of an organism".

If we were showing that this claim were self-contradictory, we'd be saying "That's crazy talk! After all, evolution literally means "to lessen the survival value of an organism", so how could it possibly optimize survival?"

## S4 · Q22 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Biologist: When bacteria of a particular species are placed in a test tube that has different areas lit with different colors of light, the bacteria move only into the areas lit with a particular shade of red. The bacteria contain chlorophyll, a chemical that allows them to produce energy more effectively from this color of light than from any other. This suggests that the bacteria detect this color of light by monitoring how much energy their chlorophyll is producing.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the biologist's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (this suggests that):** The bacteria detect red light by monitoring how much energy their chlorophyll is producing.
- **Evidence:** The chlorophyll in these bacteria allows them to get more energy from red light than from any other color of night.

  And when you put this bacteria into a tube with different areas lit by different colors, the bacteria all move to the red light district.
- **Evaluate:** Since the author is looking at a study / experiment and concluding some causal story, we can think through our Explain Curious Fact lens.

  Curious Fact ... how do these bacteria know to all move to the red light district of the test tube?

  Author's Explanation ... they feel there way there, via chlorophyll-energy (the red light gives them the most chlorophyll energy).

  We always evaluate Explain Curious Fact arguments by thinking
  1. could there be some Alternate Explanation for the curious fact?
  2. how plausible is the Author's Explanation?
- **Goal:** Since we're trying to weaken this argument, we either want to
  1. suggest an Alternate Explanation for how/why the bacteria are figuring out where the red light area is, or
  2. undermine the plausibility of the Author's notion that the bacteria can sense how much energy their chlorophyll is producing, and since red provides the most energy they find the red area by seeking the area of most chlorophyll energy.

**Answer choices**
**A. If the chlorophyll is removed from the bacteria, but the bacteria are otherwise unharmed, they no longer show any tendency to move into the areas lit with the particular shade of red.** — Strengthens (3% of students)
This is the classic "No cause, No effect" way to strengthen a causal story.

Since the author thinks that chlorophyll is how the bacteria find the red light district, she would predict that if remove the chlorophyll, they will no longer be able to find the red light zone.

That's just what this says, so it greatly strengthens her theory that the chlorophyll is involved in how they detect the red light.

**B. The bacteria show little tendency to move into areas containing light in colors other than the particular shade of red, even if their chlorophyll can produce some energy from light in those colors.** — Weak / Unclear Impact (6% of students)
It's unclear whether this answer is saying, "Given a test tube where red is an option, the bacteria show little inclination to visit any other color zones, even though they get more than no energy from those colors" or whether it's saying, "Even if you remove red from being an option, they show little inclination to move to the other color areas, which slightly energize them."

If we're talking about a test tube where red is an option, this answer doesn't weaken at all. Red might be so energizing that these bacteria would be fools to visit any other color.

If this is talking about a situation where red isn't an option, and they don't seem to be navigating towards the area of most energy, that would weaken the plausibility of the author's explanation a bit. It would start to look like maybe these bacteria aren't always seeking out the most chlorophyll energizing light.

But it's also possible that these other colors aren't even energizing enough to register on the bacteria's radar. Some energy can still be an undetectable level of energy.

**C. The areas of the test tube lit with the particular shade of red favored by the bacteria are no warmer, on average, than areas lit with other colors.** — Out of Scope: warmer (3% of students)
The author's explanation didn't seem to be making any assumptions about red being warmer. It wasn't warmth that was drawing the bacteria hither towards the red light; it was the amount of energy the bacteria's chlorophyll could harness from the light.

We have no idea if that's connected to warmth in any way.

**D. The bacteria show no tendency to move into areas lit with blue even when those areas are lit so brightly that the bacteria's chlorophyll produces as much energy in those areas as it does in the red areas.** — Correct (79% of students)
This badly weakens the plausibility of the Author's Explanation. In nerdy LSAT slang, this answer weakens by presenting Cause w/o Effect.

The author thinks that bacteria move towards the red light because they want the max energy that red chlorophyll provides and they navigate there by feeling their energy levels.

This answer is saying that when we make a blue light zone provide the bacteria with the same 'feels' as a red light would, they don't go there.

This shows that it must be something besides the chlorophyll energy that causes the bacteria to navigate there. The same chlorophyll energy, but with a different hue of light, resulted in different behavior.

**E. There are species of bacteria that do not contain chlorophyll but do move into areas lit with particular colors when placed in a test tube lit with different colors in different places.** — Weak / Unclear Impact (10% of students)
This feels like maybe it weakens plausibility as well, by providing evidence of Effect w/o Cause.

This answer describes bacteria moving towards areas lit by certain colors of light (the Effect) even though they don't have chlorophyll (which was involved in the Cause).

There are a couple reasons why this isn't the best answer, though:

1. Other species of bacteria that don't contain chlorophyll are likely to be pretty significantly different from those that do (plants have chlorophyll; animals don't). So whatever those bacteria are doing with their lives probably isn't that great to compare to our bacteria. They may have there own reasons for preferring certain colors of light and their own ways of navigating there.

Consider this analogy — Jack read Tina's LSAT Guide and did well on the LSAT, so someone concludes 'Jack did well by following the advice in Tina's LSAT Guide.'

Which one makes you doubt that more?
(D) Jill read Tina's Guide and followed its advice to the same level Jack did, and she did terribly on the LSAT. (Cause w/o Effect)

(E) Jasmine did not read Tina's Guide, but she still did well on the LSAT (Effect w/o Cause)

2. The reason this answer has unclear impact is because we don't even know if the bacteria with non-chlorophyll actually all moved to the same color of light. The description is wishy-washy, like "sure, if you put them into a test tube, they move into areas lit by particular colors of lights". Yes, but do they all move to the same color light and stay put? If not, then this isn't really even a comparable case to the bacteria with chlorophyll that we're analyzing.

## S4 · Q23 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
If a piece of legislation is the result of negotiation and compromise between competing interest groups, it will not satisfy any of those groups. So, we can see that the recently enacted trade agreement represents a series of compromises among the various interest groups that are concerned with it, because all of those groups are clearly unhappy with it.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately describes a logical flaw in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (So):** We can see that the recently enacted trade agreement represents a series of compromises among the various interest groups that are concerned with it.
- **Evidence (because):** All those groups are clearly unhappy with it,
  and
  if a piece of legislation is the result of negotiation and compromise between competing interest groups, it will not satisfy any of those groups.
- **Evaluate:** The first word out of the gate is "If". What does "if" mean to an LSAT student? That's right, it almost always means Conditional Logic (the condition attached to "if" goes on the left of the arrow).

  When we're doing Flaw and we see any Conditional Logic, what should we be thinking?

  We should be on the lookout for the #1 famous flaw of them all, confusing Necessary vs. Sufficient.

  This argument was identical to this one:
  If a person wins the Kentucky state lottery, they will be very wealthy. So, we can see that Bob apparently won the Kentucky state lottery, because he is clearly very wealthy.

  Our conversational objection to this argument would be, "Hey, there could be a ton of different reasons why Bob is wealthy. Sure, winning the KY lottery is one possibility, but we can't be sure that's why Bob is wealthy".

  Our conversational objection to this argument we just read would similarly be, "Hey, there could be a ton of different reasons why the various interest groups are clearly unhappy with this new agreement. Yes, resulting from compromise is one possibility, but we can't be sure that's why they're all unhappy".

  As soon as we see the conditional logic statement offered in the Evidence,
  for example, X → Y

  We listen for the two variations on the Necessary vs. Sufficient flaw (reasoning via an illegal negation or via an illegal reversal).

  We'd expect to hear, "Bob is Y. Thus, Bob is X"
  or, "Bob is not X. Thus, Bob is not Y".

  The conditional we were given was,

```
legislation resulted from compromise between interest groups  →  all the groups will be unsatisfied (unhappy)
```

  Does this argument say, "All the groups are unhappy. Thus, the legislation must have resulted from compromise?"

  Or does it say, "This legislation was not the result of compromise between interest groups. Thus, at least one group will be happy/satisfied."?

  It did the first one. It just tries to hide it with two moves:
  1. They supplied the Conclusion (So) before they supplied the Evidence (Because).
  2. They didn't use a verbatim word match. They're forcing us to see that "unhappy" and "not satisfied" are interchangeable here. They're also forcing us to see that "results from negotiation and compromise between groups" is interchangeable with "represents a series of compromises among the groups".

  In order to see past their shenanigans, we need to be on top of knowing that when we see "If", we're getting conditional logic. And we need to know that when we see conditional logic on Flaw questions, the authors will usually use a conditional rule in some illegal backwards or negated fashion.
- **Goal:** Look for the formal answer, saying this author scrambled a condition that is Sufficient to prove that everyone would be unhappy with a condition that is Required in order for everyone to be unhappy.

  Or look for the conversational answer saying, "That's one possible reason they're all unhappy. There might be other reasons, besides negotiation that results in compromise, that could account for why they're all unhappy".

**Answer choices**
**A. It draws a conclusion that is merely a disguised restatement of one of its premises.** — Wrong Flaw: not Circular (9% of students)
This refers to the famous Circular reasoning flaw, which shows up a ton in Flaw answer choices and is almost never correct.

The conclusion here is that the recently enacted trade agreement represents a series of compromises. Was either of the two premises saying that the new trade agreement is a results of compromises?

No. The first premise (the 1st sentence) doesn't talk about this new trade agreement at all, and the second premise (the final claim) doesn't say anything about compromises, just that people are unhappy with the new agreement.

**B. It concludes that a condition is necessary for a certain result merely from the claim that the condition leads to that result.** — Correct (76% of students)
This describes the #1 famous flaw, Necessary vs. Sufficient, in which the argument presents a conditional relationship and then applies the rule in some illegal backwards or opposite fashion.

The condition of "legislation resulting from compromise between competing groups" leads to the result of "all the competing groups are unhappy".

By arguing in this style,

```
Since ....                              We can conclude ...
all these groups are unhappy with       the recent agreement was the result of
the recent agreement                    compromise
```

... the author is making it look like "the result of compromise" is a Necessary (right side) condition that necessarily follows from knowing that the competing groups are all unhappy with the new agreement.

**C. It relies on understanding a key term in a quite different way in the conclusion from the way that term is understood in the premises.** — Wrong Flaw: not Equivocation (4% of students)
This refers to another of the top 10 famous flaws, Equivocation, in which the author uses the same term or concept but in two very different ways.

Like Circular reasoning (and like Internal Contradiction), these Equivocation answers are almost always wrong. In order to ever pick them, we need to be able to find some term that is used twice, but each usage could be defined in very different ways.

We don't have any such term here.

**D. It takes for granted that no piece of legislation can ever satisfy all competing interest groups.** — Too Strong: no / Not Assumed (6% of students)
Because this starts with takes for granted / presumes / fails to establish, we can ask ourselves, "Did the argument need to assume this?"

No, it's too harsh. This argument is only about this recent trade agreement. The author doesn't need to assume that no legislation ever could satisfy all competing groups. If we negate this assumption, we'd get "at least one piece of legislation can satisfy all competing interest groups".

That negated assumption wouldn't weaken the argument; it wouldn't even be relevant to the argument since the recent trade agreement we're focused on is one that didn't satisfy all competing interest groups.

**E. It bases a conclusion about a particular case on a general principle that concerns a different kind of case.** — Bad 2nd Half (6% of students)
This argument does have a conclusion about a particular case (the recently enacted trade agreement), and it does use a general principle (the first sentence).

But our problem with the argument isn't that the particular case isn't relevant to the general principle. It is. Our problem is just that the author is applying the principle in reverse.

## S4 · Q24 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
After a nuclear power plant accident, researchers found radioactive isotopes of iodine, tellurium, and cesium—but no heavy isotopes—in the atmosphere downwind. This material came either from spent fuel rods or from the plant's core. Spent fuel rods never contain significant quantities of tellurium isotopes. Radioactive material ejected into the atmosphere directly from the core would include heavy isotopes. After the accident, steam, which may have been in contact with the core, was released from the plant. The core contains iodine, tellurium, and cesium isotopes, which are easily dissolved by steam.

### Prompt
Of the following statements, which one is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Information:**
  - After an accident, they found isotopes of iodine, tellurium, and cesium downwind, but no heavy isotopes.
  - infer: iodine, tellurium, and cesium are not heavy isotopes
  - Those three things either came from (1) spent fuel rods or (2) the plant's core.
  - (1) never has much tellurium in it.
  - (2) would have ejected stuff that included heavy isotopes.
  - infer: both (1) and (2) have a strike against them, if we assume that this three-pack of isotopes traveled as a bunch. The fact that they had tellurium makes (1) seem like it wasn't the source. The fact that they didn't have heavy isotopes makes (2) seem like it wasn't the source.
  - After the accident, steam was released from the plant. It may have been in contact with the core, which contains those three isotopes, and those three isotopes are easily dissolved by steam.
- **Evaluate:** What a mouthful! This is a lot to take in, which is why we modeled above some of the self-talk we would be doing while reading it, to try to process where this conversation is headed.

  Even though no one is asking any questions, there appears to be a bit of a causal mystery, because researchers found these isotopes, and then we start hearing, "They either came from X or from Y", which makes it seem like we're trying to figure out which one it came from.

  We know that the tellurium didn't come from the spent fuel rods, but maybe the other two isotopes did?

  We know these three-pack of isotopes didn't eject directly from the core, because if it had then we would have found heavy isotopes in there as well, which we didn't.

  There's a possibility that the three-pack of isotopes came from the core, dissolved into steam, which was then released from the plant and then found by researchers downwind from the plant.

  But that seems pretty speculative.
- **Goal:** There's so much here that it's hard to make any specific prediction. Let's look for the most supportable answer.

**Answer choices**
**A. Radioactive material ejected into the environment directly from a nuclear power plant's core would not include tellurium isotopes.** — No Support: no tellurium in core (7% of students)
We know that stuff coming from spent fuel rods wouldn't have tellurium, but we have no idea whether tellurium gets ejected directly from the plant's core.

All we know about the plant's core is that heavy isotopes don't get ejected.

**B. The radioactive material detected by the researcher was carried into the atmosphere by the steam that was released from the plant.** — Correct (57% of students)
This seems like plausible story for why the researchers were finding these three isotopes in the atmosphere downwind from the plant.

But it's a very speculative story.

Steam may have been in contact with the core, in which case it may have picked up the three isotopes from the core, which dissolve easily into steam. (Dissolving kind of means "absorbed into" — when you dissolve salt into water, the crystals disappear but the salt molecules have not. That's why it now tastes like salt water.)

Ultimately, this answer wins because it's more supported than anything else, but it definitely goes down in the record books as one of the more speculative correct answers. Sometimes on Most Supported, the correct answer is pretty distant from the passage, but it's more conservative of a guess than anything else we're offered.

Here's another example of a Solve the Causal Mystery style Most Supported question.

**C. The nuclear power plant's spent fuel rods were not damaged.** — Out of Scope: damage (6% of students)
The passage doesn't talk at all about whether the spent fuel rods were / weren't damaged. We just know that tellurium wouldn't have come from spent fuel rods, regardless of their health / damage.

**D. The researchers found some radioactive material from spent fuel rods as well as some material that was ejected into the atmosphere directly from the plant's core.** — Contradicted (21% of students)
This answer is trying to appeal to students who are thinking that "the iodine and cesium came from the spent fuel rods, but the tellurium was ejected directly from the plant's core".

Thus, they would sign off on the idea that some of what the researchers found (the non-tellurium stuff) came from the rods and the rest came from the core.

First of all, that's an exotic hypothesis. We're now positing two different sources for where these three isotopes came from, when the passage is offering a different storyline that would only involve on source (steam that passed by the core).

Secondly, we can't really sign off on the idea that the researchers found anything that was ejected directly from the core, because we were told that stuff ejected directly would have heavy isotopes, and what researchers found id not have heavy isotopes.

**E. Spent fuel rods do not contain heavy isotopes in significant quantities.** — No Support (9% of students)
We don't know anything about whether or not spent fuel rods contain heavy isotopes.

We only know they don't contain significant amounts of tellurium (which is not a heavy isotopes to begin with).

## S4 · Q25 — Parallel

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
If ecology and the physical sciences were evaluated by the same criteria, ecology would fail to be a successful science because it cannot be captured by a set of simple laws. But ecology is a successful science, although of a different sort from the physical sciences. Therefore, it clearly is not being evaluated by means of the criteria used to evaluate the physical sciences.

### Prompt
Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its reasoning to the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (Therefore / clearly):** Ecology is not being evaluated by means of the criteria used to evaluate the physical sciences.
- **Evidence:** If ecology and the physical sciences were evaluated by same criteria, ecology would fail to be successful (it can't be captured by a set of simple laws).

  But ecology is a successful science.
- **Evaluate:** In a very generic sense, this argument just uses contrapositive logic:

  Premise 1: If A were true, then B would be true.
  Premise 2: But B isn't true.
  Conclusion: So A isn't true.

```
If Ecology and physical sciences were evaluated same  →  Ecology not successful science
```
