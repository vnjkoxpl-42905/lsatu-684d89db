## S1 · Q2 — Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Classicist: In the ancient Athenian democracy, unlike in any other political system in world history, the legislature consisted of all eligible voters. Athenian voters, not elected representatives, made all political decisions by direct vote after public debate in the Assembly. Thus, if the world has ever known true democracy, it was only in ancient Athens.

### Prompt

The conclusion of the classicist's argument follows logically if which one of the following is assumed?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion (Thus):* If the world has ever known true democracy, it was only in ancient Athens.

*Evidence:* It was only in ancient Athens that the legislature consisted of all eligible voters. Athenian voters made all political decisions by direct vote after public debate in the Assembly.

*Evaluate:* The conclusion we're trying to prove is a weird type of claim. Suppose I said, "Thus, if Shelly has any flowers in her kitchen right now, she has daisies". I'm not actually trying to prove she has daisies. I'm saying daisies are the only possible type of flower in her kitchen, but it wouldn't make me wrong if there weren't daisies. I would only be wrong if there were some other type of flower in her kitchen.

So, similarly, we don't need to prove that ancient Athens was a true democracy. We just need to know that every other political system has not been a true democracy.

Since the argument didn't give us a proving mechanism for "If xyz, then not true democracy", the correct answer will have to give us such a rule.

What do we know about all other political systems that would allow us to prove they aren't true democracies? We know that only ancient Athens had a legislature consisting of all eligible voters. So in every other political system, we know that wasn't the case.

*Goal:* So, we're probably going to see an answer like, "If a political system didn't have a legislature consisting of all eligible voters, then it wasn't a true democracy".

**Answer choices**
**A. Most political systems in world history have not been democracies.** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
Since this doesn't provide a rule that says, "If xyz, then not true democracy", it won't help us to prove the conclusion.

**B. Public debate is an essential part of any democratic decision-making process.** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
Since this doesn't provide a rule that says, "If xyz, then not true democracy", it won't help us to prove the conclusion.

**C. Athens was the only city in ancient Greece that had a democratic political system.** — Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
Since this doesn't provide a rule that says, "If xyz, then not true democracy", it won't help us to prove the conclusion.

**D. A political system is not a true democracy unless the eligible voters themselves vote directly on all political decisions.** — Correct (94% of students)
This is one of those fun Sufficient Assumption examples where the "New Concept in Conclusion" shortcut takes us all the way to the correct answer choice.

We're trying to prove that all other political systems besides ancient Athens were not true democracies. This is the only answer that provides a rule that would allow us to prove "not true democracy":

```
Eligible voters don't            not a true
themselves vote directly  →      democracy
on all political decisions
```

We were told that ancient Athens was the only political system in world history in which the legislature consisted of all eligible voters, who made all political decisions by direct vote.

So we know that this rule applies to every other political system, proving that none of them were true democracies. Thus, it allows us to derive the conclusion that "Other than possibly ancient Athens, the world has never known true democracy".

**E. Most Athenians who were eligible to vote attended the Assembly whenever it was debating important political decisions.** — Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
Since this doesn't provide a rule that says, "If xyz, then not true democracy", it won't help us to prove the conclusion.

## S1 · Q3 — Role

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Near-Earth objects (NEOs) such as asteroids threaten the Earth because they have the potential to collide with it. The goal of NEO research is to develop measures to counteract a possible hit by a sizable NEO. Government funding of this research is not a waste of money. Buying home insurance makes good fiscal sense, and governments fund NEO research for the same reason that people insure their homes.

### Prompt

The statement that buying home insurance makes good fiscal sense plays which one of the following roles in the argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion (not a waste = opinion):* Government funding of NEO research is not a waste of money.

*Evidence:* Buying home insurance makes good fiscal sense, and governments fund NEO research for the same reason that people insure their homes (it's designed to develop measures to counteract a possible hit by a sizable NEO).

*Evaluate:* Whenever we're trying to find the conclusion on Role and Main Conclusion, we remind ourselves that 90% of the arguments on those two question types are "upside-down" arguments: we hear the Conclusion before we hear the Evidence.

So we have to listen keenly for Opinion indicators or Rebuttals. The first two sentences of this argument provide some background context so that we know what NEO research is all about. Then the 3rd sentence gives us the author's opinion: government funding of NEO research is "worth it / not a waste".

*Goal:* We're being asked about the 2nd to last claim, which was one of the two premises. (99% of the time that two claims are joined by and, they are both premises). It also happens to be invoking an analogy between buying home insurance and funding NEO research.

So let's look for something like "an analogy / premise that supports the 3rd sentence".

**Answer choices**
**A. It connects an analogy made in the argument to the argument's conclusion.** — Correct (88% of students)
This wording is not instantly lovable, but it's not broken, so it ultimately wins the prize.

The author is arguing that it's good fiscal sense to invest in NEO research just as it's good fiscal sense to buy home insurance. (better to spend money now to avert having to spend even more money later).

The idea that this "connects" the analogy back to the argument's conclusion actually seems more appropriate as a description for the final claim, not the 2nd to last claim.

But this seems to be the best available answer.

**B. It is the overall conclusion of the argument.** — Wrong Role (1% of students)
The conclusion is the 3rd sentence. We're looking for something like "premise".

**C. It defines a key term used in a premise of the argument.** — Wrong Role (1% of students)
The only key term in the argument is NEO, and that is defined in the first sentence.

"Buying home insurance makes good fiscal sense" is not defining any term.

**D. It provides a contrast to the situation that is the main focus of the argument.** — Opposite (4% of students)
Bringing up home insurance is not meant to provide a contrast; it's meant to provide an analogous similarity.

**E. It is a general principle for which the argument attempts to provide support.** — Out of Scope: support provided (7% of students)
We could probably live with calling "it makes good fiscal sense to buy home insurance" a general principle.

But the argument never tries to provide support for this. "Why does it make good fiscal sense to buy home insurance?"

We probably know the answer to that (it's worth spending small amounts of money each month to protect yourself against a catastrophic financial hit if your house were ever destroyed), but the argument doesn't provide any justification for why home insurance makes sense.

## S1 · Q5 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Taste buds were the primary tool early humans used for testing foods. Sour taste warns of possible spoilage of food while bitterness is a warning of many poisons. Early humans also recognized sweet foods and salty foods as meeting nutritional needs. So the fact that people can now clearly distinguish these four tastes—sour, bitter, sweet, and salty—is completely explained by people's use of taste to test for the healthfulness of foods.

### Prompt

The reasoning in the argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Evidence:* Taste buds were the primary tool early humans used for testing foods.

Sour taste warns of possible spoilage of food while bitterness is a warning of many poisons.

Early humans also recognized sweet foods and salty foods as meeting nutritional needs.

*Conclusion:* The fact that people can now clearly distinguish these four tastes— sour, bitter, sweet, and salty— is completely explained by people's use of taste to test for the healthfulness of foods.

*Evaluate:* Wow! Completely explained? That's a mighty strong conclusion.

The evidence describes details about food that would have been important for early humans to know, like possible spoilage, and describes a correlation between these details and certain tastes like sourness and bitterness.

This correlation doesn't guarantee that needing to know about spoilage caused our ability to detect a sour taste. It doesn't guarantee that the need for nutrients caused our ability to detect sweetness or saltiness.

The evidence doesn't guarantee that this one use for our sense of taste completely explains our ability to distinguish these four tastes. The ability to distinguish these four tastes could have developed for more than one reason.

*Goal:* Since this is such a strong conclusion, a correct answer only needs to point out that there could be more than one reason for our ability to distinguish these four tastes.

**Answer choices**
**A. takes a necessary condition for the truth of its conclusion to be sufficient to justify that conclusion** — Necessary vs. Sufficient (5% of students)
This answer describes a conditional logic flaw:

```
Evidence: A → B
          B
Conclusion: A
```

This argument doesn't use a conditional statement as evidence and then draw a conclusion based on an illegal reversal.

**B. fails to consider that many people associate foods more with their smells than with their tastes** — Irrelevant Comparison (2% of students)
"Fails to consider" means that this answer choice should weaken the argument. The fact that some people associate foods with their smells more than their tastes doesn't prove or disprove the argument's claim about why we can distinguish between a certain four tastes.

**C. fails to consider that some nutritious foods are bitter when raw but not after being cooked** — Irrelevant Comparison (2% of students)
Without more information, this doesn't really impact the argument. Are these foods poisonous when raw but not after being cooked? Even if they aren't, do humans normally to eat these foods without cooking them first?

**D. fails to consider that most early humans ate a much more limited range of foodstuffs than do contemporary people** — Irrelevant Comparison (4% of students)
Does this mean that early humans had a greater need to test food before eating it, or did they have less need? Without more information, it's not clear how this comparison between early humans and contemporary people impacts the argument.

**E. takes what might be only a partial explanation of a phenomenon to be the complete explanation** — Correct (87% of students)
The need to test food could certainly help explain why people can now clearly distinguish these four tastes. It might easily be a partial explanation. But the evidence doesn't support a claim that the need to test food completely explains our ability to distinguish these tastes.

## S1 · Q6 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

To discourage congestion, a city raised on-street parking rates in a downtown business district. For restaurants in the district, whose customers require short-term parking, the surprising result was a small increase in sales.

### Prompt

Which one of the following, if true, contributes to an explanation of the increase in sales at restaurants in the district?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Given that …:* The city raised on-street parking rates in a downtown business district in order to discourage congestion.

*Why is it that …:* Restaurants in the district that require short-term parking ended up having a small increase in sales.

*Evaluate:* Raising the price of parking meters is supposed to discourage people from street parking; the fewer cars you have driving around searching for an open meter, the less congestion you'll theoretically have.

But after this city raised the price of parking meters, restaurants whose customers have to find short-term parking did even a little better than before. We would expect these restaurants to struggle, now that customers who want to go there are thinking, "Wait a sec … isn't that the place that requires street parking? Didn't they just raise the rates of the meters? Let's go somewhere else"

*Goal:* We could try to predict an answer, but on Paradox that's borderline risky, since the correct answer might surprise us.

Let's just look for a way to explain something that changed since the meters got more expensive that could somehow slightly benefit restaurants in the area.

**Answer choices**
**A. Customers of the district's businesses can also use private parking lots, which are generally more expensive than on-street parking.** — No Impact / Distinction (4% of students)
This answer could work if the private parkings lots were cheaper, but if they're more expensive than on-street parking, then this doesn't help. It will still be the case that parking was made more expensive overall, which should discourage some people from coming to the area, and yet restaurants there are doing slightly better than before.

The parking lots were presumably always more expensive, so this answer doesn't seem to offer us anything that has changed since street parking fees went up.

**B. Owners of the district's businesses, for the most part, initially opposed the raising of the on-street parking rates.** — No Impact / Distinction (0% of students)
It doesn't matter whether owners of businesses supported or opposed this measure. The measure passed.

We want to hear about something that changed once the parking fees were raised, in order to explain why restaurant sales went up. Learning about how owners felt before the parking fees were raised doesn't give us anything that has changed since street parking fees went up.

**C. Even before the change in parking rates, the staffs of the businesses in the district used only off-street parking.** — No Distinction (1% of students)
We can tell that this is wrong as soon as we read "even before the change in parking rates". After all, we want to learn about something that changed since the parking fees were raised, but this is talking about something that was equally true before and after the parking fees were raised.

If an answer doesn't tell us about something that's changed, then it's hard for it to explain something that's changed (i.e. that restaurants are making slightly more money than before).

**D. More expensive parking leads to greater turnover in cars parked in front of the businesses in the district.** — Correct (93% of students)
This answer is the only one that speaks to something that would have changed or be different, since the parking fees were raised. The parking fees made parking more expensive, and more expensive parking leads to greater turnover in cars parked in front of businesses.

Can we think about how greater turnover in cars parked out front (i.e. cars are parked in those spots for a shorter average period of time) would lead to restaurants making more money?

There are two possible angles they may have intended here:

1. If people who wanted to go to Restaurant X are often stymied by a lack of available parking, then they would just give up and find another place to eat. Once the parking rates go up and cars are vacating these parking spots more frequently, it could lead to there being more opportunities to park in front of Restaurant X, so more customers can choose to dine there.

2. If you're eating at Restaurant X and you parked at a meter that charges $4 / hr, then you're probably going to try to hurry up your meal. Maybe you only pay for 1 hour of parking and finish up your meal swiftly, whereas in the old days when the parking was cheaper, you may have paid for 90 mins of parking and had a more leisurely meal at the restaurant. The more people restaurants can get in and out of their restaurant, the more money the restaurant can make. So if a restaurant's patrons shorten their average stay from 90 minutes to 60 minutes, then the restaurant will have the capacity to serve more customers per day and thus make slightly more money.

**E. The business district is in competition with malls that offer free parking to their customers.** — Deepens Paradox (2% of students)
If parking fees go up, and people know that their other option is to go to a mall that offers free parking, then that should increase the amount of people going to malls, decrease the amount of people going to restaurants in the district, and thus hurt the business at those restaurants.

## S1 · Q9 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Gecko lizards are found in any environment where there is an abundant population of gnats. Gnats can survive only in wet climates. Because there are no gecko lizards living here, there must not be an abundant population of gnats here. Consequently, the climate here must not be wet.

### Prompt

The reasoning in the argument is flawed in that it

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion (consequently):* The climate here must not be wet.

*Intermediate Conclusion (must):* There aren't lots of gnats here.

*Evidence (because):* There are no geckos here.

```
Anywhere you find lots of gnats, you'll find Geckos.
Lots of gnats → Geckos

Gnats can survive only in wet climates.
Gnats → wet climate
```

*Evaluate:* We're doing a Flaw question, and we see conditional logic indicators (any, only). What should we be thinking? We should deeply suspect that this is going to commit the #1 Famous Flaw, Necessary vs. Sufficient, which just means that the author presents a conditional logic premise and then botches using it by making an illegal reversal or negation.

The author draws two conclusions, so we should look at each one.

The first (intermediate) conclusion is that there must not be abundant gnats. Why? Because there are no geckos, and geckos are found in every environment with abundant gnats.

```
~Geckos → ~Lots of Gnats
Here = ~Geckos
Thus, Here = ~Lots of Gnats
```

This mini-argument seems airtight. It just applies a fact (no geckos) that triggers the contrapositive of the conditional in the first sentence (lots of gnats → geckos).

Okay the main conclusion was that the climate is not wet.

We were told that gnats survive only in a wet climate. Only / only if are necessary indicators (the only is sufficient), so the idea of "wet climate" goes on the right of the arrow.

```
Gnats → Wet climate
~Wet climate → ~Gnats
```

The author's move from the 2nd to last claim to the final claim is, "If there is not an abundant population of gnats, then it must not be a wet climate." But that move looks like this:

```
~Gnats → ~Wet climate
```

That's not what the rule said. It said "if yes gnats, then yes wet climate", but the author is trying to argue "if no gnats, then no wet climate". That's an illegal negation.

*Goal:* Look for an answer describing the author's Necessary vs. Sufficient error when it comes to the 2nd conditional (the gnats / wet climate one).

Or look for an answer making the conversational objection of, "Just because there are no gnats doesn't mean it's not a wet climate. There can be wet climates that have no abundant population of gnats".

**Answer choices**
**A. presumes, without providing justification, that gecko lizards can survive only in environments with abundant gnat populations** — Not Assumed / Too Strong: only (24% of students)
This answer is itself making a Necessary vs. Sufficient error.

We were told that abundant gnats are sufficient to indicate geckos.
lots of gnats → geckos

This answer is saying that the author was assuming that abundant gnats are necessary for geckos.
geckos → lots of gnats

This answer would work if the author had messed up the conditional in the first sentence, because it's saying, "The author incorrectly saw the conditional in the first sentence in reverse". The author didn't misuse the conditional in the first sentence; he botched the one in the second sentence.

**B. presumes, without providing justification, that all wet environments contain abundant populations of gnats** — Correct (68% of students)
Did the author assume that
Wet climate → abundant gnats
~abundant gnats → ~Wet climate ?

Yes! After all, the author's final move was thinking, "There is no abundant population of gnats here. Consequently, the climate must not be wet".

That's making this move:
~abundant gnats → ~Wet climate

When we're reading a Necessary Assumption answer choice, on Necessary Assumption or on Flaw (takes for granted / presumes / fails to establish), and that answer is conditional, we generally want to just diagram how that conditional looks, and then ask ourselves whether the author made that move. Did the author go from establishing the idea on the left to thinking/concluding the idea on the right?

Crucially, we need to consider the contrapositive of the answer we're looking at, because correct answers are usually written in a contraposed form of how we would recognize the argument.

For example, here the answer gives us

Wet climate → abundant gnats
but the contrapositive form matches the argument.
~abundant gnats → ~Wet climate

**C. does not consider whether small populations of gnats can survive in climates that are not wet** — Not an Objection (4% of students)
Since this begins with fails to consider / overlooks the possibility, we can ask ourselves whether the following idea would be a weakening Objection.

Can we hurt the author's argument by saying, "small populations of gnats can survive in non wet climates"?

No that would be easily tolerated by the author. He thinks there is no abundant population of gnats, but he can still allow for there being a small population of gnats. And he thinks this climate is not wet, which is what the answer is also saying.

So this "objection" is perfectly compatible with what the author was saying.

A correct answer would be
does not consider whether small populations or no populations of gnats are sometimes found in climates that are wet.

This is just an awkward way of saying, "just because there's not an abundant population of gnats doesn't mean the climate isn't wet. You could have a wet climate, that doesn't have an abundant population of gnats."

**D. does not mention whether gecko lizards eat anything besides gnats** — Irrelevant (2% of students)
It's true, the author didn't mention whether geckos eat things other than gnats. In fact, the author never mentioned that geckos eat gnats.

Why are we talking about what geckos eat? That has nothing to do with the logical flaw of this argument, which involves assuming that "since gnats require a wet climate, any place without gnats must not be a wet climate".

**E. fails to establish that some gecko lizards could not survive in a dry climate containing only a small population of gnats** — Not Assumed (2% of students)
When an answer begins presumes / takes for granted / fails to establish, we treat it like Necessary Assumption.

Was this author needing to assume that
"some geckos couldn't survive in a dry climate containing only a small population of gnats".
It doesn't seem like the author was every specifically addressing any scenario like that.

Let's negate it and see if it would weaken:
"no gecko lizards could not survive in a dry climate with few gnats".
Let's remove the double negative:
"all geckos could survive in a dry climate with few gnats"

Would it hurt the argument to say, "All geckos could survive in a dry climate with few gnats"?

Nope. We can really only hurt the argument by saying something like, "Isn't it possible that there are wet climates that lack abundant populations of gnats"?

**Argument breakdown**

*Evidence:*

```

The final sentence is a prediction about the future which is based on those conditional statements. Predictions in LSAT arguments tend to be conclusions, and the overall structure of this argument points to the final sentence being the conclusion.

```
Chen support → new art center

Therefore, new art center depends on Chen support

```

Unlike the original argument, the evidence in this argument describes more than one possible outcome. Also, the conclusion of this argument states that success does not depend on Madsen's choice. This is not the same as the original argument, which concludes that a certain outcome will occur.

## S1 · Q12 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Art history professor: Costa criticizes my theories about the distinction between baroque and neoclassical Austrian painting. He argues that since there are no features possessed by all and only the works from a given historical period, assigning works of art to period styles is intellectually bankrupt. His reasoning can be discounted, however, since his own current theories on the transition from classical to romantic French opera also presuppose such an assignment.

### Prompt

Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the art history professor's argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Costa's reasoning can be discounted.

*Evidence (since):* His own current theories presuppose the same assignment he's criticizing me for.

*Evaluate:* We could get into the details of Costa said, but they are immaterial to recognizing the Famous Flaw that's happening here.

Whenever we're doing Flaw and we see a Rebuttal style argument, we know to look for these two of the 10 Famous Flaws:

1. Ad Hominem — in which an author dismisses the view of person X because person X has some biased interest in the matter or because person X has come conflicting past behavior.

2. Unproven vs. Proven False — in which an author concludes that X is false, since there is a lack of evidence that X is true or since someone's attempt to prove that X is true was a flawed attempt.

The conclusion here isn't saying that Costa is wrong, so we're not dealing with #2. It's saying Costa's view can be discounted / dismissed / disregarded, which is more the style of #1.

Why are we discounting Costa's critique? Because "Costa does the same thing". This is a little like getting busted by Mom for playing with matches and trying to argue that we don't deserve punishment because "Timmy did the same thing!"

Mom will say, "Okay, then both you and Timmy did something wrong."

Similarly, we could say to this professor, "Both you and Costa deserve to be criticized for trying to assign works of art to period styles."

Someone who commits tax fraud should still be listened to when they accuse person X of committing tax fraud. Someone who had their license suspended for a DUI should still be listened to when they tell person X that X has had too much to drink to get behind the wheel.

*Goal:* Look for something saying, "Just because Costa is guilty of X doesn't mean we should discount Costa when he argues that we are guilty of X".

The answer can also say that the author fails to address the substantive point Costa makes, distracting us instead with irrelevant details about what Costa himself does in his theories.

**Answer choices**
**A. The argument confuses a necessary condition for discounting a person's reasoning with a sufficient condition for discounting a person's reasoning.** — Not Necessary vs. Sufficient (2% of students)
This answer describes the #1 famous flaw Necessary vs. Sufficient, in which the author presents a conditional logic premise and then illegally applies that rule in a backwards or negated fashion.

There's no conditional logic premise here. The only premise is "Costa's own theories presuppose such an assignment".

**B. The argument overlooks the possibility that theoreticians can hold radically different theories at different times.** — Not an Objection (2% of students)
Since this answer starts with fails to consider / overlooks the possibility, we can ask ourselves whether the idea that follows would Weaken.

Can we hurt the professor's argument by telling her that "theoreticians can hold radically different theories at different times"?

No, that doesn't seem to be a relevant objection. Her premise is just about Costa's current theories, so who cares what different theories he (or other theoreticians) held at different times.

**C. The argument rejects the reasoning on which a criticism is based merely on the grounds that that very criticism could be applied to theories of the person who offered it.** — Correct (89% of students)
We could understand this answer to be a "conflicting behavior" version of the famous Ad Hominem flaw: "My dad tells me not to smoke cigarettes because it will mess up my lungs. His reasoning can be discounted, however, since he smokes cigarettes". Obviously, Dad could be right about his advice and wrong about his behavior (do as I say, not as I do).

We could otherwise understand this answer by reacting to its structure:
the argument does X merely on the grounds that Y

This means that X should match the conclusion and Y should match the evidence. Was the conclusion "rejecting the reasoning on which a criticism is based"?

Yes: Costa's reasoning should be discounted was our conclusion, and Costa's reasoning was being used to criticize the professor's theories.

Does the evidence say that "that same criticism could be applied to the theories of the person who offered the criticism"?

Yes! Costa said that it was intellectually bankrupt to assign works of art to period styles, but the author's premise (since) is saying that Costa's own theories presuppose an assignment of works of art to period styles.

**D. The argument presumes, without providing justification, that what is true of art in general must also be true of every particular type of art.** — Not Part vs. Whole (4% of students)
This answer describes the famous flaw Part vs. Whole, in which the author assumes that a trait that belongs to the Whole is therefore possessed by each Part of the whole (or vice versa).

If an answer says "presumes that what is true of X must also be true of Y", then we want to see if X matches the evidence and Y matches the conclusion.

Did the premise talk about "what is true of art in general"?

Heavens, no. The premise says "Costa's own current theories presuppose the same assignment of works of art to period styles". We can stop reading here.

The conclusion also does not match; it is not a claim about "every particular style of art". It's the claim that "Costa's reasoning can be discounted".

**E. The argument presumes, without providing justification, that theories about one type of art cannot be compared to theories about another.** — Not an Assumption (4% of students)
Since this answer starts with presumes / takes for granted, we can ask ourselves whether the idea that follows would be a Necessary Assumption.

Was the author assuming that "theories about one type of art cannot be compared to theories about another"?

Nope. That's a crazy-extreme claim, and our author isn't saying anything related to that. She's only talking about Costa's theories. Also, to the extent that the author is saying that Costa's theories are guilty of the same "sin" that Costa accuses the author's theories of, the author seems to think that at least some theories can be compared to each other.

## S1 · Q13 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Columnist: Computer voice-recognition technology currently cannot distinguish between homophones such as "their" and "there." As a consequence, until voice-recognition technology is improved to recognize and utilize grammatical and semantic relations among words, voice-recognition programs will not accurately translate a computer user's spoken words into written text.

### Prompt

Which one of the following is an assumption required by the columnist's argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Until voice recognition technology can recognize and utilize grammatical and semantic clues, voice recognition will not accurately translate text.

*Evidence:* Voice recognition cannot currently accurately tell the difference between homophones.

*Evaluate:* Think about the general form of an argument that says, "Problem X currently prevents us from having Outcome Y. Thus, until we do Action Z, we won't have Outcome Y."

What is being assumed?

This is assuming that Action Z is necessary in order to solve Problem X. But what if there are other actions that could also solve Problem X? If that's the case, then we needn't say something as harsh as, "If we don't take Action Z, we'll never get to Outcome Y."

*Goal:* The author seems to be assuming that, "if we don't take advantage of grammatical and semantic clues, we'll never solve the problem of homophones." Or to put it another way, she seems to assume that "solving the problem of homophones requires using grammatical and semantic clues."

**Answer choices**
**A. In order for computer voice-recognition technology to distinguish between homophones, it must be able to recognize grammatical and semantic relations among words.** — Correct (53% of students)
If we negated this, it would turn into an Objection.

NEGATION: there is a way for computer voice-recognition to distinguish between homophones that doesn't involve recognizing grammatical / semantic relations.

Since the author's conclusion is so strongly worded, creating any room for doubt is a big objection.

Formally, the author's conclusion was this
```
~recognizing            won't
grammatical and -------> accurately
semantic relations      translate
```

and the premise was that

```
can't           won't
distinguish ----> accurately
homophones      translate
```

So this answer is giving us that 1st to 2nd link:

```
~recognizing            can't
grammatical and → distinguish
semantic relations      homophones
```

**B. If voice-recognition technology is improved to recognize and utilize grammatical and semantic relations among words, voice-recognition programs will accurately translate spoken words into text.** — Opposite Logic of Conclusion (19% of students)
The author concluded "until we have X, we won't have Y". This is accusing him of thinking, "Once we have X, we will have Y".

Those are not equivalent expressions.

"Until we have strawberry seeds, we won't have strawberries growing in our yard" doesn't mean "Once we have seeds, we will have strawberries growing in our yard".

The strawberry seeds are necessary, not sufficient.

**C. Humans can distinguish between homophones without using information about the grammatical and semantic relations among words.** — Out of Scope: how humans distinguish (1% of students)
First of all, there's no reason why the method used by computer voice recognition has to match the methods used by humans.

Secondly, it feels more likely that the author thinks that humans are using grammatical / semantic relations to distinguish between homophones, so this answer sounds more like the opposite of what the author is probably thinking.

**D. Unless voice-recognition technology can distinguish between words that are homophones, it cannot recognize grammatical and semantic relations among words.** — Reversed Logic (27% of students)
The author is assuming "If it can't recognize grammatical/semantic stuff, then it won't be able to distinguish between homophones", whereas this answer says

"if it can't distinguish between homophones, then it can't recognize grammatical/semantic stuff".

We could try to negate this and see how it fails to hurt the argument, but that's much harder than looking at its conditional logic representation and asking ourselves if the author made that move.

Negating this would be saying that "it's possible that voice-recognition could recognize grammatical/semantic relations without being able to distinguish between homophones."

To return to an example from a previous answer choice, consider the difference between saying,
"Unless we have strawberry seeds, we can't grow strawberries in our yard"
vs.
"Unless we can grow strawberries in our yard, we won't have strawberry seeds".

That second one means something different. It's possible to have strawberry seeds but NOT be able to grow strawberries in your yard, because there might be other things also required.

Similarly, it doesn't weaken the author's argument to if voice recognition CAN recognize grammatical / semantic stuff but can't distinguish between homophones, because there might be other things besides recognizing grammatical / semantic stuff that is ALSO required before the voice recognition technology can distinguish between homophones.

**E. Computer programs that check the spelling and grammar of written text are currently able to distinguish between homophones.** — Out of Scope: what spell check can do (1% of students)
The author is only making an argument about what is needed for voice recognition technology to distinguish between homophones. It's irrelevant to that argument whether other things currently are / aren't capable of distinguishing between homophones.

It's possible that a computer that checks spelling / grammar still can't distinguish between homophones because it doesn't understand semantic relationships.

## S1 · Q18 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

In many families adults speak to babies in simplified language. Yet constantly repeating simple phrases like "Nice kitty. See the kitty?" does not provide extra help to children in learning a language. We know this because there are families in which no one speaks to babies this way, yet the children in these families master the grammatical structure of their language just as well and as quickly as other children do.

### Prompt

Which one of the following is an assumption on which the argument depends?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Constantly repeating simple phrases does not provide extra help to children in learning a language.

*Evidence (we know this because):* There are families in which no one speaks to babies this way, yet the children in these families master the grammatical structure of their language just as well as and as quickly as other children do.

*Evaluate:* There are two big problems with this argument:

The conclusion is trying to prove that "X does not help people to do Y", whereas the evidence is only going to establish that "X is not necessary for people to do Y".

It's akin to this flawed argument: "Getting a 175 does not help people get into Harvard. We know this because there are students who got lower then a 175, and they still got into Harvard".

We would object to this Harvard argument and to the baby argument the same way — "Just because people can do Y without needing to do X, that doesn't prove that X isn't helpful to those who do it."

This author is assuming that "If some people don't need X to do Y, then X isn't helpful in doing Y."

The second problem with the argument is that the conclusion is about whether or not something "helps children to learn a language", whereas the evidence is about whether or not children "mastered grammatical structure as well / as quickly as did other kids".

The author seems to think that, "If repeating simple phrases were going to help a kid learn a language, we'd see a difference in terms of how fast/effectively they master grammatical structures." But maybe repeating simple phrases is supposed to help a kid learn a language for a different reason.

*Goal:* Look for an answer speaking to the assumption of, "If some children can learn grammatical structures as well as any other children without having simple phrases repeated to them, then having simple phrases repeated to children doesn't help them learn a language".

Or look for an answer ruling out an objection, where if we negate it, it might allow us to argue that repeating simple phrases does provide extra help to children learning a language.

**Answer choices**
**A. Babies pay no extra attention to spoken phrases with simple grammatical structures.** — Irrelevant Distinction (19% of students)
If we negate this "not / no" and get, "babies do pay more attention to phrases with simple structures", does that weaken the argument?

Not in any clear way. This is talking about paying more attention not being helped extra when it comes to learning the language.

And the answer is just talking about any simply-structured phrase, not repeating simple phrases. Our author might agree that it's better to speak to babies with simple structures because they pay more attention, but still say that repeating these simple phrases has no learning value.

**B. Speaking to babies in simplified language could impair their language learning.** — Not Required (3% of students)
This would strengthen the author's conclusion, if true, but it's not necessary. The author doesn't need repeating simple phrases to damage the learning process. He's only trying to prove that it doesn't help the learning process.

If we negate this and get, "Speaking in simple language cannot impair their language learning", that doesn't hurt the argument. The author can respond, "Cool. I never said it impaired their learning, just that it doesn't provide any extra help to their learning."

**C. Any child who has mastered the grammatical structure of a language has learned the language.** — Correct (69% of students)
This seems pretty strongly worded and thus might not be tempting on a first pass.

Since it's conditional, it's best to ask ourselves whether the author's thinking seemed to make this move:

```
if a child has mastered → then the child has
grammatical structure       learned the language
```

Yes, the author seems to be thinking that. Her conclusion is about whether or not repeating simple phrases helps in learning a language.

Her evidence was about whether or not children who hadn't had simple phrases repeated to them had mastered the grammatical structures of their language.

The author thus must have been assuming a link / equivalence between those two. The author saying, "Look at these kids! No one repeated simple phrases to them, and THEY learned the language just as well and as quickly as other children do."

But she didn't say they learned the language just as well/quickly. She said they mastered the grammatical structure just as well/quickly. So she's assuming, "if you mastered the grammatical structure, you learned the language."

We don't usually recommend negating conditional answers, but if we negated this, it would be saying, "Hey, author — it's possible that a child has mastered the grammatical structure of a language, but hasn't learned the language".

Would that weaken? Sure, in the same sense we were discussing. We could say, "author, your evidence doesn't even seem clearly relevant. We're talking about whether or not repeating simple phrases helps a child learn a language, and you're talking about whether or not children have mastered grammatical structure. Those aren't the same."

**D. Many linguists believe that speaking to babies in simplified language helps the babies learn language.** — Out of Scope: linguists (2% of students)
The argument never brings up linguists at all. The author doesn't need to assume anything about them.

If we negated this answer, it would be saying, "Zero, or practically zero, linguists believe that speaking to babies in simplified language helps them learn language", and that would actually just strengthen the author's conclusion.

**E. To learn a language one must acquire its vocabulary as well as its grammatical structure.** — Opposite / Out of Scope: vocabulary (7% of students)
The author is actually acting like acquiring grammatical structure is all there is to it.

Negating this answer would say, "learning a language doesn't require vocabulary in addition to grammatical structure", which would just strengthen the author's argument, since he is pointing to kids who have mastered grammar and acting like they've learned the language.

## S1 · Q19 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Medical researcher: At the Flegco Corporation, all employees whose work involves lifting heavy objects wear back belts to prevent back injuries. However, a study found that Flegco employees who wear back belts are actually more likely to suffer back injuries than are employees who do not wear back belts. This suggests that back belts do not help to prevent back injuries.

### Prompt

The reasoning in the medical researcher's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on which one of the following grounds?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Back belts do not help to prevent back injuries.

*Evidence:* Everybody at Flegco that does heavy lifting wears back belts, and a study found that employees who wear back belts are more likely to suffer back injuries than those who don't.

*Evaluate:* With an Anti-Causal conclusion like this, we're trying to object by arguing that there is a causal benefit to wearing back belts.

Given That
the people who wear back belts are more likely to suffer back injuries than those who don't
How Could We Argue That
back belts do help prevent back injuries.

Our normal objections for Anti-Causal arguments are:
- the two groups aren't fair to compare to begin with
- things would be even worse without X (in this case, back belts)

In this argument, we already know one salient difference between people who do / don't wear back belts: the people who do wear back belts have work that involves lifting heavy objects. The people who don't wear back belts don't have to lift heavy objects.

I'm not surprised to hear that "people, wearing back belts, moving furniture all day" have more back injuries than "people, not wearing back belts, sitting at a computer all day".

This would be like arguing, "People who wear bulletproof vests are actually more likely to die of gunshot wounds than are people who don't wear bulletproof vests. Thus, bulletproof vests don't help to prevent death from gunshot wounds."

Of course they do — it's just that people who wear bulletproof vests have jobs that put them in the line of fire, so their chances of dying from a gunshot wound are much, much higher to begin with than are people with other jobs.

*Goal:* Find an answer saying that the author is failing to consider that things would be way worse without back belts for all these people doing heavy lifting, or pointing out the objection that it's unfair to compare the likelihood of back injuries of belt-wearers vs. non-belt-wearers, since the former have such a higher chance of getting back injuries in the first place based on the nature of the heavy lifting work they do.

**Answer choices**
**A. It compares the incidence of back injury between two groups who probably do not have the same risk factors for back injury.** — Correct (76% of students)
One group consists of people whose work involves lifting heavy objects. The other group consists of people whose work does not involve lifting heavy objects. Those two groups probably do not have the same risk factors for back injury.

If non-lifters have a 10% chance of back injury, while lifters (without a belt) have a 50% chance of back injury, then putting back belts on these lifters might only bring their risk of back injury down to 20%.

This situation would fit the evidence (belt wearers are more likely to get back injuries than the non-lifters), but it wouldn't fit the conclusion, because clearly back belts do help to prevent injuries if it lowered the lifters' risk from 50% to 20%.

**B. It fails to address the possibility that Flegco Corporation employees are more likely to wear back belts than are employees who perform similar tasks in other corporations.** — Not an Objection (1% of students)
It doesn't hurt this argument at all to say that Flegco employees are more likely to wear back belts than are employees elsewhere. The author isn't claiming Flegco is representative in any way.

She just looks at their employees, sees that the belt-wearing group gets more back injuries than the non-belt wearing group, and thinks that the belts apparently aren't helping them.

**C. It takes for granted that if a factor is associated with an increased likelihood of a certain effect, that factor must causally contribute to that effect.** — Bad Conclusion Match (13% of students)
When we see an answer structured like
takes for granted that if X, Y
then X should match the evidence, and Y should match the conclusion.

Does the evidence discuss a factor associated with an increased likelihood of a certain effect?

Yes, wearing a back belt is a factor associated with an increased likelihood of back injuries.

Does the conclusion say that "back belts must causally contribute to back injuries"?

Nope. It says back belts do not help prevent injuries, but it doesn't say that belts cause injuries.

**D. It confuses the claim that a phenomenon does not causally contribute to a certain effect with the claim that that phenomenon causally contributes to preventing that effect.** — Bad Premise Match (7% of students)
When we see answers structured like
confuses X with Y
then one of them should be Evidence and the other should be Conclusion.

The conclusion makes a claim that a phenomenon does not causally contribute to a certain effect: the phenomenon of wearing back belts does not causally contribute to injury-prevention.

Does the evidence make a claim that that phenomenon causally contributes to preventing that effect? No, that would have sounded like "wearing back belts causally contributes to preventing injury-prevention".

If this sounds like tortured language/logic, it is! Bail from this answer. It does not match.

Preventing injury-prevention = making more vulnerable to injury.

Just as we said with (C), the author is never saying that in evidence or conclusion that back belts cause more back injuries.

**E. It fails to address the possibility that even if a factor is sufficient to produce a certain effect, its presence may not be necessary in order for that effect to be produced.** — Wrong Flaw (3% of students)
This describes the famous flaw of Necessary vs. Sufficient, in which the author presents a conditional rule and then applies that rule in an illegally backwards or opposite fashion to derive her conclusion.

There was no conditional logic here, unless you count the "all" in the first sentence, "If you work at Flegco and your job involves lifting heavy objects, then you wear a back belt."

If the author had committed the Necessary vs. Sufficient flaw with that rule, it would have sounded like "Melanie wears a back belt. Thus Melanie must work at Flegco, lifting heavy objects."

## S1 · Q24 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Nuts are high in calories. All else being equal, the more calories one consumes, the more likely one is to become overweight. However, studies have found that people who eat nuts regularly are actually less likely to be overweight than people who never eat nuts.

### Prompt

Which one of the following, if true, would most help to resolve the apparent discrepancy described above?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Given that ...:* Nuts are high in calories, and that the more calories you consume, the more likely you become overweight.

*How is it that ...:* People who regularly eat nuts are less likely to be overweight than people who never eat nuts.

*Evaluate / Goal:* Since these nut-eaters have a high calorie habit, which skews towards being overweight, we need to know something else about nut-eaters that skews towards not being overweight (maybe they exercise more / maybe they have higher metabolisms / maybe they eat less of other high-calorie foods like desserts).

Look for a difference between nut-eaters and nut-avoiders that relates to weight gain / weight loss / burning calories.

**Answer choices**
**A. The likelihood of becoming overweight depends not only on how many calories one consumes but also on how rapidly one burns those calories through physical activity.** — No Distinction (7% of students)
This doesn't present any distinction between people who eat nuts and those who don't, so it's functionally useless to us.

If they had told us that "people who eat nuts "burn calories faster than those who don't", then we've got ourselves an answer.

**B. Due to the fact that nuts are rich in calories, consuming a sufficiently large quantity produces a feeling of satiation.** — No Impact (11% of students)
This answer just tells us that people who eat nuts love those nuts. It doesn't give us a way to explain why people who eat nuts are less likely to be overweight.

**C. If people who avoid a certain kind of food are more likely to be overweight than people who consume that food regularly, then that food should not be avoided for weight loss purposes.** — No Impact (1% of students)
I think this answer choice is lost. It seems to have wandered into a Paradox question, but it belongs on some other task, like Strengthen+Principle.

We don't have an argument here. The idea of what foods should / shouldn't be avoided is totally meaningless to this conversation.

We're just trying to find an answer that causally explains how people who eat nuts are less likely to be overweight.

**D. On average, people who never eat nuts consume the same total calories per day as people who regularly eat nuts.** — Weak Impact (3% of students)
This helps to explain why nut-eaters are not more likely to be overweight. Even though they eat this high-calorie food, they're total calories per day are the same as people who don't eat nuts.

But that would make those two groups equally likely to be overweight.

We need a way to go beyond that and explain why people who eat nuts are less likely to be overweight.

**E. Most people who regularly eat nuts eat correspondingly less of foods whose taste, unlike that of nuts, stimulates a hunger response.** — Correct (77% of students)
This gives us a potential mechanism for explaining who nut-eaters are less likely to be overweight.

Most of them (most = strong, which is what we want on Strengthen / Weaken / Paradox) eat less of foods that stimulate a hunger response.

In other words, eating other foods stimulates a hunger response, which presumably leads to more eating, which leads to more calories.

Eating nuts is unlike that.

Person 1 eats nuts. Person 2 doesn't eat nuts; they eat food X instead.

The taste of nuts doesn't stimulate a hunger response.

The taste of X does, which means Person 2 gets hungry and decides to also eat more X or to eat food Y. Food Y might also stimulate a hunger response, and so you can create a positive feedback loop where the taste of the last food you ate stimulates hunger for more food.

Thus, even if nuts are higher in calories than are foods X or Y, a person eating nuts stops with nuts. Meanwhile, non-nut people get caught in this feedback loop of eating lower-calorie foods that stimulate a response to eat even more foods.

So this gives us a potential story for how people who eat nuts, which are high in calories, might still end up consuming fewer calories overall (since nuts don't stimulate a hunger response as other foods do), and thus could be less likely to be overweight than people who don't eat nuts.

Consider this "argument":
If A then B. Thus, if A then D, since B presupposes C.

Without thinking too hard, we can guess that the missing link is between C and D, since they are only mentioned once, whereas A and B are each mentioned twice.

Metaphor presupposes literal language. Literal language involves regimentation.
Metaphor → literal language → regimentation

If you're against regimentation, as they say Cummings was, then by contrapositive, you would be against literal language and against metaphor.

The author is saying, "If Cummings stood against metaphor, then Cummings stood against something essential to the work he did."

*Goal:* Thus, we want something like "metaphor was essential to the work Cummings did".

**Answer choices**
**A. Not all poets use metaphor.** — Unrelated to Goal / Weak Language (1% of students)
There will almost never be correct answers on Sufficient Assumption, Strengthen, Weaken, or Paradox that use "at least one" language like could, may, might, some, sometimes, not all, need not, not necessarily.

The conclusion is that Cummings stood against something essential to the work he did. What was essential to the work he did? The evidence never defined that, so the correct answer must.

**B. Metaphor was essential to E. E. Cummings's work.** — Correct (86% of students)
This is what we predicted.

If metaphor was essential to Cumming's work and Cummings stood against metaphor, then we would prove the conclusion that Cummings stood against something essential to his work.

Do we know that Cummings stood against metaphor?

Yes, indirectly, since metaphor presupposes regimentation, and Cummings stood against regimentation.

**C. There can be no literal language without metaphor.** — Unrelated to Goal (9% of students)
The conclusion is that Cummings stood against something essential to the work he did. What was essential to the work he did? The evidence never defined that, so the correct answer must.

**D. Poetry cannot be regimented or standardized.** — Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
The conclusion is that Cummings stood against something essential to the work he did. What was essential to the work he did? The evidence never defined that, so the correct answer must.

**E. E. E. Cummings did not use literal language.** — Opposite of Goal (3% of students)

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* It's a myth that a significant amount of music online is the result of people downloading others' music and reworking it into new music of their own. (i.e. there is not a significant amount of music online that came from people downloading something, sampling it in a new creation, and uploading that creation)

*Evidence:* 99% of people who download music do not publish new music of their own on the internet.

If only 1% of people who download music publish new music of their own on the Internet, how could we say a ton of music online came about by people downloading music, sampling it into something they made, and then publishing it?

Maybe among those 1% are a lot of avid music producers. 99% of us are just downloading, but the 1% are an army of people like Drake and Post Malone, publishing song after song after song.

To put it a different way, say there are only 100 people. The author is thinking that since only 1 of them (1%) publishes their music online, then only 1% (an insignificant amount) of the music online could be coming in the form of download/sample/publish.

But, if this 1 person creates 50 songs that way, and the other 99 people don't publish any new music of their own, then when it comes to those 100 people, the only music they're contributing to the Internet is those 50 songs that were of the download/sample/publish variety.

Does it hurt the argument to say that "a small number of people can create a significant amount of new music"? Sure!

It's basically doing the classic LSAT move of "your Evidence is compatible with your Conclusion being wrong."

The Evidence was that only 1% of people could possibly be contributing to the pool of music that results from downloading others' music, sampling it, and publishing it in a new work of your own.

If this small number of people can create a significant amount of new music, then we can say to the author, "See, buddy? There is a significant amount of music that comes from downloading / sampling / publishing."

Does it hurt the author's argument to say that "it's wicked easy to publish music on the Internet"?

Nope. He would just say, "Cool, well however easy it is, that doesn't change the fact that 99% of people don't do it."

If a defense attorney manages to convince the jury that Tony wasn't the killer, she's done her job. She doesn't also have to solve the crime for the police.

Similarly, the author can win his argument by convincing us that most of the music doesn't come from Source X. He doesn't also have to explain for us where most of the music does come from.

Alternate explanations definitely can help someone prove that "X wasn't the cause of Y", but they're not necessary.

A defense attorney can get Tony off the hook by selling the jury on an alternate story (maybe Tony didn't kill Boris, but rather Boris's disgruntled former employee killed him), but the attorney can also get Tony off the hook by just presenting a clear alibi (the murder happened in Denver on August 1, and Tony was in Paris that night, as all these witnesses testify).

If we said, "You know sometimes people download music into new creations but don't publish it on the Internet", the author would be like, "Yeah, I know. That's part of why I said there isn't a significant amount of music on the Internet that has been downloaded / re-worked / published".

What people prefer is totally out of scope. The truth value of this conclusion is merely about the quantity of a certain type of music on the Internet.

The author certainly did commit herself to the extreme assumption that in 100% of cases, Internet users prefer original music to music that has downloaded samples in it.

## S2 · Q10 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Wei: A respected automobile expert claims that most cars will develop transmission problems unless their transmissions are serviced at least once every three years. But this claim is false. I have owned both of my cars for over twelve years, I have never had the transmission serviced on either one, and neither one has ever developed a transmission problem.

### Prompt

The pattern of flawed reasoning in Wei's argument is most similar to the pattern of flawed reasoning exhibited by which one of the following?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion (but - rebuttal):* This claim is false.

rephrase: Even if you don't get the transmission serviced at least one every three years, most cars won't develop transmission problems.

*Evidence:* I've owned both of my cars for over 12 years, never had the transmission services, and never developed a transmission problem.

*Evaluate:* This argument would be similar to arguing, "A respected epidemiologist claimed that most people will get a pretty mild case of covid unless they are older than 65 or immunocompromised. But this claim is false. After all, I am younger than 65 with a normal immune system and my case of covid nearly hospitalized me!"

We would respond, "Sorry to hear that, dude, but you're just atypical. It's still true that for most non-geriatric people with normal immune systems, they will have a mild case."

We can't fight a generalization with a handful of data points. When we say "most NBA players are taller than average", someone can't argue with us and say, "Nuh-uh ... average height is 6' 1" and several point guards in the NBA are only 6 feet or shorter".

So the flaw in the original argument is thinking that, "Since MY cars didn't have transmission problems, even though I never serviced the transmission, the same would be true for MOST cars."

*Goal:* Parallel Flaw loves to test these Famous Flaws (Nec vs. Suff / Part vs. Whole / Sampling / Ad Hominem). Since this argument has a Sampling issue (the author is fighting a genera claim based on a just a couple of nonconfirming data points), let's look for an argument relying on a sample that is too small.

The sample might be from personal experience, as was the case here, but using personal experience isn't why this is flawed. It's just because the set of data points yielded by personal experience is so shallow.

Similarly, this argument had a rebuttal structure, so the correct answer might, but the Flaw was assuming that "what was true for this small sample of cases would be true for most cases", so to replicate the flaw we wouldn't necessarily need a rebuttal structure.

**Answer choices**
**A. My veterinarian told me that most cats who do not have yearly teeth cleaning will develop gum problems. However, I have not had my cat's teeth cleaned for the past ten years, and she has not had any gum problems. Therefore, my veterinarian was wrong.** — Correct (93% of students)
This has the same structure and flaw as the original.

The author is rebutting a general claim, "most cats who don't have yearly cleanings will have gum problems", by pointing to one lone data point from personal experience.

"Nuh-uhhhh ... my cat didn't have yearly cleanings, and she doesn't have gum problems."

**B. Car dealers claim that customers save money in the long run if they purchase extended warranties for new cars. But car dealers have a strong interest in making money from the warranties. Thus, the car dealers' claim is false.** — Different Flaw (0% of students)
This is committing the Ad Hominem flaw, in which an author dismisses someone else's view because the source of that view has a biased motive or has conflicting past behavior.

In the original argument, the author is disputing a general claim by saying that her personal experience doesn't line up with that claim.

In this answer, the author is disputing a general claim by saying that the source of the claim has a biased interest in that claim being true.

**C. A high-ranking traffic safety official claims that driving over the speed limit increases the chance of having an automobile accident. But this claim is not true. I never drive over the speed limit, and I have had two accidents in the past year.** — Bad Evidence Match / Additional Flaw (5% of students)
Overall, this answer is promising. An author is rebutting an expert's claim on the basis of the shallow sample of their own personal experience.

However, this argument has an additional flaw. Its putative counterexample isn't really a counterexample.

Suppose we're considering the offensive claim,
"blond people are usually not very smart".

A counterexample would be saying, "Nuh-uhh. Stacey is a blond person and she is very smart."

What this answer choice did was attempt to provide a counterexample by saying, "Nuh-uhh. Stacey has black hair, and she is not very smart."

When we say "Most A's are B" or "All A's are B", then the feel of a counterexample is something that is "A, but not B".

This answer is providing something that is "B, but not A".

For this argument to match the original, the premises should be saying, "This is not true. After all, I drive over the speed limit, but I have not had accidents."

**D. Boot manufacturers claim that a good pair of boots will last at least five years. But this claim is false. The boots I got on sale at The Discount Store lasted only two years.** — Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
Similar to (C), this has a good overall feel because the author is rebutting someone's claim on the basis of the shallow sample of their own personal experience.

But just like (C), the personal experience isn't a clear counterexample of the general statement.

The general statement was "most good boots last more than 5 years". A correct-sounding counterexample would be, "I had this pair of good boots, but it lasted less than 5 years".

However, in this argument, the author never establishes that we're talking about a pair of good boots, so it's not clear whether it's a counterexample.

(The writers seemed to even throw us the idea of "These boots I got from the Discount Store" to suggest that they're probably not high quality boots).

**E. A study of local rainfall shows that we have received at least 20 centimeters (8 inches) of rain per year for the past 50 years. Thus, we will probably get at least 20 centimeters of rain this year, too.** — Bad Conclusion / Evidence Match (0% of students)

## S2 · Q11 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

A part of the brain called the amygdala is involved in sensing fear. People who have Urbach-Wiethe disease, which destroys the amygdala, generally do not experience fear. They do, however, experience extreme fear when given a breath that is very high in carbon dioxide, just as some people with normal amygdalae do.

### Prompt

Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Information:*

causal (involved in)
The amygdala has some influence on how / whether we sense fear.

causal (destroys)
U-W disease destroys the amygdala.

pivot (however)
People with U-W disease generally don't experience fear, but they experience extreme fear if they're given a breath that's high in CO2 (low in oxygen), just as some people with normal amygdala do.

*Evaluate:* Usually when we see pivot wording in Most Supported, it creates some tension between statements. The way we resolve that tension is frequently connected to the correct answer.

Here, how can we synthesize the idea of people who have U-W disease, who thereby don't have a working amygdala (which is involved in sensing fear), who still experience fear?

Is there something special about the fear of suffocating (a breath that is very high in carbon dioxide) that doesn't involve the amygdala?

**A. Extreme fear is not the only intense response that people can have to a breath that is high in carbon dioxide.** — Unsupported: other responses (1% of students)
The only response we have been told people have to a breath high in CO2 is extreme fear. It's certainly very plausible that people could have other responses (this is very mild wording), but we have no support for any other responses.

**B. Sensing fear is not the only function the amygdala can serve.** — Unsupported: other functions (2% of students)
The only function we have been told the amygdala has is related to sensing fear. It's certainly very plausible that the amygdala could have other functions (this is very mild wording), but we have no support for any other function.

**C. Urbach-Wiethe disease is not the only disease that can destroy the amygdala.** — Unsupported: other diseases (0% of students)
The only disease we have been told that destroys the amygdala is U-W disease.

It's certainly possible that other diseases can also have that effect, but we have no information about any other diseases, so we have no support for this answer.

**D. The amygdala is not the only part of the brain that can be affected by Urbach-Wiethe disease.** — Unsupported: other parts of brain (1% of students)
The only part of the brain we were told is affected by U-W disease is the amygdala.

It's possible that this disease affects other parts of the brain, but we have no support for that idea.

**E. The amygdala is not the only part of the brain that can be involved in sensing fear.** — Correct (95% of students)
This is thinly supported, but supported, and since we had no support for the other answers, thinly supported = most supported.

Since we're being told about people who don't have amygdalae (or functionally speaking don't, since it's been destroyed) are still capable of sensing fear.

## S2 · Q12 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Bauer: It is a mistake to criticize the city for being overzealous in its issuance of parking tickets. Can you imagine how much worse parking would be if parking regulations were not enforced?

### Prompt

The reasoning in Bauer's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* It is a mistake to criticize the city for being overzealous in its issuance of parking tickets.
translation:
The city's not being overzealous (or if they are, we shouldn't criticize them for it).

*Evidence:* Can you imagine how much worse parking would be if parking regulations were not enforced?

*Evaluate:* This is a Straw Man argument, which is a term people use in real life to describe it, but LSAT has never used that wording to describe it. The idea of a Straw Man rebuttal is that you're unfairly representing the position being debated.

If I say, "I think we should extend unemployment benefits another six months, while we recover from the pandemic" and you say, "Well I don't think it's the government's job to make people rich for doing nothing!" ...

... you're not arguing with my position; you're fighting a Straw Man position you designed, because it makes for an easy opponent (a piñata you can knock down).

Extending unemployment benefits (which means paying a jobless person around $1200 / month, which is well below the poverty line) is not making people rich for doing nothing. That's not an intellectually respectful rebuttal to the original position.

In this parking ticket argument, the original position in the critics':
the city is being overzealous in its issuance of parking tickets.

Think about what that means. Give yourself a mental picture, a couple examples. What does it mean for a meter maid to be issuing tickets overzealously?

Maybe the meter runs out for some car at 5:58pm, but the rules for that spot are that you only need to pay until 6pm, after which it's free. Rather than letting it slide, the overzealous meter maid gives you a ticket for those 2 unpaid minutes.

Maybe your car is parked a little too far up, in terms of the parking spot lines or in terms of the red part of the curb where you can't park. Rather than tolerate your front bumper being six inches over the red line, they overzealously give you a ticket for illegal parking.

The author's method of arguing is saying, "You shouldn't criticize the status quo, because the alternative is worse!" You shouldn't criticize this obnoxiously overzealous ticketing by the meter maids, because the alternative, a wold in which no parking regulations are enforced, would be worse.

That's like a parent handing their kid a bowl of lentil soup, and the kid saying, "Ouch, that's too hot", and the parent responding, "You shouldn't criticize. Can you imagine how much worse it would be if it were super cold, with ice cubes in it?"

The kid would properly respond, "Well, that seems like a False Choice. Why does it have to be scalding hot or unappetizingly cold? Isn't there a middle ground?"

*Goal:* Let's look for an answer making a similar complaint:

Hey, author, isn't there a middle ground between overzealous ticketing and no ticketing at all? You can't say the former is justified because the latter is worse, unless those are our only two options.

**Answer choices**
**A. misrepresents a criticism about the consequences of a practice as a criticism about the intrinsic value of the practice** — Out of Scope: consequences (22% of students)
This one is very tempting. That first verb is perfect. Yes, yes, he's misrepresenting the original complaint - it wasn't about whether having parking regulations enforced had any intrinsic value (whether we should have them at all), it was about whether parking regulations are being enforced in a bad way (overzealously).

Although we've all tried, it's pretty icky to stretch the wording of this answer choice so that "overzealous enforcement of parking regulations" is a "consequence of enforcing parking regulations".

The fact that the practice of enforcing parking regulations is currently being carried out overzealously isn't a consequence of that practice. And this answer even says "the consequences", plural, so that's also icky.

A logical consequence is a must be true implication of an idea. When we write conditionals, such as A → B, we say that B is a consequence of A. The practice of enforcing parking tickets does not logically guarantee overzealous enforcement. So we can't say overzealousness is a logical consequence.

A causal consequence is a result that follows from this action transpiring. A causal consequence of enforcing parking tickets would be things like
- the city gets more revenue
- the driver of that car gets furious and the rest of their day is crappy

Overzealous parking enforcement isn't a consequence of the practice of enforcing parking. It is just one style of conducting that practice.

**B. takes for granted that a certain authority should be respected merely because it is an authority** — Bad Premise Match (1% of students)
If a flaw answer takes the form
takes for granted that X merely because Y
we'll ask ourselves whether X matches the conclusion and Y matches the support (because).

Did the author conclude that a certain authority should be respected?

Eh. We could make it work. She concluded that it's wrong to criticize meter maids for being overzealous, so that's in the neighborhood of "respect their authority".

Does the premise say something like, "after all, all authorities should be respected"? No, not at all. So we definitely can't match up the merely because it is an authority part.

**C. takes for granted that a particular practice is good simply because it is the way things have traditionally been done** — Bad Premise Match (2% of students)
If a flaw answer takes the form
takes for granted that X simply because Y
we'll ask ourselves whether X matches the conclusion and Y matches the support (because).

Did the author conclude that "a particular practice is good"? No, she concluded that it's wrong to criticize meter maids for being overzealous. That's not close enough to keep reading.

As it turns out, the premise part is miles away. The evidence never mentions "how things have been traditionally done".

**D. confuses the cause of a certain phenomenon for an effect of that phenomenon** — Wrong Flaw: not Causal (7% of students)
This describes one response to the famous Causal flaw. This argument had no causal interpretations happening. The author wasn't trying to explain what was causing overzealous ticketing. She was just defending its existence.

Note: this formulation of the Causal flaw answer is always wrong, because the answer is sure of itself. We can never definitively say that the author confused cause for effect. We can only point out the possibility that she failed to consider that possibility.

**E. defends the current situation merely by suggesting its superiority to an implausible alternative** — Correct (69% of students)
If a flaw answer takes the form
does X merely by doing Y
we'll ask ourselves whether X matches the conclusion and Y matches the support (by).

Was the author's conclusion defending the current situation?

Yes. it was saying, "Back off these meter maids, y'all. Stop criticizing them for overzealous ticketing."

Was the author's evidence saying that current situation is superior to some alternative scenario?

Yes, the author is arguing that things would be worse (inferior) if parking regulations were not enforced at all.

Is that alternative scenario implausible?

Yes! That's a ridiculous Straw Man position. Who is saying 'Defund the Parking Police'? We're just saying, "please stop being a jerk about giving tickets when we're just a few inches over the red line or when there's like a 2 minute unpaid gap between when the meter runs out and when the spot becomes a free spot."

Why would any city go to the trouble of creating parking regulations, posting signs, installing meters, etc., and then do zero enforcement? No one is suggesting that.

### Stimulus

Advertisers typically prohibit graphic designers from signing their works because the purpose of a graphic design is to draw attention to an advertised product or service, not to the designer. Nonetheless, it is better that graphic designers not remain anonymous. Anonymity undermines effective graphic design by making it difficult to hold designers accountable for their work.

### Prompt

Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion drawn in the argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* It is better that graphic designers not remain anonymous.

*Evidence:* Anonymity undermines effective graphic design by making it difficult to hold designers accountable for their work.

*Evaluate:* When we're doing Main Conclusion and Role, we prime our brain with one big tendency before we start reading the paragraph: the vast majority of the time, the Conclusion will come before the Evidence.

On Main Conclusion, this manifests in two big patterns:
- the very first claim of the paragraph is the Main Conclusion
or
- the author does a little warm-up with Background or Counterpoint ideas, and then pivots into her Conclusion, usually with a "but, yet, however".

This argument followed that second pattern, introducing a Background / Counterpoint idea (graphic designers aren't allowed to sign their works).

Then it pivots with a "nonetheless" into the Conclusion. Then it supports that Conclusion with Evidence.

*Goal:* On Main Conclusion, if we found the conclusion, we already did the hard part. Picking a correct answer means that we're just making ourselves pick whichever answer choice is closer in meaning to the claim we identified (and possibly highlighted) as the Main Conclusion.

So we want something that says, "It is better that graphic designers not remain anonymous".

Consider what an equivalent rephrasing might sound like, to make your listening ears more nimble: "it would be cool if graphic designers did sign their names" / "having graphic designers remain anonymous is overall not great".

**Answer choices**
**A. Prohibitions against graphic designers' signing their works are common in advertising.** — Background / Counterpoint (0% of students)
This is the practice that the author is arguing against. Her conclusion is that "it would be better if there were NOT prohibitions against graphic designers' signing their works"

**B. In advertising, the purpose of a graphic design is to focus attention on an advertised product or service rather than on the designer.** — Premise for the Counterpoint (1% of students)
This is the reason the advertisers would offer for the practice of forbidding graphic designers from signing their name.

Our author is concluding it would be better if we didn't have that practice, based on her premise that putting their name on their work would better hold graphic designers accountable.

**C. It is not desirable that graphic designers remain anonymous.** — Correct (84% of students)
This means basically the same thing as "it is better that graphic designers not remain anonymous".

**D. Graphic design is made less effective by anonymity on the part of graphic designers.** — Out of Scope: less effective (7% of students)
This answer is probably tempting, but it's coming from a place of trying to make this question more complicated than it is.

Does "it is better that GD's not remain anonymous" mean the same thing as "anonymity makes GD's less effective"?

No. The first claim is unspecific about why it would be better that GD's not remain anonymous. The second one giving a specific reason why it would be better.

So this answer is a poor match for the conclusion. Additionally, the author's specific reason (revealed in her premise in the final sentence) is not that it makes GD's more effective, but that it makes them more accountable.

**E. Holding graphic designers accountable for their work is difficult when those designers are anonymous.** — Assumption, Not Stated (8% of students)
This claim was never said, so it can't possibly be a good match for the author's explicit conclusion.

This answer just feels "true" because it's a necessary assumption linking the author's premise to her conclusion. Picking this answer is again overcomplicating our actual task: just find the answer choice that matches the claim you picked as the conclusion.

If you get down to 2 or 3 choices, you should be asking yourself, "Which one better matches the claim I highlighted as the conclusion?"

You can also ask yourself "Why should I believe this?" to test whether the answer choice you're considering was supported in the paragraph.

Why should we believe that ... holding GD's accountable is hard if they're anonymous ?

There's no reason provided. Meanwhile, for correct answer (C),

Why should we believe that ... it is not desirable that GD's remain anonymous?

### Stimulus

Superintendent: Within the school district overall, 11 percent of high school students drop out. However, of the high school students who participate in work internships, only 1 percent drop out. Clearly, then, participation in a work internship decreases the chance that a student will drop out.

### Prompt

The reasoning in the superintendent's argument is flawed because the argument

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion (Clearly, then):* Participation in a work internship decreases the chance that a student will drop out.

*Evidence:* Of the high school students who participate in work internships, only 1% drop out (compared to 11% of students overall).

*Evaluate:* The author is looking at a curious fact (an asymmetric comparison) and then making a causal conclusion (X decreases the chance of Y).

Curious Fact ... how come the dropout rate for students in internships is way lower than it is for students overall?

Author's Causal Explanation ... it must be that participating in the internship causes students to be less likely to drop out.

On Flaw (and Weaken and Evaluate and Necessary Assumption), we primarily react to these Explain Curious Fact arguments by thinking, "could there be some Alternate Explanation for the curious fact?"

With correlations, we're programmed like robots to think about potential alternate explanations like Reverse Cause / 3rd Factor:

- Reverse Cause: is it possible that not-dropping out is causing students to have the internship?

- 3rd Factor: could there be some underlying factor (like good grades) that is causing students to stay in school and causing them to be a part of an internship?

*Goal:* With Flaw, we're mainly trying to call out the fact that the author is overconfidently selling us on one possible causal explanation for a curious fact, without ruling out other possible explanations.

So look for an answer to object to the move from a correlation between internships and dropout rates to an assumption that the two are causally related.

Or look for an answer that discusses an alternate explanation for this association between internships and lower dropout rates.

**Answer choices**
**A. uses evidence that is in principle impossible to disprove** — Too Strong: impossible to disprove (0% of students)
The author's evidence tells us about the dropout rates for high school students overall compared to the dropout rate for high school students who participate in work internships.

The dropout rate is not something that is "in principle impossible to disprove". The only evidence that really qualifies for that label would be private, internal thoughts, or conversations with someone who is now dead or unable to verify their supposed remarks.

**B. uses the key term "student" in one sense in a premise and in another sense in the conclusion** — Not Equivocation (1% of students)
This answer refers to one of the 10 Famous Flaws, Equivocation, in which the author uses the same term or concept two different times to mean two very different things.

This answer is almost always wrong.

In both cases that the author uses "students", she is consistently referring to people in school.

**C. generalizes from a single instance of a certain kind to all instances of that kind** — Not Sampling (6% of students)
This answer refers to one of the 10 Famous Flaws, Sampling, in which the author relies on a sample that we are given reason to think is too small, self-selecting, or unrepresentative for some reason.

This author relies on statistical data, not a sample. And she's not going from any evidence that cites a single instance of a certain kind.

This answer describes an argument that sounds like, "The cheerleader I met loves tofu. Thus, all cheerleaders love tofu."

**D. infers a specific causal relationship from a correlation that might well have arisen from another cause** — Correct (93% of students)
This answer refers to one of the 10 Famous Flaws, Causal, in which the author presents a curious fact such as a correlation and then overconfidently assumes one possible causal explanation for that correlation.

Any time a Flaw answer is structured like
infers X from Y
we would want X to match the conclusion and Y to match the evidence.

Was the conclusion talking about "a specific causal relationship"?

Yes, "Participating in work internships decreases the chance that a student drops out".

Was the evidence a correlation?

Yes, the author presented evidence showing that "students who do internships are less likely than those who don't to drop out".

Could this correlation be explained some other way, such as that both traits are really being caused by some underlying condition?

Sure, maybe family wealth is the secret underlying cause. Because the family is wealthy, they can afford to help their children succeed in school (either by having time to help them or hiring tutors if needed). And the family wealth also means that the student doesn't feel pressured to go out and get a paid job. They can do an unpaid internship, since their family isn't counting on them to bring in some more income.

We don't have to actually think of this 3rd factor, such as family wealth, to pick this answer. It's just an example of what they're alluding to when they say the correlation may have another cause.

**E. contains a premise that presupposes the truth of the conclusion** — Not Circular (1% of students)
This answer refers to one of the 10 Famous Flaws, Circular Reasoning, in which the author's evidence restates the conclusion or requires the conclusion to be true.

This answer is almost always wrong.

Neither premise requires that the conclusion is true.

## S2 · Q15 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Journalist: Despite the recent spate of depressing political news, many investors are putting money into stocks. Clearly, these investors are confident of increased growth in the country's economy. Thus, since voter confidence in the economy tends to favor incumbent political leaders, it is likely that the party now in power will retain power after the upcoming elections.

### Prompt

Which one of the following is an assumption required by the journalist's reasoning?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion (Thus):* It's likely that the party now in power will retain power after the upcoming elections.

*Evidence (since):* Voter confidence in the economy tends to favor incumbent political leaders.

*Intermediate Conclusion (Clearly):* Investors are confident the economy will see increased growth.

*Evidence:* Many investors are putting money into stocks.

*Evaluate:* There's a real keyword gumbo going on here: clearly, thus, since. These indicate multiple conclusions, so we theoretically have different reasoning moves to analyze, if we're looking for assumptions.

The logic of all the indicator words works like this:

```
Despite
[counterpoint], opposing idea
claim 1. prem supporting 2
Clearly, claim 2. conc, supported by 1.
Thus, conc, supported by 2.
since claim 3, prem, supporting 4.
claim 4. conc, supp by 2+4
```

I typed that and it looks like crazytalk even to me, but each of those indicator words tells you a story of who's supporting whom or opposed to whom. It's not a replacement for comprehension, but it's a way to understand things when you're not comprehending very well. (sort of like relying on conditional logic indicator words to deal with conditional logic).

Our author's two linking assumptions, therefore, would sound like this

```
If ... Then ...
investors are putting       investors are confident
money into stocks → in economic growth

If ... Then ...
investors are confident
in economic growth       The incumbents
and → will retain power
voter confidence tends
to favor incumbents
```

The first linking assumption was joining claim 1 to claim 2.
The second was joining claims 2 and 3 to claim 4.

More conversationally, this author is expecting the incumbents to win the upcoming election, because when voters are confident in the economy, incumbents usually win.

Okay .... are voters confident in the economy?

The author would say "Sure, why just look at how confident investors are right now!"

Okay ... does investor confidence match voter confidence?

*Goal:* The correct answer could test the move from "putting money into stock market" to "confident in growth of economy", but it's more likely to address the switch the argument makes from talking about investor confidence to talking about voter confidence, while acting like we're still talking about the same thing.

**Answer choices**
**A. The economic policies pursued by the government are a major factor in producing stock market growth.** — Too Strong: major factor (4% of students)
The author could believe major factor, minor factor, no factor ... it wouldn't make any difference in her argument.

She only needs to believe that voters tend to vote for incumbents when the economy is doing well. That may be because voters are convinced that the incumbent government must be a major factor in why the economy is sizzling, but our author doesn't need to have that belief.

She's just predicting an election result, not evaluating it as a right / wrong.

**B. The investment choices of voters tend to reflect their political preferences.** — Out of Scope: investment choices (11% of students)
This argument doesn't need to assume that voters invest any money, let alone that they invest it in a way that reflects their politics.

The author has to assume that "if investors are confident in the economy, then so too are voters", but that doesn't require that voters are themselves investors.

Our author is thinking that the political preferences of voters will tend to reflect their confidence in the economy, not that their political affiliations will tell them where to invest their money.

**C. The economic attitudes of investors do not differ greatly from those of voters in general.** — Correct (77% of students)
In order for the author's claim 2 and claim 3 to join forces, we need to assume that "if investors are confident, then voters are confident".

This answer choice is addressing that gap.

If we hadn't anticipated this gap, we should still have slowed down and considered this answer when we saw its enticing ruling out "no / not" language (which is a hallmark of many correct Necessary Assumption answers).

If we negate this answer, does it weaken?

The economic attitudes of investors do differ greatly from those of voters in general.

Yes! The author only established that investors are confident in the economy. But her election prediction hinges on the idea that voters are confident in the economy and will thus reward the incumbent party by "renewing their lease".

**D. Voters generally attribute some responsibility for the state of the economy to the policies of previous governments.** — Opposite, if anything (8% of students)
The notion of previous governments is completely out of scope. We only care about who will likely win this upcoming election.

Since the author is predicting that voters will reward the incumbents with a victory based on the successful economy, she is probably thinking that voters credit the current government with the state of the economy.

**E. Voters are usually more loyal to parties than to individual politicians.** — Out of Scope Comparison (1% of students)
Nothing in this argument deals with the distinction between supporting a party and supporting an individual politician.

The author's logic doesn't care whether people are usually more loyal to A than B, more loyal to B than A, or loyal to both of them about equally. It has no logical bearing on her prediction about the upcoming election.

## S2 · Q16 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Archaeologist: The people who lived in this area deposited their rubbish in pits near their dwellings. Some claim that the rubbish found in those pits provides great insight into the possessions these people had, but this rubbish by itself actually tells us relatively little about those possessions; among other reasons, the pits have been subject to erosion over long periods of time, with destructive effects on the rubbish within.

### Prompt

Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the grounds presented by the archaeologist for drawing the conclusion in the argument above?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* The rubbish tells us very little about the possession of the people who lived nearby.

*Evidence:* The pits have been subject to erosion over long periods of time, with destructive effects on the rubbish within.

*Evaluate:* This question stem is asking us to strengthen the grounds on which the conclusion is based, so it's specifically asking us to improve the force / relevance of the evidence.

In order for us to figure out what the answer might sound like, we often benefit from thinking about how we'd object to the argument. If we adopt a Weaken mindset, we can get our brain to think of objections, and then those objections become ways to Strengthen the argument (if we rule out or go the opposite direction of those ideas).

given that erosion has had destructive effects on the rubbish within these pits,
how could someone still argue that the rubbish does tell us a lot about the possessions of the people who lived nearby?

I guess we'd just say, "sure it's been damaged, but you can still figure out what most of it is ... or you can learn a lot about their potential possessions based on the types of chemical materials you see in there".

*Goal:* Since want to strengthen the evidence, we would want to rule out these objections we just made.

Correct answers could sound like:
- oh, no you can't. You can't make out what any of this stuff is. Too damaged.
- nope, can't learn anything important by doing a chemical analysis of the eroded trash.
- this was the worst kind of erosion

**Answer choices**
**A. The pits contain certain tools not found in dwellings or at other above-ground locations.** — Weakens (5% of students)
This makes it sound like these trash pits actually do tell us some interesting things about the possessions of the people who lived nearby. We just discovered new tools they had!

**B. Scavengers routinely salvaged the most durable items from the rubbish pits.** — Correct (71% of students)
This adds to the force of the author's evidence:

That pile of rubbish? You're not gonna find anything in there. Erosion has had really destructive effects on it. Also, the stuff that was durable that might have still been worth finding has already been looted by scavengers.

This answer helps shoot down a potential objection: "couldn't there be some stuff in the trash pile that erosion hasn't ruined?"

According to this answer, "Nah ... the durable stuff got stolen from the pits already. Scavengers be scavenging, amirite?"

If we were bothered that this felt like a new premise rather than an augmentation of the current evidence, we might notice that the author did say "among other reasons, the pits have been subject to erosion".

**C. The soil surrounding the rubbish pits was sometimes removed for the manufacture of bricks.** — Weak / Unclear Impact (8% of students)
Words with minimal force like "some, sometimes, can, may, might, not all" are almost always wrong on Strengthen, Weaken, and Paradox.

This answer makes it sound like the soil around the pits might tell us that the people made bricks? Or is it saying that more modern humans have used this soil for bricks? We can't tell. Even if it's talking about the original inhabitants of the dwellings, this answer has nothing to do with whether or not the trash in the pit is still in good enough shape to teach us anything about the people hwo lived there.

**D. The pits in which the rubbish was deposited had earlier been used by this group of people as burial sites.** — Weakens, if Anything (5% of students)
Mainly this answer just makes me think less of these people — you converted your graveyard into your landfill? Someone at that village council meeting was like, "Well ... a graveyard is kinda like a trash can for humans!"

This answer has no clear impact or if anything it suggests that going through the rubbish in these pits we might also stumble across some remains of the people who lived there. Since people are often buried with some of their possessions, this would increase the likelihood of learning something by going through these pits.

**E. Certain types of items were never discarded by members of this group of people.** — Weaker Than Correct Answer (12% of students)
This answer seems to somewhat strengthen. Learning that certain possessions would never be found in the trash can help the author say "this trash will tell us little about their possessions".

But the weakness of "certain types of items" leaves room for there to still be plenty of stuff that did go in the trash that we could learn from.

### Stimulus

Anthropologists have long debated whether the customs of a culture invariably function to preserve it. According to one proposed definition, a culture is the totality of the customs practiced by those whose culture it is. If this definition is correct, then the customs of a culture necessarily function to preserve it, even if only in the most trivial sense, because, under this conception of a culture, the abandonment of any custom by a culture's members would by definition constitute that culture's destruction.

### Prompt

Which one of the following most accurately describes the role played in the argument by the statement that under a certain conception of culture, the abandonment of any custom by a culture's members would constitute that culture's destruction?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* If it's correct to define culture as the totality of customs practiced by those whose culture it is, then the customs of a culture necessarily function to preserve it, even if just in a trivial sense.

*Evidence (because):* Under this definition of culture, if a culture were to abandon any custom, then by definition it would destroy that culture.

*Evaluate:* This author is making a weird, nerdy argument that will make nerdy logicians snap their fingers in nerdy approval.

Anthropologists are debating whether the customs in a culture help to preserve that culture. (i.e. does celebrating Thanksgiving help to preserve American culture?)

The author is not engaging with the substantive truth of whether customs are a way of reinforcing and solidifying cultural identity. Instead, the author is interested in just making an observation about what is technically true, in a vacuous sense.

Suppose you define a culture as "the unique set of customs practiced by that culture".

Culture X does customs A, B, and C.
Culture Y does customs A, B, and D.

Even though they have customs in common, the totality of ABC is different from the totality of ABD, so according to this definition they are each different cultures.

The author's cute observation is that if Culture X were to drop custom C, then they would only do customs A and B. Thus, they would no longer be Culture X, since that culture was defined as the ABC set of customs.

A culture with an AB set of customs is different from a culture with an ABC set of customs. Thus, getting rid of custom C made the group that used to be Culture X no longer be Culture X.

Abandoning that custom destroyed their status as Culture X, so, technically, having custom C preserves Culture X.

*Goal:* We're being asked about the 2nd sentence. This provides a definition of "culture" as "unique set of customs" so that the author can make her cute mathematical argument.

We could maybe call it a premise, because the author is definitely using it in tandem with the final claim in order to derive the conclusion. But it feels like we could also call it a background claim that the author just scoops up and says, "If this happened to be true, then inference X would be true because premise Y."

The answer will probably sound relational (not as simple as premise / conclusion / background / opposing) and convey the idea that the author is just saying, "by THIS definition, culture definitely preserves culture in a technical, mathematical sense".

**Answer choices**
**A. It is the main conclusion drawn in the argument.** — Wrong Ingredient (5% of students)
The main conclusion is the first half of the last sentence, which we can tell because it's supported with the Support Indicator "because" (FABS = for, after all, since, because).

**B. It is a definition of a technical term used in the argument.** — Not a Technical Term (2% of students)
It is a definition, but is "culture" a technical term? It seems like a basic, familiar noun.

Technical terms are like "glissando, depth of field, covariation"; they are terms that people outside a certain field would not necessarily know.

**C. It is a claim that is used to support a conditional conclusion drawn in the argument.** — Correct (79% of students)
Is the conclusion conditional?

Sure, it says,
If this definition → customs preserve culture
is correct       at least in a technical sense

Does the definition in the 2nd sentence used to support this conclusion? Yes, in the sense that the premise (the final claim) only makes sense when that definition is applied to it.

If we weren't using that definition as our operative definition of "culture", then we wouldn't accept that "abandoning any custom would by definition destroy that culture".

**D. It is a claim that is used to support the view that a culture should be understood as a totality of customs.** — Bad Conclusion Match (12% of students)
The author is not concluding or suggesting that we should understand culture as a totality of customs.

Whenever an author says "If X is true, then Y", the author is not in any way hinting that she thinks X is true or should be true.

Furthermore, in this case, the author is showing that if we used this definition of culture then we would get the absurd implication that any time a culture decides to drop or add a custom, that entire culture ceases to exist. (Who would think we should define culture in a way that would mean we're constantly losing and gaining cultures based on slight changes).

**E. It is a claim ascribed by the argument to some anthropologists but dismissed by the argument as trivial.** — Not Ascribed / Not Called Trivial (2% of students)
The second sentence does not make clear who has proposed this definition. The fact that anthropologists are discussed in the first sentence doesn't mean that they proposed this definition.

And the author isn't dismissing the definition as trivial. The author is saying, "If this definition were correct, then we could get this implication, which means that in some trivial way the idea that customs preserve culture would be true."

## S2 · Q19 — Necessary Assumption

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Doctor: The patient had been experiencing back and leg pain. A computerized scan suggested that there was pressure on a nerve exiting to the leg from a lower vertebra. Such pressure can cause nerve inflammation, which can in turn cause pain. I decided that if the nerve was inflamed, the best way to reduce the inflammation would be a cortisone injection to the nerve, and this, in fact, did result in significant pain relief. I conclude, therefore, that pressure on the nerve in question was causing this patient's back and leg pain.

### Prompt

Which one of the following is an assumption required by the doctor's argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion (conclude / therefore):* The pressure on the nerve in question was causing this patient's back/leg pain.

*Evidence:* A computer scan suggested there was pressure on a nerve going from the lower vertebra to the leg.

This type of pressure can cause inflammation, which can cause pain.

If the nerve was inflamed, cortisone would reduce the inflammation. We injected cortisone and then the patient experienced pain relief.

*Evaluate:* Since the doctor is trying to Solve the Causal Mystery of this patient's nerve/back pain, we could use our Curious Fact / Explanation mindset.

The doctor is trying to explain the curious back/leg pain and explains it via pressure on the nerve in question. So we would worry about Alternate Explanations for the back/leg pain and we would worry about the plausibility of the doctor's diagnosis.

The most important moment to notice is when the doctor introduces a conditional claim:

if the nerve was inflamed, cortisone would help.
Cortisone helped. Thus, the nerve was inflamed.

What's wrong with that mini-argument?

Well, that's an illegal reversal of course. Just because cortisone helped doesn't show that the doctor's diagnosis was correct.

Perhaps there are other possible causes for which cortisone would also help. We haven't ruled out any of those possibilities.

*Goal:* Look for an answer saying something necessary to the internal logic of the doctor's diagnosis, or look for an answer ruling out the possibility of an alternate diagnosis.

**Answer choices**
**A. Computerized scans are the most accurate way of diagnosing certain kinds of ailments.** — Too Strong: most accurate (3% of students)
The word "most" is wrong 99% of the time we see it in Necessary Assumption. The doctor is definitely assuming that computerize scans can provide accurate diagnoses.

There's no reason she needs to assume it's the #1 most accurate way. If it were the #2 most accurate way, her argument wouldn't be changed.

**B. The cortisone injection did not reduce pressure on the inflamed nerve in the patient's leg.** — Opposite, if anything (8% of students)
When we see a Necessary Assumption answer choice ruling out an idea with the word "not", we get enticed. We want to negate these and see if they turn into an objection.

Would this negation weaken?
the cortisone shot did reduce pressure on the inflamed nerve in the leg?

That doesn't sound like a weakener at all to me. The doctor is perfectly happy believing the cortisone shot reduced inflammation AND reduced pressure on the nerve (in fact, reducing inflammation is, I believe, meant to reduce pressure).

Since the negation might even strengthen, this answer can't be right.

**C. The pain relief did not occur merely through the patient's belief in the efficacy of the cortisone.** — Correct (69% of students)
When we see a Necessary Assumption answer choice ruling out an idea with the word "not", we get enticed. We want to negate these and see if they turn into an objection.

Would this negation weaken?
the pain relief did occur merely through the placebo effect

Sure, that weakens. That provides an alternate cause. It's not saying "in addition to the therapeutic value of the cortisone shot, the patient also got a placebo effect." It's saying, "The pain relief was merely from / purely from the patient's psychological state."

Who knows whether the root cause of the pain was ever a pressured nerve. The author's evidence for that included the fact that a cortisone shot provided relief (and a cortisone shot is a treatment for the supposed problem). But this negation is saying, "the cortisone shot did not provide relief. The placebo effect did", so that badly weakens the author's signature piece of evidence in favor of her diagnosis.

**D. Only cortisone injections can cause a reduction of an inflammation of a nerve like that in the patient's leg.** — Too Strong: only / Only Thing Mentioned ≠ Only Thing (10% of students)
The author doesn't need to assume such an extreme claim that "ONLY cortisone shots reduce inflammation". This is just doing an age ol' trick on Necessary Assumption, where they try to make students think that the only thing of a certain type that was mentioned is therefore the only thing of that type.

Like if an author says "French cheese is delicious", there will be a trap answer accusing him of thinking, "only French cheese is delicious".

**E. The best treatment for back and leg pain is usually a drug that relieves inflammation.** — Too Strong: best / usually (10% of students)
We have another massively overstated answer choice here. The author assumed that a drug (cortisone) that relieves inflammation would be a treatment for this dude's inflammation.

She doesn't need to assume that more than 50% of the time it's the #1 anti-inflammation option.

If it were only the #1 option 49% of the time, that's plenty good.
If it's the #2 option 100% of the time, that's plenty good.

acclaimed or popular

No A's are B = All A's are ~B
Few A's are B = Most A's are ~B
Not all A's are B = Some A's are ~B

That's why I wrote the two few statements in most language and why I wrote the no statement in all language.

O → HO       HO → LC

Yes, they both talk about highly original, so we would just get a chain saying
Oil → Highly Orig → Late Career
~Late Career → ~Highly Orig → ~Oil

Now we can look at the other quantified statements and see if any of them are using a trigger from these rules:

Most O are ~CA and ~P

Nope. This statement ends with ~CA and ~P, neither of which are triggers that allow us to extend that claim.

Most A are ~HO

Yes! This ends with ~HO, which is a trigger that leads to ~Oil.

```
Most A are ~HO
+ ~HO → ~O
---------------------
Most A are ~O
```

*Goal:* We would expect that the correct answer will either give us the idea that "Most of her abstract works were not oil paintings", or it will be something derivable from the conditional chain we assembled:

Oil → Highly Orig → Late Career
~Late Career → ~Highly Orig → ~Oil

By contrast, the inference we made, "Most abstract are not-oil" was possible because we did have a statement about "most abstract".

You'll never see a correct answer say "Most X are ____ " if we were never provided a statement about Most / Few X.

**B. Few of the works produced late in Albarran's career are abstract works.** — Unknown Group (18% of students)
We would immediately translate this into positive:
Most [late career] are [not abstract]

This is talking about "Most late career works". We don't have any statements about most-late career, so it's not going to be a correct answer.

By contrast, the inference we made, "Most abstract are not-oil" was possible because we did have a statement about "most abstract".

You'll never see a correct answer say "Most X are ____ " if we were never provided a statement about Most / Few X.

### Stimulus

### Written (single style)

*Surprising Claim:* But in that same scenario, the volume of the ice caps would increase.

*Evaluate / Goal:* Given That
Rising seawater from a warmer planet would melt the ice caps considerably,

How Can We Say That
The ice caps would grow in volume?

**Answer choices**
**A. As global temperatures increase, more seawater evaporates into the atmosphere, thereby leading to increased snowfall at the polar caps, which then melts and refreezes as ice.** — Correct (81% of students)
How could the volume of the ice caps increase?

According to this, more seawater evaporation would lead to more snow at the polar caps, and then that snow would melt and refreeze as ice.

So even though rising seawater is melting the ice caps, it's also sending more snow their way, and that snow adds onto the ice cap, increasing its volume.

**B. As global temperatures increase, more seawater evaporates into the atmosphere, thereby lowering the ocean level, which then results in less contact between the ice caps and the warmer water, thereby stabilizing the size of the ice caps.** — Too Weak (5% of students)
How could the volume of the ice caps increase?

According to this, the end result of the causal chain would be stabilizing the size of the ice caps. We're trying to explain how the ice caps would increase in size, not stabilize (i.e. stay the same).

How could the volume of the ice caps increase?

According to this, the end result of the causal chain would be that the water melts the ice even faster.

We're trying to explain how the ice caps would increase in size, and this is saying the opposite, that they would melt even faster.

According to this, the end result of the causal chain would be that the freezing point of seawater is even lower.

That means it needs to be colder for seawater to turn into ice caps. It would be harder for the ice caps to grow, if the world is warmer than before and the temperature needed to turn seawater into ice is lower than before.

How could the volume of the ice caps increase?

According to this, the end result of the causal chain would be less variation in temperature overall.

### Stimulus

One reason swimming immediately after eating is thought to be dangerous is that it could cause muscle cramps. But there is no reason to believe this. ### Prompt

Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion drawn in the argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Opposing Idea:* One reason swimming immediately after eating is thought to be dangerous is that it could cause muscle cramps.

*Conclusion:* There is no reason to believe this [swimming immediately after eating could cause muscle cramps].

*Evidence:* Muscle cramps are usually caused by muscle fatigue and dehydration, which are unrelated to eating.

Reduced blood flow to muscles during digestion might also be a cause [of muscle cramps], though this is disputed.

In any case, not enough blood goes to the stomach to aid in digestion after a meal to reduce blood flow to muscles.

*Evaluate / Goal:* This argument follows a common pattern. A claim or idea is stated in the first sentence. The next sentence pivots to a contrasting idea. That contrasting idea is the main point of the argument—the conclusion. The rest of the argument contains evidence supporting that conclusion.

The conclusion is in the second sentence of this argument. It claims that there is no reason to believe that swimming immediately after eating causes muscle cramps. We'll look for a correct answer that makes this same claim.

**Answer choices**
**A. Swimming immediately after eating is not dangerous.** — Bad Conclusion Match / Too Strong (9% of students)
This is tempting. However, when the conclusion states that there is "no reason to believe this," it means that there is no reason to believe that muscle cramps cause swimming immediately after eating to be dangerous. "Swimming after eating is not dangerous because of X" is not the same as saying "swimming after eating is not dangerous at all." It might still be dangerous for some reason other than X.

**B. Reduced blood flow to muscles is not a cause of muscle cramps.** — Premise / Too Strong (1% of students)
This answer resembles part of the evidence. It's not the conclusion.

Also, the evidence doesn't state that reduced blood flow to muscles is not a cause of cramps. "This is disputed" only means that there is disagreement about reduced blood flow during digestion causing muscle cramps. The wording of this answer choice is too strong.

**C. There is no reason to believe that swimming immediately after eating causes muscle cramps.** — Correct (88% of students)
We have a winner! To see that this is correct, we need to recognize that the second sentence in the stimulus contains the conclusion. We also need to understand that the word "this" in that second sentence refers back to swimming after eating being the cause of muscle cramps.

**D. Blood going to the stomach to aid in digestion after a meal is not a cause of muscle cramps.** — Premise (1% of students)
This information is part of the evidence. It's not the conclusion.

**E. Blood going to the stomach to aid in digestion after a meal would not reduce blood flow to the muscles.** — Premise / Last Claim Bait (1% of students)
This statement is given as evidence. It's not the conclusion. The final sentence or statement in an argument is often the conclusion, but not always.

**A. Most English people want important archaeological items to be housed in museums.** — Out of Scope: want / Too Strong: most (2% of students)
The word "most" is wrong 99% of the time we see it on Necessary Assumption (it's generally only correct if the word "most" appears in the conclusion).

After all, the correct answer on Necessary Assumption is an idea that would weaken the argument if we negated it.

But when you go from "Most English people want X" to "Not-Most English people want X", you're really just switching from "at least 51% want it" to "at most 49% want it". Not a big deal, so not a big objection.

The argument also has nothing to do with what English people want. This argument is only about what the author considers to be an adequate / inadequate level of access. Like all Missing Link / Idea correct answers, this answer choice contains wording that is all internal to the stimulus.

This is establishing that the author clearly seems to be thinking that this helmet was part of the English people's archaeological heritage, and that because the law failed to get the helmet into a museum, it failed provide adequate access to that heritage.

Totally! That would mean that the author's evidence is completely irrelevant to her conclusion. That's a huge weakener.

She's concluding something about the law, in relation to its ability to give access to English people's archaeological heritage. Her only evidence is about how the law handled this Roman helmet. If the helmet isn't part of the English heritage, then her evidence has nothing to do with her conclusion.

This answer is saying "more valuable", which is a Relative idea.

Switching from absolute to relative language (or vice versa) is almost never correct.

Additionally this uses the ever-toxic word "most", which is almost always wrong on Necessary Assumption.

Does it make any difference to the author's argument whether the helmet is more valuable than at least 51% of the items housed in English museum vs. whether it's only more valuable than 49% of the items?

Of course not. Since negating this answer would have no effect on the argument, it can't be right.

At first this looks enticing, because the word "not" is famously part of correct answers on Necessary Assumption (Defender answers).

Would it become a big objection to this argument if we said, "The private collector did pay more for the helmet than an English museum would have"?

Not at all. The author is saying the law failed to get this helmet into a museum. It's not talking about specific payment amounts at all.

**E. No English museum could have paid more for the Roman helmet than the private collector did.** — Out of Scope Comparison: pay more / Too Strong: no (2% of students)
As we mentioned with (D), the concept of how much the private collector or a museum paid is totally irrelevant to this argument.

If we negate this and say, "There is at least one English museum that could have paid more for the helmet than the collector did", that wouldn't hurt the argument at all.

**Correct answer:** D

### Stimulus

Late blight is a disease affecting tomato plants that causes blue-grey patches to appear on the leaves. Applying a copper solution to the blighted plants is an effective treatment for the disease. Therefore, late blight must be caused by a deficiency of copper in the plant's mineral intake.

### Prompt

The flawed nature of the argument can be most effectively demonstrated by noting that, by parallel reasoning, we could argue that

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Evidence:* Applying a copper solution to blighted plants is an effective treatment for the disease [late blight].

*Conclusion (Therefore):* Late blight must be caused by a deficiency of copper in the plant's mineral intake.

*Evaluate:* LR questions often contain arguments that contain evidence about a correlation—two things that tend to happen together. Based on just that correlation, the argument claims that one of the things causes another. This is called a Causal Flaw, which is often explained by saying "a correlation does not prove causation."

This argument's conclusion claims that late blight is caused by a copper deficiency. But the evidence is a bit stronger than a mere correlation. The copper solution is described as an "effective treatment." This indicates that the copper solution causes the plants to be cured of the disease.

So, what's the exact flaw here? The conclusion implies that tomato plants need copper. The assumption is that the copper solution provides the plants with this copper that they need. But none of that is stated in the evidence. We're never told what the copper solution actually does, only that it works. It certainly might cure a copper deficiency, but there are other, equally plausible alternative explanations.

For example, late blight could be caused by some type of organism that is killed by the copper solution. If this is true, the conclusion doesn't really make sense. It would be the same as saying, "Antibiotic cream is an effective treatment for an infected wound. Therefore, infected wounds are caused by a deficiency of antibiotic cream in a person's diet."

*Goal:* Look for an answer with evidence that describes some type of causal connection without explaining exactly how that connection works. The conclusion will be based on an assumption that there is only one explanation, when other reasonable possibilities exist. Watch out for answers containing arguments that aren't flawed, or ones that contain a completely different flaw.

**Answer choices**
**A. since radiation therapy can be an effective treatment for some types of cancer, it follows that higher doses of radiation will be more effective in treating those same types of cancer** — Bad Conclusion Match (0% of students)
The conclusion of the original argument doesn't claim that higher doses of copper solution would be more effective in treating late blight. The conclusion in this answer choice doesn't involve the same flaw of favoring one explanation when others are just as plausible.

**B. since scurvy is caused by a deficiency of vitamin C, it follows that vitamin C is an effective treatment for scurvy** — Bad Evidence Match / Bad Conclusion Match (3% of students)
This wouldn't be a terrible answer, except the premise and conclusion are completely reversed. It would be similar to the original argument if it read, "since vitamin C is an effective treatment for scurvy, it follows that scurvy is caused by a deficiency of vitamin C."

**C. since malnutrition can cause health problems, it follows that the more food you eat, the healthier you will be** — Bad Conclusion Match (1% of students)
This is similar to answer choice A. The original argument does not claim that, because something is effective, even more of that thing is more effective.

**D. since paracetamol is effective in treating headaches, it follows that headaches are caused by a lack of paracetamol in the bloodstream** — Correct (93% of students)
This describes the same type of situation as the original argument. If we replace "paracetamol" with "copper," replace "headaches" with "late blight," and replace "bloodstream" with "mineral intake," we basically have the same argument with the same flaw.

**E. since calcium deficiency in apple trees causes brown spots on the apples, it follows that apple trees growing in soils that are rich in calcium will not produce apples with brown spots** — Bad Evidence Match / Bad Conclusion Match (2% of students)
This is similar to answer choice B in that this reverses the premise and conclusion. The conclusion of the original argument claims that X causes Y, while that is stated as the evidence in this answer choice.

Which one of the following conforms most closely to the principle illustrated in Martin's position?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Even if doctors' advising their patients to change diet/exercise rarely results in weight loss, that's no reason for doctors to abandon giving that advice.

*Evidence:* Changes in diet/exercise can have other beneficial effects.

**A. The fact that only one person can win a marathon is no reason for Jen to avoid participating in the marathon, since every participant still has a chance of winning.** — Bad Conclusion/Premise Match (2% of students)

Just because X doesn't do A doesn't mean we shouldn't suggest X.

The fact that [engineer's work] could not solve the problem is no reason to deem their work bad work, since their work nonetheless had other beneficial effects.

It's definitely not identical structurally, but they both play off the same principle of, "let's not be sour on X. Even though it didn't do A, it managed to some other good thing!"

That would be like if the original argument had said, "If doctors keep giving this advice, they'll generally help someone lose weight eventually."

The original was saying, "Just because an action [saying "change your diet / exercise"] doesn't result in the intended outcome [weight loss], that doesn't mean the action was bad, because the action has some other beneficial effect."

## S4 · Q10 — Flaw

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Columnist: The dangers of mountain climbing have been greatly exaggerated by the popular media. In the 80 years from 1922 to 2002, there were fewer than 200 climbing fatalities on Mount Everest, one of the most dangerous mountains in the world. Contrast that with the more than 7,000 traffic fatalities in France alone in 2002.

### Prompt

The reasoning in the columnist's argument is flawed because it fails to consider

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Mountain climbing isn't as dangerous as the popular media likes to claim.

*Evidence:* From 1922 - 2002, there were less than 200 climbing fatalities on Mount Everest, which is one of the most dangerous mountains in the world. In 2002, alone, there were 7,000 traffic fatalities in France.

*Evaluate:* Given this crazy asymmetry between deaths on Everest vs. those on the roadways of France, how could we still argue that mountain climbing is as dangerous as the popular media has said (which we'll assume is "quite dangerous"?)

One move we have is saying, "Cool, well the roadways in France are crazy dangerous, so no wonder there are so many deaths. Just because mountain climbing isn't as dangerous, that doesn't mean it's not dangerous!"

If you claimed that the Empire State Building in NYC was a really tall building, and someone said "No, it's not. There's a couple buildings in Dubai that are taller", that's not a good objection.

The popular media didn't say mountain climbing is the most dangerous thing ever, so pointing out that something else is potentially more dangerous is a Relative vs. Absolute type flaw.

But we should also think about how unfair the comparison is. If I tried to argue that "skydiving is more dangerous than flying in an airplane", and you said, "Nuh-uh — more people die in airplanes each year", it's not a fair comparison.

We don't measure danger in terms of the raw number of injuries / fatalities. We judge danger in terms of the likelihood of getting an injury / fatality.

If 13% of skydivers die each year, but only 1% of airplane riders die, then it seems like skydiving is more dangerous, regardless of the absolute number of deaths. There will be more deaths from airplane riders, since there are hundreds of millions of people who fly each year, whereas there might only be 1 million people who skydive each year.

*Goal:* We have two possible objections:
- "Just because one thing is even more dangerous doesn't prove the first thing isn't plenty dangerous itself"

- "What if the relative rate of deaths is much higher for Everest, and the absolute number is lower just because there are only a few hundred people who try to climb Everest each year?"

**Answer choices**
**A. whether the number of traffic fatalities in France was higher in 2002 than in other years** — Too Weak (1% of students)
Yes, it weakens somewhat if the author cherry-picked one of the higher fatality years in France. However, it barely changes the logical force of her argument. What if the average fatalities is more like 5000 a year?

It would be the same argument if she were saying "80 years = 200 Everest deaths", "1 year = 5000 French auto deaths".

**B. whether the number of traffic fatalities in France is usually higher than that in other countries** — Out of Scope: other countries (1% of students)
This is just like (A), in the sense that it somewhat looks like a Sampling issue if the author picked one of the deadliest years in France, or if the author picked one of the deadliest countries for driving.

But even if the average auto deaths for a country is 3,000, the logical force of the evidence is the same: "80 years, 200 deaths from climbing Everest" vs. "1 year, 3000 deaths from driving in country X"

**C. whether the number of fatalities among climbers on Mount Everest could be reduced by implementing stricter safety measures** — Out of Scope: lowering fatalities (0% of students)
Discussing what could be done to reduce fatalities is totally beside the point. We're assessing whether mountain climbing is as dangerous as the media says it is.

How safe it is in the future (if certain measures are implemented) is totally irrelevant.

**D. how many climbers were on Mount Everest during those 80 years and how many people traveled on French roads in 2002** — Correct (91% of students)
This is one of those correct answers that isn't helping you out with understanding the objection, if you didn't already think it.

Since there are about 100-200 people who climb Everest each year, you're talking about around 10,000 people who have tried to climb it in those 80 years.

There are way more than 10,000 people who drive in France each year.

If you're offended, on LSAT's behalf, that I'm pulling in outside knowledge there, yeah, this test sometimes rewards common sense about the world.

However, even if these were made up names, it would be a fair correct answer to say "The absolute numbers of deaths are irrelevant to assessing danger. We need the relative rates."

They've only given us the numerator of those fractions (Total Deaths / Total Participants). This answer choice is simply saying we need the denominators in order to calculate what % of people climbing Everest vs. driving in France have died.

**E. how many climbing fatalities there were during those 80 years on mountains other than Mount Everest** — No Impact (7% of students)
This is like (A) and (B). They're all trying to point out that there is some Sampling distortion.
2002 was a deadlier than average year!
France is a deadlier than average country to drive in!
Maybe Mount Everest isn't a representative mountain.

Not only are these objections too small to matter, given how absurdly different the statistics were for Everest vs. France, but the author was actually generous to his opponents by picking one of the most dangerous mountains.

If you were trying to hide the danger of mountain climbing, then you would use a safer mountain as a comparison. He used the most dangerous one to make the point that even the most dangerous mountain is still not lookin' so dangerous, compared to driving in France.

2. there's a reason why there would be a plausible causal path from writing poetry to being melancholy (poetry --> profound and engrossing activity --> can be depressing --> melancholy).

**A. It is a premise offered as evidence for another premise, which in turn is offered in support of the argument's overall conclusion.** — Out of Scope: Intermediate Conclusion (7% of students)

1. most of the poets I know are melancholy
2. an engrossing activity like writing poetry can be depressing

This answer correctly identifies our claim as a Premise, but this answer choice, like (A), incorrectly accuses the argument of having an Intermediate Conclusion, because it's saying there is a Premise supported by another Premise. That would make the former Premise count as an Intermediate Conclusion.

This argument didn't have any Intermediate Conclusion. There are two separate reasons given to support the idea that "writing poetry makes many poets melancholy":
1. most of the poets I know are melancholy
2. an engrossing activity like writing poetry can be depressing

Neither 1 nor 2 support each other. They each just provide independent support for the main conclusion.

Why should we believe that, "an activity as profound and engrossing as writing poetry can be depressing"? Did the argument offer any evidence to support that?

No. Instead, it just prefaced that premise by saying "as everyone knows", which indicates it's just a commonly accepted axiom that the author doesn't feel any need to support.

**C. It is the overall conclusion of the argument.** — Wrong Role (1% of students)
The middle sentence, adorned with a "Thus" and a "one can conclude", is the main conclusion.

The final claim of the paragraph, on Role and Main Conclusion, will be the main conclusion only about 10-15% of the time. We go into those two question types specifically reminding ourselves not to expect the conclusion at the end (in 85-90% of cases, the conclusion on those two question types is found earlier than at least one of its supporting ideas).

By clarifying my first claim, I haven't given anyone any reason for believing it; I've simply gotten more specific about what my claim is.

1. most of the poets I know are melancholy
2. an engrossing activity like writing poetry can be depressing

So, yes, our claim is a premise that directly supports the main conclusion. (On other problems, they might call this sort of thing "a premise that provides partial support for the conclusion", and that term just means that there's more than one premise).

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

People's antagonism to development in their neighborhoods can be harmful to a city. For example, nightclubs tend to be unpopular with neighbors because of the late hours they keep. So if neighborhoods are allowed to block new nightclubs, a city will never get new nightclubs.

### Prompt

Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* People's antagonism to development in their neighborhoods can be harmful to a city.

*Intermediate Conclusion (So):* If neighborhoods are allowed to block new nightclubs, a city will never get new nightclubs.

*Evidence (for example):* Nightclubs tend to be unpopular with neighbors because of the late hours they keep.

*Evaluation:* There are two conclusions drawn, so we can analyze each of those two reasoning moves.

If we grant that nightclubs tend to be unpopular w/ neighbors,
do we have to accept that if neighborhoods are allowed to block new nightclubs, a city will never get new nightclubs?

No, never seems too harsh. It said that nightclubs tend to be unpopular, not that they're always unpopular. Maybe a certain nightclub would atypically be popular with neighbors (perhaps it's really good at keeping its volume down and it brings a lot of people to the area who boost the local economy).

If we grant that neighborhoods will block the development of new nightclubs
Do we have to accept that people's antagonism to development in their neighborhoods can be harmful to a city?

No, we don't have to accept that. Who says that rejecting all new nightclubs is harmful to the city? Do cities need new nightclubs?

*Goal:* Let's look for an answer addressing the move from "clubs tend to be unpopular" to "neighborhoods would never allow a new nightclub to be developed in their hood".

Or look for an answer addressing the move from "a city never gets new nightclubs" to "harmful to the city".

**Answer choices**
**A. New nightclubs would usually be approved if the decision whether to approve was made at the city level.** — Illegal Opposite / Too Strong: usually (5% of students)
This is doing the classic "flip the illegal lightswitch" move that's so common in Necessary Assumption trap answers.

If the paragraph says "black lives matter", the answer will say the author is assuming "non-black lives don't matter".

Here, the paragraph said "if decision is made by neighborhoods, no new nightclubs will be approved", so this answer is incorrectly saying that the author assumes "if decision is not made by neighborhoods, new nightclubs will be approved".

We can also pick at the quantifier "usually", which means "most of the time". The word/concept most is wrong on Necessary Assumption 99% of the time we see it.

Does the author really need to assume that cities would approve new nightclubs at least 51% of the time? Would it make any difference if it was only 49% of the time? Of course not.

**B. All neighborhoods in a city are equally opposed to getting new nightclubs.** — Too Strong: all / equally (13% of students)
There's no reason the author needs to commit to this insanely extreme claim that every single neighborhood is identically opposed to new nightclubs.

If there were slight variation in how strongly they were opposed, would that hurt the argument?

Of course not. As long as they're opposed, it doesn't matter how much they're opposed.

**C. It is a bad thing for a city if the city never gets new nightclubs** — Correct (70% of students)
This just connects the evidence, "People's antagonism towards nightclubs would prevent new nightclubs from being developed" to the conclusion "People's antagonism towards development can be harmful to a city".

The author is claiming that being anti-development leads to harm. The only anti-development example is preventing new nightclubs. So the author must be assuming that preventing new nightclubs can harm a city.

If we negated this answer and said, "Hey, author — if a city never gets any new nightclubs. that's not a bad thing", then she would no longer have any support for her conclusion that "anti-development can be harmful to a city."

**D. Restaurants that do not keep late hours are rarely unpopular with neighbors.** — Illegal Opposite (1% of students)
This is doing the classic "flip the illegal lightswitch" move that's so common in Necessary Assumption trap answers.

If the paragraph says "the boys on the chess team like to dance", a trap answer will say the author is assuming that "boys on other teams don't like to dance" or assuming that "the girls on the chess team don't like to dance".

Here, the paragraph said "nightclubs tend to be unpopular because of their late hours", so this answer is incorrectly saying that the author assumes "restaurants tend to be popular because of their non-late hours".

If we negated this and said that, "Restaurants that don't stay open super late are nonetheless usually unpopular with neighbors", that would hurt the argument. If anything, it might help the author's argument that people's antipathy towards development can be harmful to the city.

**E. New nightclubs invariably produce some benefits for the neighborhoods in which they open.** — Too Strong: invariably / City benefit vs. Neighborhood benefit (11% of students)
The conclusion is soft, saying that antagonism towards development can be harmful to a city. The author is assuming that if we never get new nightclubs, that would be harmful.

That's different from assuming that every single new nightclub provides some benefit.

If we negate this and say, "Hey, author — there is at least one new nightclub that wouldn't produce benefit for the neighborhood in which it opens", that doesn't hurt the argument.

The author could say, "Sure. In that instance, people's antagonism towards the development of that nightclub would be good for their neighborhood. But opposing all new nightclubs can still be harmful to the overall city."

The gist of this paragraph is that foreign protection isn't working (at its goal of enabling the firms to grow big enough to compete internationally).

We don't have any support for this idea that "we need protection from foreign competition or else most firms will go out of business".

We were given a conditional that said, "If it were possible for protection to enable this country's firms to take on foreign rivals, then 10 years of protection would have already accomplished that goal".

## S4 · Q19 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Store owner: My customers are not worried about crime in this neighborhood; every day I talk to people who shop at my store, and they tell me that they are not worried. So crime is not adversely affecting my business by reducing the number of people willing to shop at my store.

### Prompt

The reasoning in the store owner's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Crime is not adversely affecting by business by reducing the number of people willing to shop there.

*Evidence:* Every day I talk to people who shop at my store and they tell me they're not worried about crime.

*Evaluate:* This sounds like something we could rewrite for modern COVID times by saying, "Supposedly people are scared of COVID, but clearly this is untrue. I ask my customers every day whether they're afraid of COVID and they all say no."

We would object — "You're going to a church and asking people if they're religious? You're going to a baseball game and asking people if they like baseball? Of course the customers at your store aren't scared of COVID. The fact that they're at your store reveals that. The people who are scared of COVID aren't leaving their house, so you're not getting a chance to interview them."

One of the ten famous flaws is Sampling, when an author relies on a sample that may be too small, self-selecting, or unrepresentative.

The store owner here seems to be relying on a self-selecting sample.

*Goal:* Look for an answer pointing out the sampling bias of asking people who came to the store "are you too scared to come to the store", or for an answer pointing out the objection that "customers who are afraid of crime in the neighborhood will have stopped coming to your store".

**Answer choices**
**A. infers that something is not the case on the grounds that there is only a small amount of evidence for its being the case** — Bad Premise Match (4% of students)
Any time an answer is structured like
infers that X on the grounds that Y
X should be the conclusion and Y should be the evidence.

Did we conclude that "something is not the case"?

Yes, we concluded that "it is not the case that people's fear of crime has reduced the number of customers".

Was the evidence saying "there is only a small amount of evidence that people's fear of crime has reduced the number of customers"?

No, the evidence says "I interview customers every day, and they all serve as counterevidence to this phenomenon".

This answer choice is pretty close to describing another famous flaw, Unproven vs. Proven False.

**B. appeals to personal opinion to establish a factual claim** — Wrong Flaw (6% of students)
This refers to the famous Inappropriate Appeal (to Opinion) flaw.

Does the author appeal to personal opinion?
Yes, he appeals to the opinions of his customers "they are not worried".

Is he trying to establish a factual claim?
Yes, but it's about opinion. He's assessing whether people's opinion about crime is making them unwilling to shop at the store.

So we wouldn't blame him for citing opinion in the evidence, since those opinions are related to assessing the anti-causal conclusion.

We would blame him for not considering the opinions of the people who aren't any longer coming to the store.

**C. generalizes about the whole neighborhood based on the case of one store** — Bad Conclusion Match (10% of students)
Any time an answer is structured like
does X based on Y
X should match the Conclusion and Y should match the Evidence.

Does our Conclusion "generalize about the whole neighborhood"?

Nope. It's just talking about his store. We can stop considering this answer at this point.

**D. draws a conclusion on the basis of a biased sample** — Correct (73% of students)
The people who are still coming to the store, by definition, are willing to shop at the store, so they are a biased sample.

If the store owner wanted to assess whether fear of crime was keeping people from visiting his store, he would need to interview people who aren't at his store and find out why they aren't coming (is it the crime wave, or is it they just don't like store owners who commit Famous Flaws)?

This argument also would have a biased sample:
"People aren't excited about the Biden campaign. Every day I talk to other people at the call center where we volunteer for the Trump campaign, and they tell me that they are not exited about the Biden campaign's ideas."

**E. fails to consider that crime might affect the neighborhood negatively without affecting businesses negatively** — Not an Objection (8% of students)
Would it weaken this argument so say, "hey, author, even though crime isn't affecting businesses negatively, it might still be affecting the neighborhood negatively"?

No. That would only weaken a conclusion that sounded like, "Thus, we have no reason to worry about crime."

This conclusion was only about whether or not crime was affecting his store. Anything beyond that is inadmissible to the issue this argument is adjudicating.

## S4 · Q21 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Engineer: Air bags in automobiles occasionally cause injuries by accidentally inflating when no collision has occurred. Automobile manufacturers are attempting to fix the problem by inventing more elaborate computer control systems for air bags. But the more complex such a system is, the more ways there are in which it can fail. Thus, the new computer control systems will probably only make the problems with accidental air bag inflation even worse.

### Prompt

The engineer's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on which one of the following grounds?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* The new computer control systems will probably only make the problems with accidental air bag inflation worse.

*Evidence:* The new system will be more complex, and the more complex a system is, the more ways there are in which it can fail.

*Evaluate:* The author is essentially making a move from "there are more ways the system can fail" to "it is more likely that the system will fail".

Mathematically, that doesn't have to be true.

Say there are only two ways to fail, and each way of failing has about a 30% chance of occurring.

Avoiding failure would mean that both things go right, which is 70% probability times 70% probability
7/10 * 7/10 = 49/100 = 49% chance it doesn't fail.

No say there are four ways to fail, but each of them only has about a 10% chance of occurring.

Avoiding failure would mean that all four things go right, is 90% * 90% * 90% * 90%.
[checks calculator] = 65% chance it doesn't fail.

*Goal:* Find an answer saying that "just because there are more ways to fail doesn't mean that a system is more likely to fail", or one saying "fails to consider that the probability of any of these myriad ways the new system could fail is much lower than the probability was that the old system could fail".

**Answer choices**
**A. It fails to address adequately the possibility that the likelihood of a system's failing need not increase with the number of ways in which it can fail.** — Correct (59% of students)
Does it weaken the argument to say, "Hey, author, it's possible that the number of ways in which a system can fail increases, but the likelihood of the system failing does not increase"?

Yes! That's basically saying, "Hey, author, your conclusion need not follow from your evidence".

**B. It takes for granted that any failure in an air bag's computer control system will cause that air bag to inflate accidentally.** — Too Strong (21% of students)
The author doesn't need to assume that every single failure in a control system will cause the air bag to accidentally inflate. If there is at least one component in an air bag's computer control system that doesn't trigger the air bag to inflate when it fails, that wouldn't hurt the author's argument at all.

**C. It fails to address adequately the possibility that air bags may often accidentally inflate even when their computer control systems do not fail.** — Not an Objection (4% of students)
Does it weaken the author's argument to say, "Hey, author, even if there were no computer control systems at all, there would still sometimes be accidental inflations."

No, because that would only weaken someone who had promised that "if we do X, there will be no more accidental inflations".

Our author said, "if we do X (use this more complex system), we're increasing the risk of additional inflations".

**D. It overlooks the possibility that, even if the new computer control systems exacerbate the problems with accidental air bag inflation, they may have advantages that outweigh this disadvantage.** — Even If [Conclusion is True] (12% of students)
This form of answer choice is always wrong, since you can't hurt an argument by saying, "Even if your Conclusion is right, ______ ."

The whole point of an argument is only that the Conclusion is true/correct/right. Once we establish that, the author won the case.

If you're defending a client against murder, and you say "even if eddie did commit murder, there are a lot of good things he's done too", you will not be doing anything to help acquit him of murder.

This answer choice would weaken an argument that said, "With its more complex design, the new computer control system will increase the risk of accidental air bag inflation. Thus, we should not implement the new design."

**E. It overlooks the possibility that, even if air bags occasionally cause accidental injuries, they may nonetheless be responsible for preventing many more injuries than they cause.** — Bad Conclusion Match (3% of students)
This is arguing against an author who concluded that we should get rid of air bags.

It's objecting to an argument that would sound like this, "The accidental inflation of air bags is responsible for many injuries each year. Thus, it is time for us to get rid of air bags in cars." or "Thus, air bags increase the likelihood of being injured in a car"

### Stimulus

Educator: Environmental factors clearly have little effect on whether a teenager will participate in sports. ### Prompt

Which one of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the educator's argument?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* Environmental factors clearly have little effect on whether a teenager will participate in sports.

*Evidence:* The strongest environmental factor is probably family life, yet it's common for one teenager in a family to participate in sports while others in the same family don't like sports.

Also, school programs designed to get inactive teens to play sports are generally ineffective.

*Evaluate:* We have an Anti-Causal conclusion, so we'll be trying to argue that "environmental factors DO have much effect on whether teens participate in sports".

We can weaken the argument by "making excuses" for the author's premises or by introducing new evidence that helps us say "environmental factors do have a lot of effect".

Given That
Within a family, some teens play sports and others don't
How Could We Argue That
family life is operating as an environmental factor?

We would say a number of things:
- who says that the family is trying to influence all its kids in the same direction? (maybe you have an old-school Dad who tries to move his son towards sports but moves his daughters away from sports).
- what if the younger siblings want to differentiate themselves from the older sibling that got into sports?

Given That
School programs that try to get inactive teens into playing sports usually don't work,
How Could We Argue That
environmental factors do influence whether teens play sports?

Maybe we could say that these cheesy assemblies exert influence, just in an opposite, backfiring direction. Maybe teens are being convinced out of getting into high school sports based on who unappealing these school programs are.

Or we could just say that these programs are poorly designed and that if properly designed they would be able to exert some environmental influence.

*Goal:* Look for an answer "explaining away" either of the two premises in a way that protects the idea that environmental factors are playing a role, or for any answer that helps us argue that environmental factors are playing a role.

**Answer choices**
**A. Athletic ability varies, even among siblings, and teenagers who have demonstrable athletic ability are more likely than those who do not to participate in sports.** — No Impact or Strengthens (29% of students)
Athletic ability is not an environmental trait, it's an innate one. Environmental factors can certainly lead someone to augment or diminish their abilities, but this answer is explaining the variation within families in a way that seems closer to Innate factors than Environmental factors.

The tall / fast / strong kid is more likely to play sports than the short / slow / weak kid. That's not environmental factors having effect; that's innate physiological factors.

**B. Some teenagers, even those in schools that do not have any sports programs, are more enthusiastic about participating in sports than their parents are.** — Too Weak (11% of students)
The word "some" is wrong 98% of the time you see it in Strengthen, Weaken, and Paradox.

Can we really weaken this argument by saying, "Oh, author, did you know that at least one teenager exists who likes sports more than their parents?"

It isn't even really worth figuring out whether that data point agrees or disagrees, when it's just one measly data point that can't sway this conversation much.

**C. Adults' enthusiasm for participating in sports generally is directly proportional to the extent to which they participated in sports when they were younger.** — Out of Scope: adults' enthusiasm (10% of students)
This seems to suggest that environmental factors (did you play sports growing up) can have a big effect on whether an adult will participate in sports.

But it's not doing much to assess how that adult got into spots as teenager in the first place (Environmental or Innate factors?)

**D. The proportion of teenagers who participate in sports varies greatly from society to society and from decade to decade.** — Correct (44% of students)
This is a legendarily horrid correct answer. It's in my top 20. It has nothing to do with either Premise, which is why it's easy to dismiss on a first pass.

But, it allows us to push back against the author's conclusion. There aren't that many types of factors in the world: Environmental, Innate, and maybe that's it (random factors?)

Given that the proportion of teens who plays sports varies greatly across societies and decades, the author (who thinks Environmental factors have little effect) would have to say that Innate factors are the reason?

Even if you grant the author the suspect idea that Innate factors vary greatly across societies, nobody's gonna say that Innate factors vary greatly from decade to decade.

Since these data points are not compatible with a "Innate factors determine whether teens participate in sports" hypothesis, they suggest instead that Environmental factors must be at play here.

**E. School programs designed to encourage inactive teenagers to participate in sports widely vary in success, with only a few being highly successful.** — No Impact / Strengthens (6% of students)
We already knew that these school programs are generally ineffective (51% or more of them). But that premise left plenty of room for the idea that some of them are effective, even highly successful.

Since this fact is fully absorbed within the negative space of the 2nd premise, it doesn't change the conversation.

And since the conclusion isn't totally absolute, just "Environmental factors clearly have little effect", the author is willing to tolerate some tiny exceptions to his broad generalization.

To the extent that this answer says, "Hey ... we've tried tons of different approaches, and only a few have worked", it actually beats back one of our anticipated objections (what if your school programs are just lame, and a well-designed one would actually work?)

This answer suggests that we've tried a wide spectrum of approaches, and the fact that the vast majority of them failed starts to help the author's case.

This answer confuses me, so feel free to let me know if you have a different take. My theory is that it is suggesting something resembling Reverse Causality.

Whenever we're given correlations, such as
"people w/ trauma but no PTSD tend to have higher cortisol response levels than do people who have not experienced a trauma"
the author assumes one thing causes another, but often fails to establish that the supposed cause came before the supposed effect.

In this case, the author is assuming that these people did not have higher than average cortisol responses to stress until after their traumatic experience.

But maybe these people already had higher than average cortisol prior to this traumatic event. If so, then this answer would explain why these high cortisol people didn't develop PTSD.

Given our correlation ...
Trauma + No PTSD || Higher cortisol response

The author is thinking that the left caused the right. This answer is suggesting that the right caused the left (the no PTSD part).

**C. People experiencing a traumatic event produce more cortisol than they would under less severe instances of stress.** — No Impact (6% of students)
This answer links trauma to cortisol and stress, but it only speaks to the current moment of being traumatized, not its aftermath.

All people produce cortisol in response to stress. Traumatized people just produce more of it. But this answer choice is talking about during a traumatic event, you're producing tons of cortisol.

Of course! You're experiencing a traumatic event, which sounds like an extreme form of stress. If stress produces cortisol responses in everyone, then we'd naturally expect that a super stressful traumatic event would have really high cortisol levels.

But this answer isn't covering the causal mystery of this problem — now that the trauma is over, why are these traumatized people still having higher than average cortisol responses to stress?

**D. Many effective treatments for PTSD are designed to reduce how much cortisol those with PTSD produce when exposed to stress.** — Out of Scope: people with PTSD (6% of students)
If an effective treatment for PTSD involves reducing the cortisol response, then we can infer that "high cortisol levels worsen PTSD symptoms".

But what does that have to do with assessing why people who didn't get PTSD to have higher cortisol responses to stress?

If anything it seems like this would strengthen, because it adds more associations between trauma and higher cortisol levels.

**E. Experiencing a traumatic event can damage the gland that produces cortisol, resulting in that gland producing more cortisol.** — Strengthens (14% of students)
This adds plausibility to the idea that trauma causes a new cortisol response, by explaining the causal mechanism by which that would work.

## S4 · Q25 — Must be True

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

The technical sophistication of commercial fishing equipment increased steadily from 1960 through 2010, which enabled the commercial fishing industry to harvest a greater percentage of the total amount of fish, by weight, in the world's oceans in each succeeding year during that time. The commercial fishing industry's harvest, by weight, increased steadily from 1960 until 1995 but did not increase after 1995.

### Prompt

If the statements above are true, which one of the following must also be true?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Statements:* From 1960 to 2010, the fishing industry was able to harvest a greater percentage of the total amount of fish, by weight, in the world's oceans, in each succeeding year.

The harvest, by weight, increased steadily from 1960 to 1995, but didn't increase after 1995.

*Evaluate:* We usually read Must Be True looking for Conditional, Causal, or Math-y type ideas. This one seems to giving us Math-y ideas, so reconciling the first and second sentences will give us some mathematical inference.

We know that the fishing industry caught a higher percentage of the weight of the fish in the ocean each year.

Let's pretend there are 1,000 tons of fish in the ocean each year.

```
       % caught   tons caught
1960   10%        100
1965   11%        110
1970   12%        120
....
1995   20%        200
2000   25%        200
2005   50%        200
```

We complied with two different constraints there:
1. percentage keeps increasing from 1960 to 2010
2. overall weight of fish caught increases from 1960 to 1995
3. overall weight of fish caught stops increasing from 1995 to 2010

But notice something funky with our numbers. For a while they kept equaling 1000 tons of fish in the sea, but once the percentage goes up and the tonnage caught stays the same, it means that the total weight of fish in the sea has to start decreasing.

```
       % caught   tons caught   total in sea
1960   10%        100           1000
1965   11%        110           1000
1970   12%        120           1000
....
1995   20%        200           1000
2000   25%        200           800
2005   50%        200           400
```

The inference they're going for here is the same as this:
my rent has stayed the same for the past 10 years, but nowadays my rent is a bigger percentage of my income.

What can we infer?
My income is lower now than it used to be.

If I'm paying a higher tax rate this year than last year, but the government is still getting the same money from me, then that means my taxable income has gone down.

*Goal:* We don't know for sure what's going on from 1960 through 1995. It's possible that the overall weight of fish in the ocean goes down, stays the same, or goes up.

But once we get to 1995, we know for sure that the total weight of fish in the ocean starts going down, because the % of available fish weight being caught keeps rising but the actual weight of fish being caught does not increase.

**Answer choices**
**A. After 1995, the number of fish harvested each year by the commercial fishing industry increased, but the average weight of each fish harvested decreased.** — Out of Scope: number of fish (27% of students)
Nothing in this paragraph discusses number of fish at all, just weight, so we can't derive anything about number of fish.

**B. After 2010, improvements in commercial fishing equipment did not enable the fishing industry to harvest a greater percentage of the total amount of fish, by weight, in the world's oceans.** — Out of Scope: beyond 2010 (10% of students)
We don't have any information about anything after 2010, other than that the fishing industry's harvest by weight did not increase.

It's still quite possible that the % of available fish kept increasing. Just because the first sentence has a cutoff date doesn't mean that after that date things changed.

I can say "From 1960 to 2010, humans lived on Earth". That's a true statement and it does not logically imply that "after 2010, humans did not live on Earth".

**C. The total amount of fish, by weight, in the world's oceans decreased during the period from 1995 to 2010.** — Correct (56% of students)
This was our prediction. Since the fishing industry is catching a higher % of the available fish each year, but the amount they're catching is not going up, that means that the total available fish is going down.

**D. The commercial fishing industry's harvest, by weight, was significantly lower in 2010 than it was in 1995.** — Too Strong: significantly lower (6% of students)
We were told that the harvest weight did not increase from 1995 to 2010; but this is saying that the weight was significantly lower. We can't go from didn't increase to got much lower.

**E. No significant improvements in commercial fishing equipment occurred before 1960.** — Out of Scope: before 1960 (2% of students)
Similar to (B), we don't know anything about what happened prior to 1960. Just because technological sophistication of fishing equipment increased from 1960 to 2010 doesn't mean that this time period was the first time there had ever been a significant improvement in fishing equipment.

## S4 · Q26 — Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus

Current evidence indicates that there is no methane on Planet 253. If that is the case, it is certain that there is no life on Planet 253. Since microbes always produce methane, if there is no methane then there are no microbes.

### Prompt

The conclusion drawn above follows logically if which one of the following is assumed?

### Written (single style)

**Argument breakdown**

*Conclusion:* If there's no methane on Planet 253, then there's no life on Planet 253.

*Evidence (since):* Microbes always produce methane.
(if there is no methane, no microbes)

*Evaluate:* It's very annoying to find the Conclusion here because none of the claims sound obviously like an opinion, and there aren't argument indicator words otherwise helping to guide us.

The one indicator word we get is the Since. Any time we see,
Since _____ , _____ .
it means that the Since-claim is support for the second claim, which is a conclusion.

But there's no way the conclusion we're supposed to be proving with the correct answer is the second half of that last sentence.

Why? Because that's already a logically guaranteed conclusion. All they're doing in that last sentence is saying, "Since X → Y, if there's not Y then there's not X."

Since microbes always produce methane
Micr → Meth
We know if there's no methane, no microbes
~Meth → ~Micr

Okay, so the final sentence is just a statement and its contrapositive. That can't be the conclusion we need to prove.

The first sentence is just a factual claim about current evidence. So by process of elimination, we need to be considering the 2nd sentence the main conclusion.

The author is trying to prove that "if there's no methane, there's no life", which would look like this, if we elongated the arrow to show the intended logic path:

```
CONCLUSION
~methane ------------------------> ~life

EVIDENCE
~methane ----> ~microbes

MISSING PIECE OF THE PATH

~microbes ----> ~life
```

*Goal:* Let's find an answer saying, "If there are no microbes, then there's no life", or its contrapositive, "Wherever there is life, there are microbes".

Since we need to prove a claim about "no life" but the evidence never spoke about that, the correct answer absolute must be a claim that allows us to prove "no life". We can summarily reject any answer that doesn't mention Life or any answer that would only let you prove "there is life".

**Answer choices**
**A. There is, in fact, no methane on Planet 253.** — Unrelated to Goal (4% of students)
This doesn't mention "life" so it's immediately wrong. The conclusion of this argument was a conditional statement:

if there's no methane, then there's no life

Whenever we encounter conditional conclusions, we will never be trying to strengthen or weaken the idea that the trigger is true.

Suppose I made this conditional conclusion:
if humans colonized Mars, we would build roller coasters there.

You can't strengthen this by saying that we will colonize Mars or weaken this by saying we won't. I'm not saying we're actually going to colonize Mars. I'm only making a definitive claim "we would build roller coasters" about a hypothetical world in which we have colonized Mars.

So when we see a conditional conclusion, we just accept the Trigger as an implicit premise. You can only debate my roller coaster conclusion if you first just accept the supposition that this is a world where we've colonized Mars.

**B. If methane exists on Planet 253 then we would be able to detect its presence.** — Unrelated to Goal (8% of students)
Since this doesn't mention "life", we can immediately reject it.

**C. If there is no methane currently on Planet 253 then there has never been methane on Planet 253.** — Unrelated to Goal (3% of students)
Since this doesn't mention "life", we can immediately reject it.

**D. If there are no microbes on Planet 253 then there is no life on Planet 253.** — Correct (80% of students)
This is what we predicted.

The evidence told us this:
if no methane, no microbes
This answer adds on this:
if no microbes, then no life
which allows us to derive the conclusion:
If no methane on P253, then no life on P253

**E. If there is methane on Planet 253 then there must be life on Planet 253.** — Unrelated to Goal (5% of students)
This mentions "life", but this is a rule that is set up to prove "there is life". We need a rule that proves there isn't life.
