# Section 2 (Logical Reasoning)

## S2 · Q1 — Must be True

### Stimulus
One should not do anything that has the potential to produce serious harm to one’s society. The public actions—or inactions—of celebrities and of people who are widely respected are widely emulated. Some celebrities do not vote. Serious harm befalls a society in which many people refrain from voting.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles can be properly inferred from the statements above?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given:**
  - One should not do anything that has the potential to produce serious harm to one’s society.
  - The public actions of celebrities and of people who are widely respected are widely emulated.
  - Some celebrities do not vote.
  - Serious harm befalls a society in which many people refrain from voting.
- **Evaluate:** Since this is a Must Be True question, we want to combine statements that are about the same concepts. Does anything show up in more than one statement?

  Statements #2 and #3 are both about celebrities. We're told that celebrities' public actions or inactions are widely emulated, and that some celebrities do not vote. We can conclude that, if celebrities' decisions to not vote were made public, many other people would refrain from voting.

  "Harm" shows up in statements #1 and #4. We're told that one should avoid doing anything that might cause serious harm to one's society, and that serious harm will occur when many people in a society refrain from voting. From these statements, we can conclude that one should not do anything that would cause many people to refrain from voting.

  We can infer an additional recommendation by combining these two conclusions: if celebrities are going to refrain from voting, they should not make the public aware of it.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that is absolutely irrefutable. It will probably be derived from combining at least two of the given statements, and maybe more. But it doesn’t have to tie in every statement to be correct. Beware answers that are too strong, bring in out of scope ideas, or express unsupported relationships between the concepts in the stimulus.

**Answer choices**
**A. A society should require all celebrities to vote.** — Too Strong: require all (5% of students)
This answer says society should "require all celebrities to vote." The stimulus never makes prescriptions about what society should require — it only states what an individual "should not do." Additionally, the statements do not demand that every celebrity vote. The key insight from the stimulus is that celebrities' public inactions are emulated. A celebrity could refrain from voting without causing harm, as long as that decision is not made public. Requiring all celebrities to vote goes far beyond what the premises support.

**B. One should vote only if one expects that doing so will cause many other people to do likewise.** — Too Broad: one / Too Strong: should vote (3% of students)
This answer says "one should vote only if one expects that doing so will cause many other people to do likewise." The word "one" applies the principle to everyone, but the stimulus specifically focuses on celebrities and widely respected people — those whose public actions are widely emulated. The stimulus does not make claims about ordinary individuals whose behavior is not emulated. Furthermore, the stimulus never says anyone "should vote." The argument establishes that celebrities should not publicly refrain from voting, which is very different from saying they should vote. This answer also wrongly introduces a condition about expecting to cause others to vote, which has no basis in the premises.

**C. Celebrities who do not vote should not be widely respected.** — Too Speculative (4% of students)
This answer says celebrities who do not vote "should not be widely respected." While the stimulus does establish that celebrities who publicly refrain from voting can cause societal harm, it never addresses what consequences should follow for such celebrities. The stimulus provides no basis for concluding that someone should lose respect because of their voting behavior. The argument is about what actions should or should not be taken publicly — not about whether someone deserves respect. This answer requires speculative reasoning that goes beyond what the premises directly support.

**D. People should not emulate celebrities who are not widely respected.** — Too Speculative (2% of students)
This answer says "people should not emulate celebrities who are not widely respected." The stimulus discusses celebrities and widely respected people together in the second premise, stating that their public actions are widely emulated. This answer asks us to speculate about a category not addressed in the stimulus: celebrities who are not widely respected. The stimulus provides no information about whether people emulate such celebrities or what they should do about it. Additionally, the stimulus never tells people who they should or should not emulate — it addresses what celebrities and respected people should or should not do publicly. This answer rearranges the stimulus concepts into a combination the premises do not support.

**E. Widely respected people should not publicly refrain from voting.** — Correct (86% of students)
This answer follows directly from combining the stimulus's premises. Widely respected people's public actions are widely emulated (premise 2). If widely respected people publicly refrain from voting, many others will follow suit. Serious harm befalls a society where many refrain from voting (premise 4). One should not do anything that could produce serious harm to one's society (premise 1). Therefore, widely respected people should not publicly refrain from voting. The stimulus specifically mentions celebrities in premise 3, but premise 2 extends the emulation effect to "people who are widely respected" as well. This answer captures the correct scope and avoids the overreach of requiring people to vote — it only prohibits publicly refraining from voting.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given:** One should not do anything that could produce serious harm to one's society. Celebrities' and widely respected people's public actions are widely emulated. Some celebrities do not vote. Serious harm befalls a society where many people refrain from voting.
- **Evaluate:** Combining these statements: if celebrities publicly refrain from voting, many people will emulate them, causing serious societal harm. Therefore, widely respected people should not publicly refrain from voting.
- **Goal:** Find an answer that must be true based on combining these statements. It will likely address widely emulated people keeping their non-voting private.

**Answer choices**
**A. A society should require all celebrities to vote.** — Too Strong: require all (5% of students)
The stimulus says what individuals should avoid doing, not what society should require. Celebrities can refrain from voting without causing harm if they do not do so publicly — there is no basis for requiring all of them to vote.

**B. One should vote only if one expects that doing so will cause many other people to do likewise.** — Too Broad: one / Too Strong: should vote (3% of students)
The stimulus focuses on celebrities and widely respected people, not everyone. And it never says anyone "should vote" — only that widely emulated people should not publicly refrain from voting. These are different claims.

**C. Celebrities who do not vote should not be widely respected.** — Too Speculative (4% of students)
The stimulus never addresses whether non-voting celebrities should lose respect. The argument is about avoiding publicly harmful actions, not about who deserves to be respected.

**D. People should not emulate celebrities who are not widely respected.** — Too Speculative (2% of students)
The stimulus does not address celebrities who lack widespread respect, nor does it instruct people about who they should or should not emulate. This answer rearranges the stimulus's concepts into an unsupported combination.

**E. Widely respected people should not publicly refrain from voting.** — Correct (86% of students)
Combining the premises: widely respected people's public behavior is emulated, mass non-voting causes serious harm, and one should avoid causing serious harm. Therefore, widely respected people should not publicly refrain from voting. This follows directly from the given statements.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given:** Four puzzle pieces on the table: (1) do not do things that could seriously harm society, (2) celebrities and widely respected people are walking billboards whose public behavior gets copied, (3) some celebrities skip the ballot box, and (4) widespread non-voting is seriously bad for society.
- **Evaluate:** Snap these pieces together: if a celebrity publicly sits out an election, a herd of admirers will follow suit. Mass non-voting causes serious harm. And doing things that cause serious harm is a no-no. The escape hatch? The word "public." Nobody said celebrities must vote — just that if they are going to skip it, they should keep that to themselves.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that connects these dots without overreaching. Anything demanding all celebrities vote or applying the rule to everybody goes too far.

**Answer choices**
**A. A society should require all celebrities to vote.** — Too Strong: require all (5% of students)
Nobody in the stimulus is drafting legislation here. The stimulus talks about what a person "should not do" — a moral recommendation, not a government mandate. And "all celebrities" is a tall order. The argument leaves a perfectly good loophole: celebrities can skip voting as long as they do not broadcast it. Requiring every single one of them to vote is like banning umbrellas because some people poke others with them.

**B. One should vote only if one expects that doing so will cause many other people to do likewise.** — Too Broad: one / Too Strong: should vote (3% of students)
The stimulus is talking about famous people whose every move gets copied — not your neighbor Dave. Applying this principle to "one" (meaning everyone) stretches the argument well past its breaking point. And the stimulus never actually tells anyone to vote. It says influential people should not publicly sit elections out. That is a very different instruction. "Do not publicly skip voting" and "you should vote" are about as similar as "do not publicly eat the last donut" and "you should bake donuts."

**C. Celebrities who do not vote should not be widely respected.** — Too Speculative (4% of students)
The stimulus says influential people should not publicly skip voting. It does not say they should be exiled from the respect club if they do. Going from "your public non-voting could cause harm" to "you do not deserve respect" requires a logical leap the stimulus never takes. The argument is about behavior management, not reputation management. Plenty of widely respected people have done worse things than not voting — the stimulus is not trying to build a celebrity report card.

**D. People should not emulate celebrities who are not widely respected.** — Too Speculative (2% of students)
This answer takes the stimulus's ingredients, throws them in a blender, and produces something the stimulus never ordered. The stimulus is about what celebrities and respected people should avoid doing publicly. It is not a guide for the general public on whose behavior to copy. "Celebrities who are not widely respected" is a category the stimulus does not even acknowledge exists, let alone offer advice about. This is a word salad of stimulus terms arranged into a thought the argument never had.

**E. Widely respected people should not publicly refrain from voting.** — Correct (86% of students)
This is the domino chain the stimulus sets up, played out perfectly. Widely respected person publicly does not vote. Fans see it, fans copy it. Mass non-voting causes serious harm. Causing serious harm is a no-no. Conclusion: widely respected people should keep their non-voting to themselves. Notice the precision here — nobody is being told to vote. They are being told not to broadcast it if they do not. The word "publicly" does all the work, and this answer nails it.

## S2 · Q2 — Weaken

### Stimulus
The sculptor Barajas died before she could even begin sculpting the statue called *Sonora*. However, because *Sonora* was sculpted by Barajas’s assistants, working from three sketches Barajas drew in preparing to create the statue herself, the statue probably looks very much like it would have looked if she had lived to complete it.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence (because):** *Sonora* was sculpted by Barajas’s assistants, working from three sketches Barajas drew in preparing to create the statue herself.
- **Conclusion:** The statue probably looks very much like it would have looked if Barajas had lived to complete it.
- **Evaluate:** The evidence in this argument is pretty flimsy, leaving the argument vulnerable to a host of different objections. Do we know if Barajas’s assistants were skilled enough to sculpt *Sonora* as well as Barajas could herself? Did the sketches provide enough information for the assistants to know how Barajas wanted the completed statue to look? What if Barajas' statues often ended up looking quite different from her sketches?
- **Goal:** A correct answer might bring up any of these possible objections. But correct answers to Weaken questions don't always match our predictions, so we'll need to keep an open mind and think about how each answer would impact the argument. Watch out for answers that have little impact, if any, or ones that strengthen the argument rather than weakening it.

**Answer choices**
**A. Ordinarily, Barajas’s ideas for her statues were revised substantially throughout a series of dozens of preliminary sketches.** — Correct (78% of students)
This answer directly undermines the assumption that three sketches were sufficient to convey Barajas's final vision for *Sonora*. If Barajas ordinarily revised her ideas substantially throughout dozens of preliminary sketches, then three sketches represent a very early stage of her creative process — far from the refined vision she would have ultimately executed. The assistants were working from what amounts to the first chapter of a very long creative journey. With only three sketches, they lacked access to the extensive revisions Barajas would normally have made before arriving at her final design. This makes it much less likely that their version of *Sonora* resembles what Barajas would have produced.

**B. One of the assistants who worked closely with Barajas in planning *Sonora* did not participate in sculpting it.** — Weaker Impact (3% of students)
This answer states that one assistant who worked closely with Barajas in planning *Sonora* did not participate in sculpting it. While this might seem concerning, we do not know how many assistants were involved in total, how many participated in the planning phase, or how significant this one absent assistant's contribution would have been. For all we know, several other assistants who also planned with Barajas did participate in the sculpting. The impact of one single assistant's absence is uncertain and could range from negligible to significant, depending on unknown factors. This answer has some weakening potential but is far less definitive than the correct answer.

**C. *Sonora* is composed partly of materials that Barajas did not frequently use in the statues that she completed during her lifetime.** — Weaker Impact (18% of students)
This answer notes that *Sonora* is composed partly of materials Barajas did not frequently use. At first glance, unfamiliar materials seem problematic. However, the answer is carefully hedged: "partly" of materials she did not "frequently" use. The unfamiliar materials could constitute a very small portion of the statue. And "not frequently" does not mean "never" — Barajas may have used these materials in some previous works, giving her assistants experience with how she handled them. The hedged language limits this answer's weakening force considerably.

**D. Barajas always worked from sketches when she sculpted a statue.** — Opposite (if anything) (0% of students)
This answer states that Barajas always worked from sketches when sculpting. If anything, this strengthens the argument rather than weakening it. If Barajas always used sketches as her guide, then her assistants' approach of working from sketches mirrors her own process. This makes it more plausible, not less, that the assistants could produce a statue resembling what Barajas would have created. The answer reinforces the relevance of the sketches as a guide rather than undermining it.

**E. Barajas never took as long to complete a statue as the assistants did to sculpt *Sonora*.** — Irrelevant Distinction (0% of students)
This answer states that Barajas never took as long to complete a statue as her assistants did to sculpt *Sonora*. The time required to complete the statue has no clear bearing on whether the finished product resembles what Barajas would have created. Working more slowly does not mean the result looks different — the assistants might have been more methodical precisely because they were trying to faithfully follow the sketches. The argument's conclusion is about resemblance, not efficiency. Speed of completion is an irrelevant variable.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence (because):** *Sonora* was sculpted by Barajas's assistants, working from three sketches Barajas drew in preparation.
- **Conclusion:** The statue probably looks very much like it would have if Barajas had lived to complete it.
- **Evaluate:** The argument assumes three sketches were sufficient for the assistants to know how Barajas wanted the finished statue to look. If Barajas typically used many more sketches and revised substantially throughout the process, three sketches might represent a very early stage of her vision.
- **Goal:** Find an answer suggesting the three sketches were insufficient to convey Barajas's final vision, or that the assistants lacked the ability to replicate her work.

**Answer choices**
**A. Ordinarily, Barajas’s ideas for her statues were revised substantially throughout a series of dozens of preliminary sketches.** — Correct (78% of students)
If Barajas typically revised her ideas across dozens of sketches, three sketches represent a very early stage. The assistants were working from an incomplete vision, making it less likely the statue matches what Barajas would have created.

**B. One of the assistants who worked closely with Barajas in planning *Sonora* did not participate in sculpting it.** — Weaker Impact (3% of students)
We do not know how many assistants total were involved or how significant this one absent assistant's role would have been. The impact is too uncertain to meaningfully weaken the argument.

**C. *Sonora* is composed partly of materials that Barajas did not frequently use in the statues that she completed during her lifetime.** — Weaker Impact (18% of students)
"Partly" could mean a very small portion, and "not frequently" does not mean "never." The hedged language severely limits this answer's impact on the argument.

**D. Barajas always worked from sketches when she sculpted a statue.** — Opposite (if anything) (0% of students)
If Barajas always worked from sketches, the assistants' sketch-based approach mirrors her own process. This makes it more likely the result resembles Barajas's vision, strengthening the argument.

**E. Barajas never took as long to complete a statue as the assistants did to sculpt *Sonora*.** — Irrelevant Distinction (0% of students)
How long the assistants took compared to Barajas does not indicate whether the result looks the same. The conclusion is about visual resemblance, not production speed.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence (because):** Barajas's assistants built *Sonora* using three sketches the artist drew before she died. Three sketches — that is the entire blueprint.
- **Conclusion:** The statue probably looks like it would have if Barajas had finished it herself.
- **Evaluate:** Three sketches sounds like a reasonable amount of guidance — until you wonder whether Barajas was the type of artist who drew three sketches and was done planning, or the type who drew fifty sketches and was still changing her mind. If she normally went through dozens of revisions, those three sketches might represent the "rough draft of the rough draft" stage. The assistants would basically be finishing a painting from the artist's napkin doodle.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that makes those three sketches look woefully inadequate.

**Answer choices**
**A. Ordinarily, Barajas’s ideas for her statues were revised substantially throughout a series of dozens of preliminary sketches.** — Correct (78% of students)
Turns out Barajas was the kind of artist who typically went through dozens of sketches, making substantial changes along the way. So those three sketches the assistants had? That is like reading the first three pages of a mystery novel and trying to write the ending. Barajas's creative process was apparently a long and winding road, and the assistants had the map for the first mile. The statue they built might be beautiful, but "looks like what Barajas would have made" is a stretch when they were working from rough draft number three of thirty.

**B. One of the assistants who worked closely with Barajas in planning *Sonora* did not participate in sculpting it.** — Weaker Impact (3% of students)
One assistant who helped plan the statue sat out the sculpting. That sounds bad — but was this the head assistant who knew Barajas's every artistic preference, or the assistant who mostly fetched coffee during planning meetings? The argument does not specify, and this answer does not either. Maybe five other planning assistants did participate. Maybe this one missing person is irrelevant. There is a possible problem here, but it is floating in a sea of unknowns.

**C. *Sonora* is composed partly of materials that Barajas did not frequently use in the statues that she completed during her lifetime.** — Weaker Impact (18% of students)
"Partly" and "not frequently" are doing a lot of damage-control for this answer. "Partly" could mean two percent of the statue uses different marble. "Not frequently" could mean Barajas used these materials in three previous works instead of thirty. So the assistants might be dealing with a small portion of slightly-less-familiar materials — which sounds more like a minor inconvenience than a serious obstacle to getting the statue right.

**D. Barajas always worked from sketches when she sculpted a statue.** — Opposite (if anything) (0% of students)
So Barajas always worked from sketches? Great — that means sketches were central to her process, which means the assistants had the right type of starting point. This is like learning that a chef always works from recipes and then being asked whether giving the chef's recipe to someone else weakens or strengthens the chance they will produce the same dish. The answer is obviously "strengthens." Wrong direction for a Weaken question.

**E. Barajas never took as long to complete a statue as the assistants did to sculpt *Sonora*.** — Irrelevant Distinction (0% of students)
The assistants took longer than Barajas would have. And? A slow painter can produce the same painting as a fast one — speed reveals nothing about accuracy. Maybe the assistants were slower because they were being extra careful to match Barajas's vision. Maybe they were slower because they kept arguing about what the sketches meant. The time difference is a red herring that says nothing about whether the statue looks right.

## S2 · Q3 — Most Supported

### Stimulus
Although there are immediate short-term gains in crop yield from a single application of certain hydrocarbon-based pesticides to fields on which they have not been previously used, studies have shown clearly that long-term use gradually depresses crop yield from this initially elevated level. We should conclude that use of these pesticides _______.

### Prompt
Which one of the following would most logically complete the argument above?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given:**
  - There are immediate short-term gains in crop yield from a single application of certain hydrocarbon-based pesticides to fields on which they have not been previously used.
  - Studies have shown clearly that long-term use gradually depresses crop yield from this initially elevated level.
- **Evaluate:** The word "although" indicates a concession, and concessions are typically Opposing Ideas. So the first sentence about immediate short-term gains looks like an idea that the conclusion of the argument will contradict.

  “We should conclude that” tells us that whatever fills the blank will be the conclusion, but it also tells us that the statement immediately before this sentence—the statement about long-term use—must be the evidence.
- **Goal:** We're looking for a conclusion about the use of these pesticides that is based on the fact that they gradually decrease crop yields. This conclusion will probably dampen our enthusiasm about the initial short-term gains. For a Most Strongly Supported question, beware of answers that are too strong for the evidence we're given or any answer with a new idea which might be out of scope.

**Answer choices**
**A. is uneconomic** — Too Speculative (6% of students)
Calling pesticide use "uneconomic" goes beyond what the evidence supports. The evidence states that long-term use depresses crop yield from the initially elevated level, but we do not know by how much. The yield could still remain higher than it would be without pesticides, in which case the pesticides would still provide an economic benefit — just a decreasing one. "Uneconomic" means the costs outweigh the benefits entirely, and the evidence does not establish that threshold. Additionally, economic analysis would require information about the cost of the pesticides relative to the value of increased yield, which the stimulus does not provide.

**B. damages the environment** — Out of Scope (8% of students)
The argument discusses crop yield, not environmental damage. Nothing in the stimulus addresses the environmental impact of hydrocarbon-based pesticides. While pesticides might damage the environment, this is an entirely separate topic from the agricultural economics discussed in the stimulus. The conclusion must follow from the given evidence about yield patterns, not from unstated concerns about ecology.

**C. will eventually make pest problems unmanageable** — Too Speculative / Too Strong: unmanageable (3% of students)
This answer makes two unsupported leaps. First, the stimulus never discusses pest problems at all — it discusses crop yields. While pests and crop yields are related, the evidence does not address pest populations, resistance, or management. Second, "unmanageable" is an extreme descriptor that the evidence cannot support. Even if we could infer something about pest problems from crop yield data, the evidence only describes gradually declining yields, not catastrophic pest-management failure. This answer introduces both a new topic and an extreme conclusion.

**D. is probably not occurring in accordance with manufacturers’ instructions** — Out of Scope (1% of students)
The argument provides no information whatsoever about manufacturers' instructions. We do not know what those instructions recommend regarding application frequency, dosage, or duration. The evidence describes what happens when the pesticides are used long-term, but it does not compare actual usage patterns to recommended ones. Without any reference point for what manufacturers advise, we cannot conclude that usage is or is not occurring in accordance with their instructions.

**E. gives financial returns that diminish over time** — Correct (82% of students)
This answer correctly captures the diminishing-returns pattern described in the evidence without overstating it. The evidence establishes that initial pesticide application produces gains in crop yield, and long-term use gradually depresses yields from that elevated level. "Financial returns that diminish over time" accurately describes this trajectory — there are returns (unlike answer A, which claims the whole enterprise is uneconomic), but they decrease over time. The answer avoids claims about environmental damage, pest management failure, or manufacturers' instructions, staying within the bounds of what the evidence supports. It correctly treats the initial yield gain as a financial return and the gradual decline as diminishing returns.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given:** First application of certain hydrocarbon-based pesticides produces short-term gains in crop yield on new fields. However, long-term use gradually depresses crop yield from the initially elevated level.
- **Evaluate:** The concession word "although" introduces the short-term gain, while the main point focuses on long-term decline. The conclusion must follow from the evidence about gradual yield depression.
- **Goal:** Find a conclusion about pesticide use that is supported by the long-term decline in yields without being too strong. It should acknowledge diminishing returns without claiming the pesticides are entirely harmful.

**Answer choices**
**A. is uneconomic** — Too Speculative (6% of students)
The evidence says yields decline from the elevated level but does not establish they drop below the pre-pesticide baseline. The pesticides could still be economically worthwhile, just less so over time. "Uneconomic" overstates the case.

**B. damages the environment** — Out of Scope (8% of students)
The argument is about crop yield trends, not environmental impact. Nothing in the stimulus addresses whether the pesticides damage the environment.

**C. will eventually make pest problems unmanageable** — Too Speculative / Too Strong: unmanageable (3% of students)
The stimulus discusses crop yields, not pest management. "Unmanageable" is extreme and unsupported — declining yields do not establish that pests become impossible to control.

**D. is probably not occurring in accordance with manufacturers’ instructions** — Out of Scope (1% of students)
The stimulus provides no information about manufacturers' instructions. We cannot conclude anything about whether usage aligns with those recommendations.

**E. gives financial returns that diminish over time** — Correct (82% of students)
The evidence shows initial yield gains followed by gradual decline. "Financial returns that diminish over time" captures this precisely — there are returns, but they decrease. This avoids overstating into "uneconomic" while accurately reflecting the declining trajectory.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given:** These pesticides are the ultimate short-term relationship: great first impression, diminishing returns over time. The first application gives a nice yield boost. Then, with continued use, yields start sliding back down from that high point.
- **Evaluate:** The "although" at the beginning is LSAT code for "yes, but actually..." The short-term gain is the concession; the long-term decline is the real story. And notice what the evidence does not say — it does not say yields drop below where they started. They drop from the "initially elevated level." So the pesticides might still be helping, just less and less.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that captures "diminishing returns" without overcommitting to "total waste of money" or "environmental catastrophe."

**Answer choices**
**A. is uneconomic** — Too Speculative (6% of students)
"Uneconomic" means the whole enterprise is a loss — money in, nothing worthwhile out. But the evidence only says yields go down from the high point. They could still be above where they started. Imagine buying a stock that doubles and then loses thirty percent — disappointing, sure, but not exactly uneconomic. The evidence supports "increasingly less profitable," not "throw your money in the trash."

**B. damages the environment** — Out of Scope (8% of students)
The stimulus is talking about crop yields going up and then gradually coming down. Nobody mentioned the environment. For all this argument reveals, the pesticides could be made of unicorn tears that heal the ozone layer. The evidence is strictly about agricultural output, and the conclusion needs to stay in that lane.

**C. will eventually make pest problems unmanageable** — Too Speculative / Too Strong: unmanageable (3% of students)
The argument is about crop yields slowly declining, and this answer jumps to "pest apocalypse." The stimulus does not even mention pests directly, let alone claim they become unmanageable. Going from "yields decrease gradually from an elevated level" to "pest problems spiral out of control" is like going from "my car gets slightly worse gas mileage over time" to "my engine will explode." Scale and topic are both wrong.

**D. is probably not occurring in accordance with manufacturers’ instructions** — Out of Scope (1% of students)
Manufacturers' instructions? The stimulus never mentions a label, a manual, or a customer service hotline. There is no indication what the manufacturers recommend. Maybe they say "use once and done." Maybe they say "apply weekly forever." The evidence describes what happens when these pesticides are used long-term; it says nothing about whether that usage pattern matches what the manufacturer had in mind.

**E. gives financial returns that diminish over time** — Correct (82% of students)
This is the Goldilocks answer — not too hot, not too cold. The pesticides do produce returns (unlike "uneconomic"), but those returns get smaller over time (which is exactly what "gradually depresses crop yield from this initially elevated level" means in financial terms). It is the difference between "this investment is a total loss" and "this investment keeps making money, just less and less each year." The evidence supports the second, and this answer nails it.

## S2 · Q4 — Weaken

### Stimulus
Economist: Our country needs as much capital as possible from overseas investors in order to sustain our economy. Hence, we cannot afford any reduction in the amount of capital that overseas investors have invested here. Therefore, to sustain our economy, we should pass laws making it much more difficult for overseas investors to remove their capital.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the economist’s reasoning?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** Our country needs as much capital as possible from overseas investors in order to sustain our economy.
- **Intermediate Conclusion (Hence):** We cannot afford any reduction in the amount of capital that these investors have invested here.
- **Main Conclusion (Therefore):** To sustain our economy, we should pass laws making it much more difficult for overseas investors to remove their capital.
- **Evaluate:** There isn't a huge gap in logic between needing as much capital as possible (the evidence) and not wanting any reduction in the existing amount of capital (the intermediate conclusion).

  But there is a significant gap between the intermediate conclusion and the main conclusion. Wanting to avoid any reduction in investments does not automatically support the idea that we should pass laws making it more difficult for investors to remove their capital.

  This main conclusion is a recommendation. Plans and recommendations tend to be vulnerable to a variety of objections, and the correct answer to a Weaken question might focus on any one of them. It helps to think about the kinds of objections that this argument is particularly vulnerable to.

  For example, a recommendation might have the opposite effect. Maybe these laws would discourage investors from making future investments, which would prevent us from acquiring "as much capital as possible." Or there might be some reason why this particular recommendation can't be implemented, or won't be effective. Maybe these types of laws are almost impossible to enforce, and therefore tend to be ineffective.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that suggests the recommendation might backfire or be impossible to implement correctly. But, since objections can take many forms, read answers with an open mind. We want to notice if one attacks the recommendation in another way.

**Answer choices**
**A. To sustain its economy, the country needs to diversify its investments more evenly across the country’s industries.** — Out of Scope: diversify (2% of students)
This answer discusses diversifying investments across the country's industries. The argument is about attracting and retaining overseas investment capital, not about how that capital is distributed across industries. Whether the country needs to diversify its own investments is a separate issue from whether it should restrict overseas investors' ability to remove their capital. This answer addresses a different economic question entirely.

**Argument breakdown**
- **Given:**
  - *contrast:* Mm's generated by cosmic rays just outside Earth's atmosphere travel at speeds approaching the speed of light, **whereas** mm's in the lab are nearly at rest.
  - *math-y contrast:* Time it takes for lab-mm to decay < time it takes for space mm to reach our detection apparatus.
  - *conditional + **implicit fact** that triggers contrapositive:* If mm's traveling through the atmosphere at speeds approaching the speed of light typically decay as fast as lab-mm's, then we should detect only about one one-hundredth of the number **we actually do detect.**
- **Evaluate:** It's helpful to be clear about the information we've been given and how the different statements connect to one another. We're told that mu mesons—let's just call those MMs—are created just outside of Earth's atmosphere and travel to Earth at close to the speed of light. That's fast. We know something about the speed of MMs created in the laboratory: they're nearly at rest—so, almost not moving. We're told that these resting MMs in the lab typically decay faster than the time it takes for an MM to travel from outside the atmosphere to a detector on Earth. If you notice some connections between these statements, that's great. But there's something very interesting to notice about the fourth sentence. It contains a conditional statement: "If mu mesons traveling through the atmosphere at speeds approaching the speed of light typically decay as fast as they do in the laboratory, then we should detect only about one one-hundredth of the number we actually do detect." This statement is already telling us that the necessary condition—the "then" part—is not true. We are not detecting one one-hundredth of the number we actually do detect. We are detecting the number that we actually do detect. Think about the contrapositive: If we don't detect one one-hundredth of the number that we actually do detect, then MMs traveling through the atmosphere do not decay as fast as they do in the laboratory. The sufficient condition—the "if" part—is automatically triggered. We don't detect one-one hundredth of the number that we actually detect. We detect the number that we actually detect. This means that the necessary condition—the "then" part—must be true. MMs traveling through the atmosphere do not decay as fast as they do in the laboratory. The final sentence in the stimulus, the one we need to complete, mentions MMs "moving at speeds near the speed of light." Those are the ones in the atmosphere. We can expect the rest of this sentence to state that they don't decay as fast as ones in the laboratory.
- **Goal:** If we can look past the scientific terms and get a handle on the essence of what these statements are telling us, we can make a very good prediction. We expect a correct answer to indicate that MMs traveling through the atmosphere decay more slowly that ones created in the lab.
- **Helpful Tip:** Don't let the LSAT distract or intimidate you with fancy science terms. You don't need to know what a mu meson is. It's a thing. You don't need to know the exact speed of light. It's fast. We don't even need to know exactly what "decay" means in this context. Whatever it is, we're told it's something that can happen at different speeds. That's all we really need to understand. You could literally replace "decay" with "change color" or "turn into elephants" and still get the correct answer. Try it. The science might not make sense, but the logical connections would stay the same. And we're being tested on our understanding of the logical connections, not the science.

We detect far more atmospheric mu mesons than the lab decay rate predicts, meaning they survive longer than expected, not that they travel slower.
