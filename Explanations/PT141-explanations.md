# Section 2 (Logical Reasoning)

## S2 · Q2 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Jeneta: Increasingly, I've noticed that when a salesperson thanks a customer for making a purchase, the customer also says "Thank you" instead of saying "You're welcome." I've even started doing that myself. But when a friend thanks a friend for a favor, the response is always "You're welcome."

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the discrepancy that Jeneta observes in people's responses?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that ...:** We say "you're welcome" when a friend says "thank you for doing me that favor".
- **Why is it that ...:** We say "thank you" when a cashier says "thank you for your purchase"?
- **Evaluate / Goal:** We need a way to differentiate these two situations so that it makes sense why in a store we reciprocate a "thank you" with our own "thank you", whereas with friends we reciprocate a "thank you" with a "you're welcome".

While it's usually dangerous to predict answers to Paradox, this one seems fairly normal to understand from normal life.

With a friend for whom we've done a favor, they've gotten benefit but we haven't (other than the fuzzy feeling of doing something nice for a friend).

But when you make a purchase at a store, the cashier has benefited by getting your money and you have benefited by getting the product you just purchased. So maybe we say "thank you" because we appreciate the product we're receiving, or maybe the salesperson helped us find the right product or did something else to benefit us.

**Answer choices**
**A. Customers regard themselves as doing salespeople a favor by buying from them as opposed to someone else.** — Deepens Paradox (4% of students)
This makes the store and the friend situations more similar. We need a way to distinguish them.

When we've done a friend a favor, we regard ourselves as having done them a favor (obvi), so we say "you're welcome".

If we regard purchases similarly as doing the salesperson a favor, then we would seemingly also say "you're welcome".

**B. Salespeople are often instructed by their employers to thank customers, whereas customers are free to say what they want.** — No Distinction / No Impact (1% of students)
This provides no distinction between the store scenario and the friend scenario.

In the store, we are free to say what we want (we can say "thank you", "you're welcome", or anything we choose).

Okay, but in the friend scenario we are also free to say what we want.

So since that doesn't present any distinction between the two situations, it doesn't offer a way to explain why we say "thank you" in one situation but "you're welcome" in the other.

**C. Salespeople do not regard customers who buy from them as doing them a favor.** — Unrelated to Goal (3% of students)
This answer would explain why a friend would say "Thanks!" when we've done them a favor but a salesperson wouldn't say "Thanks" when we've made a purchase.

But in both cases, the friend and the salesperson do say "Thanks", so the question still remains why our response to one is different from our response to the other.

The correct answer should be about the customer, not the salesperson.

**D. The way that people respond to being thanked is generally determined by habit rather than by conscious decision.** — No Distinction (9% of students)
This answer applies to both the store and the friend situation similarly. In both cases, we are responding to being thanked, and so in both cases our response will be determined by habit.

But why do we have a habit of saying "thank you" to salespeople and saying "you're welcome" to friends?

**E. In a commercial transaction, as opposed to a favor, the customer feels that the benefits are mutual.** — Correct (82% of students)
This provides a distinction; it even spells out the fact that in the case of doing someone a favor you do not feel mutual benefit.

The benefit is one-sided. You helped your friend move apartments. They benefited a lot. You worked your butt off. So you say "you're welcome" when they thank you because it's asymmetric.

When you give the salesperson your money and he gives you an Xbox, you feel like you just benefited, so you express your gratitude for your new Xbox by saying "Thank you".

## S2 · Q3 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus
Some video game makers have sold the movie rights for popular games. However, this move is rarely good from a business perspective. After all, StarQuanta sold the movie rights to its popular game *Nostroma*, but the poorly made film adaptation of the game was hated by critics and the public alike. Subsequent versions of the *Nostroma* video game, although better than the original, sold poorly.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the argument is most vulnerable to criticism in that the argument

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Selling movie rights is rarely good business for video game makers.
- **Evidence:** StarQuanta sold the rights to Nostroma, the film flopped, and subsequent Nostroma game sequels (despite being better than the original) sold poorly.
- **Evaluate:** The conclusion is a general claim about all (or most) video game makers selling movie rights. The evidence is one company's experience with one game. A single case can't establish what's "rarely good" across the industry. The argument leaps from one example to a broad rule.
- **Goal:** Find an answer that names the move from a single case to a general conclusion — i.e., the small-sample/anecdote-to-rule flaw.

**Answer choices**
**A. draws a general conclusion on the basis of just one individual case** — Correct (86% of students)
This is the flaw. The conclusion is general — that selling movie rights is rarely a good business move — but the only evidence is one company's bad experience with one game. A single case can't establish what is "rarely" the case across an industry. That's the textbook leap from anecdote to general rule.

**B. infers that a product will be disliked by the public merely from the claim that the product was disliked by critics** — Bad Description (3% of students)
This says the argument leaps from "critics disliked it" to "the public disliked it." But the stimulus actually says the film "was hated by critics and the public alike" — it doesn't infer one from the other. So this answer mischaracterizes the structure.

**C. restates as a conclusion a claim earlier presented as evidence for that conclusion** — Circular Reasoning (2% of students)
The premise (StarQuanta's bad outcome) and the conclusion (selling rights is rarely good) are different claims — one is about a single company, the other is a generalization about all game makers. The conclusion isn't snuck into a premise. The flaw is a small sample, not circularity.

**D. takes for granted that products with similar content that are in different media will be of roughly equal popularity** — Bad Assumption (6% of students)
This says the argument assumes products with similar content in different media will have roughly equal popularity. But the argument doesn't need or use that assumption — it just notes that the film flopped and that game sequels sold poorly afterward. The argument's real flaw is sample size, not an assumption about cross-media popularity equivalence.

**E. treats a requirement for a product to be popular as something that ensures that a product will be popular** — Necessary vs. Sufficient (3% of students)
This describes a "necessary treated as sufficient" flaw — but the argument isn't about what makes a product popular or what guarantees popularity. It's about whether selling movie rights is generally a good business move, supported by one anecdote. Wrong flaw category entirely.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Selling movie rights is rarely good business.
- **Evidence:** One company (StarQuanta) had a bad experience with one game.
- **Evaluate:** One case can't support a general claim about how the industry usually fares.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that names generalizing from a single instance.

**Answer choices**
**A. draws a general conclusion on the basis of just one individual case** — Correct (86% of students)
The argument generalizes from a single case (StarQuanta/Nostroma) to a broad industry rule.

**B. infers that a product will be disliked by the public merely from the claim that the product was disliked by critics** — Bad Description (3% of students)
The stimulus already says both critics and the public hated the film; no inference is being made.

**C. restates as a conclusion a claim earlier presented as evidence for that conclusion** — Circular Reasoning (2% of students)
The premise and conclusion are distinct; the flaw is generalization, not circularity.

**D. takes for granted that products with similar content that are in different media will be of roughly equal popularity** — Bad Assumption (6% of students)
The argument doesn't rely on equal cross-media popularity; the flaw is small sample.

**E. treats a requirement for a product to be popular as something that ensures that a product will be popular** — Necessary vs. Sufficient (3% of students)
The argument isn't about conditions for popularity; the flaw is small sample.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The author argues that video game makers selling movie rights is rarely a good idea.
- **Evidence:** The entire case is built on one company — StarQuanta — and its experience with one game, Nostroma. The film bombed, the sequels sold poorly.
- **Evaluate:** That's a single data point. To argue that something is "rarely good," you'd want to look at a bunch of cases and see how they generally turn out — not just point to one bad outcome.

It's like saying "Restaurant chains rarely succeed when they expand internationally" and then citing only one chain's failed expansion. Maybe that one chain blew it. Or maybe the next ten chains all succeeded. We can't tell from one case.
- **Goal:** The right answer will say the argument is built on too small a sample — one case generalizing to a rule.

**Answer choices**
**A. draws a general conclusion on the basis of just one individual case** — Correct (86% of students)
This is exactly the problem. The conclusion claims something about how movie rights deals usually go for video game makers — a sweeping claim. The evidence is one company's experience with one game.

You can't establish "rarely good" with one example. The same company might have had a different result with another game; another company might have had a great result. A single anecdote isn't enough to support a general rule about an industry. (A) names this directly.

**B. infers that a product will be disliked by the public merely from the claim that the product was disliked by critics** — Bad Description (3% of students)
Look back at the stimulus — it actually says the film was hated by critics *and* the public. So the author isn't inferring public hatred from critic hatred; both are stated as facts. (B) describes a move that isn't happening.

**C. restates as a conclusion a claim earlier presented as evidence for that conclusion** — Circular Reasoning (2% of students)
This says the conclusion was already assumed in a premise. But the premise (StarQuanta's outcome) and the conclusion (a general rule about all makers) are different scopes. The argument leaps between them — that's the actual flaw — but it doesn't repeat itself.

**D. takes for granted that products with similar content that are in different media will be of roughly equal popularity** — Bad Assumption (6% of students)
This says the author is assuming a film and the game it's based on should have similar popularity. But the argument doesn't actually need that — it just observes that both went poorly. The real flaw is generalizing from one case, not making a claim about cross-media popularity.

**E. treats a requirement for a product to be popular as something that ensures that a product will be popular** — Necessary vs. Sufficient (3% of students)
This describes the "treats a requirement as a guarantee" flaw. But the argument doesn't set up necessary or sufficient conditions for product popularity. It just generalizes from one case. (E) is naming a different kind of flaw entirely.

## S2 · Q5 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Science writer: Lemaitre argued that the universe began with the explosion of a "primeval in atom," a singular point of infinite gravity in space and time. If this is correct, our current observations should reveal galaxies accelerating away from one another. This is precisely what we observe. Yet because there is another theory—the oscillating universe theory—that makes exactly this same prediction, Lemaitre's theory must be considered inadequate.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the science writer's reasoning?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Lemaitre's theory (that the universe began with the explosion of a singular point of infinite gravity in space and time) must be considered inadequate.
- **Evidence (because):** Even though his theory predicts galaxies accelerating away from one another, which matches our observations, there is another theory (oscillating universe) that makes exactly this same prediction.
- **Evaluate:** When your theory predicts X, and we observe X, that counts as evidence for your theory (definitely not proof of your theory).

If someone else's theory also predicts X, and we observe X, then where does that leave your theory?

In the same exact spot! Both your theory and this other person's theory both have some support. At this point, we wouldn't have any reason for believing more in yours or in theirs.

Yet our author here thinks that if someone else's theory also predicts X, then that shows your theory must be inadequate. This doesn't make any sense. Their theory making the same correct prediction as our theory shows us that the two theories are currently tied, in terms of how accurate they seem.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer complaining that "Just because another theory also makes the same prediction Lematire's theory makes doesn't mean that reflects poorly on Lemaitre's theory. His theory is currently just as likely to be correct as is the other theory."

**Answer choices**
**A. The conclusion is derived partly from assertions attributed to a purported expert whose credibility is not established.** — Out of Scope: appeal to an expert (2% of students)
One of the ten famous flaws is *Inappropriate Appeal* (to Emotion or a to dubious expert). This is not committing that flaw since none of the evidence provides an assertion attributed to a purported expert.

The only attributed assertion is Lemaitre's theory, but that's not part of the author's evidence; that's what the author is trying to refute.

**B. The conclusion is based on a shift in meaning of a key term from one part of the argument to another part.** — Not *Equivocation* (1% of students)
This answer refers to another one of the ten famous flaws, *Equivocation,* which is almost never the correct answer.

There was no term being used two different times in two different ways.

**C. The science writer takes for granted the existence of a causal connection between observed phenomena.** — Not Causal (5% of students)
This is presenting language that sounds like another of the ten famous flaws, Causal Overconfidence, in which an author presents a funky fact (like a correlation) and then overconfidently concludes one possible explanation for the fact, while failing to consider other possible explanations.

This argument has nothing to do with presenting a correlation and then concluding that one thing caused the other.

This argument is saying, "Since Theory X makes the same observed prediction as Theory Y, Theory Y must be inadequate".

**D. The science writer fails to see that one theory's correctly predicting observed data cannot itself constitute evidence against an alternative theory that also does this.** — Correct (85% of students)
This is saying the author fails to understand that just because the oscillating universe theory (O.U.T.) correctly predicts that galaxies accelerate away from each other, that doesn't do anything to make Lemaitre's theory look bad. His theory also makes that prediction.

**E. The science writer presumes, without providing justification, that there are only two possible explanations for the phenomena in question.** — Too Strong: only (6% of students)
The argument only presented two explanations for why galaxies are accelerating away from one another, but that doesn't mean the author is assuming that these are the only two possible explanations.

There are a bajillion trap answers that take this form. They try to make people think that "the only thing mentioned having trait X is therefore the only thing that has trait X".

This is the same mistake "All Lives Matter" people make when they hear "Black Lives Matter" and think this claim is somehow saying that "Black lives are the only type of life that matters".

## S2 · Q6 — Principle (Justify)

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Critic: The criticism of the popular film comedy *Quirks* for not being realistic is misguided. It is certainly true that the characters are too stylized to be real people. That could be problematic, but in this case the resulting film is funny. And that is the important thing for a comedy.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning in the critic's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The criticism of the comedy Quirks, that it's not realistic, is misguided.
(i.e., it's dumb to criticize Quirks for not being realistic)
- **Evidence:** The film is funny. That's the important thing for a comedy.
- **Evaluate:** The argument is essentially saying, "Sure, it's not realistic, but we're talking about a comedy! We don't care about realism as long as it's funny, and this one was funny."
- **Goal:** So we're probably looking for something like,
"If a comedy manages to funny, we don't care if it's realistic",

or something like
"As long as it did the important thing for its genre, we shouldn't criticize it for being unrealistic."

**Answer choices**
**A. Films should be judged on how well they accurately capture the world.** — Opposite (2% of students)
This sounds more like the opposite of what we want. By this principle, you should criticize the film for being unrealistic.

**B. Films are successful as long as they are popular.** — Weak Conclusion Match / Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
This gives us a rule that says

popular → successful

We know that Quirks was popular, so by this rule we can say it was also successful.

We're not trying to conclude, "This film was successful". We're trying to conclude "it was dumb to criticize this for being unrealistic". However, the correct answer also is a rule that allows us to derive that Quirks was successful, so that can't be the reason we pick (D) over this answer.

It's more about the lack of a premise match here, compared with a solid premise match in (D). And it's somewhat about the meaning of "success".

Correct answers to Principle questions are all about connecting the Evidence language to the Conclusion language. The detail that Quirks is "popular" is not part of the Evidence. It's just a modifier used to introduce the noun Quirks in the first sentence.

In defending the movie, does out author care that it was popular?

No. That's just a tangential detail. In defending the movie, our author cares that it was funny, which is the important thing for a comedy to achieve.

The sort of 'success' this answer is talking about sounds more like commercially successful, whereas the sort of 'success' that (D) is talking about sounds more like artistically successful.

When it comes to shutting up critics who are nitpicking at the film's lack of realism, it's more important to tell a critic that a film was artistically successful than it is to say it was commercially successful.

**C. Film comedies should find their humor in their stylistic portrayals.** — Bad Conclusion Match (19% of students)
The conclusion isn't about instructing comedies where they should find their comedy. The conclusion is about telling critics "they shouldn't criticize a funny comedy for being unrealistic."

We actually don't even know if Quirks finds its humor in its stylistic portrayals; we only know that the film has stylistic portrayals and is funny. The former isn't necessarily the main reason for the latter.

**D. Films are successful if they succeed within their genre.** — Correct (76% of students)
Well, I'm taken aback that we're using the idea of "the film was a success" as a match for our conclusion, "It's dumb to criticize the film for being unrealistic".

This rule applies to movies that succeed within their genre. Did Quirks succeed within its genre?

Yes. The "important thing for a comedy" is to be funny, and Quirks was funny. So this answer allows us to conclude that Quirks was a successful film.

Does that prove the conclusion? No. But it strengthens the conclusion. If the film was successful (we don't mean in terms of box office revenue, but in terms of its aesthetic purpose), then a film critic starts to seem misguided for being picky about realism.

**E. Films should try to stay entirely within a single genre.** — Bad Conclusion Match (1% of students)
The argument isn't offering advice to filmmakers about how they should make a movie. It's trying to shut down critics who are complaining that a funny comedy is unrealistic.

Assuming that Quirks stayed entirely within the single genre of comedy (which is not definitively established), this answer would just allow us to conclude, "When it comes to adhering to a genre, Quirks did what it was supposed to do."

It doesn't help us to argue that, "When it comes to realism, this film need not be criticized."

## S2 · Q7 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Party X has recently been accused by its opposition, Party Y, of accepting international campaign contributions, which is illegal. Such accusations are, however, ill founded. Three years ago, Party Y itself was involved in a scandal in which it was discovered that its national committee seriously violated campaign laws.

### Prompt
Which one of the following contains flawed reasoning most similar to the flawed reasoning in the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The accusations that Party X illegally accepted international campaign contributes is ill founded.
- **Evidence:** Party X is being accused by Party Y, who three years ago were involved in a scandal in which they violated campaign laws.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we do Parallel Flaw, we know to listen extra carefully for the Top 5 famous flaws (not the argument always commits one of those, but a high percentage of the time it does): *Necessary vs. Sufficient, Part vs. Whole, Unproven vs. Proven False, Ad Hominem,* and *Correlation vs. Causation.*

This seems to be an example of Ad Hominem, in which the author disqualifies the legitimacy of a claim because of its source, rather than its content. Specifically, the author argues "Don't listen to A. After all, A has a biased interest in the matter / After all, A has conflicting past behavior".

This is an example of the latter. "Don't listen to Y when it says that X violated a campaign law. After all, three years ago, Y violated a campaign law."

According to this logic, an arsonist can't tell you who started the fire. Someone who has cheated on their taxes can't tell you whether someone else is committing tax fraud. Someone who has made a grammatical error in the past can't correct your grammar.
- **Goal:** Look for an argument in which the author disqualifies a point of view because their past behavior includes some offense similar to the one they're alleging against someone.

**Answer choices**
**A. The plaintiff accuses the defendant of violating campaign laws, but the accusations are ill founded. While the defendant's actions may violate certain laws, they are not immoral, because the laws in question are unjust.** — Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
At first, the similarity in topic and language (campaign laws / ill founded accusations) makes this seem like a trap answer. But all five answers have that trait, so that can't be our sole reason for disliking it.

This argument is dismissing a point of view (the plaintiff's accusations are ill founded). Does the evidence say, "After all, this plaintiff did something similar in the past"?

Nope. It says that the laws in question are unjust.

**B. The plaintiff accuses the defendant of violating campaign laws, but these accusations show the plaintiff to be hypocritical, because the plaintiff has engaged in similar conduct.** — Bad Conclusion Match (27% of students)
This correctly matches the Evidence: the person making the accusations was guilty of similar accusations in the past.

But it doesn't match the conclusion. The conclusion of the original argument wasn't that, "In accusing Party X, Party Y has shown itself to be hypocritical." That would be a more valid conclusion.

Instead, it was "Party Y's accusations are ill-founded". Here we would need something like "The plaintiff's accusations are ill-founded".

Citing someone's past behavior to support the idea that they're being hypocritical isn't flawed. That's how you prove hypocrisy: your actions don't match your rhetoric.

Citing someone's past behavior to support the idea that their claim is wrong or should be ignored is what's flawed.

Someone who is decrying instances of sexual abuse should be taken seriously, even if they themselves have been accused of sexual abuse in the past.

**C. The plaintiff accuses the defendant of violating campaign laws, and, in the past, courts have declared such violations illegal. Nevertheless, because the plaintiff recently engaged in actions that were similar to those of the defendant, the plaintiff's accusations are ill founded.** — Correct (70% of students)
This matches the original argument's flaw -- it tries to prove that certain accusations are ill founded by citing conflicting past behavior on the part of those making the accusations ("because the plaintiff recently engaged in similar actions, the plaintiff's accusations are ill founded").

**D. The plaintiff accuses the defendant of violating campaign laws, but these accusations are ill founded. They are clearly an attempt to stir up controversy, because they were made just two weeks before the election.** — Weak Evidence Match (2% of students)
This is the other variation on Ad Hominem, in which you try to invalidate someone's claim or argument by citing their biased interest. This argument is trying to invalidate the plaintiff's accusations based on the idea that the plaintiff stands to gain from making these allegations just before the election.

We wanted an argument focused on conflicting past behavior not based on ulterior motives.

**E. The plaintiff accuses the defendant of voting only for campaign laws that would favor the defendant's party. This accusation is ill founded, however, because it attacks the defendant's motivations instead of addressing the arguments the defendant has put forth justifying these votes.** — Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
This argument is also trying to invalidate some accusations. Is it doing so by saying, "The accuser has some past behavior that is goes against what they're currently saying?"

No. It's saying the accuser is attacking motives, just like (D), rather than engaging with the ideas themselves.

## S2 · Q8 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Biologist: Marine animals known as box jellyfish have eyes with well-formed lenses capable of producing sharp images that reveal fine detail. But the box jellyfish's retinas are too far forward to receive a clear image, so these jellyfish can receive only a blurry image that reveals prominent features of objects but not fine detail. This example shows that eyes are adapted only to an animal's needs rather than to some abstract sense of how a good eye would be designed.

### Prompt
The argument requires assuming which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (this shows that):** Eyes are adapted only to an animal's needs rather than to some abstract sense of how a good eye would be designed.
- **Evidence:** Despite the fact that box jellyfish have eyes with well-formed lenses capable of fine detail, their retinas are too far forward to receive a clear image, so the jellyfish get only a blurry image that reveals prominent features but no fine detail.
- **Evaluate:** This argument is presenting the somewhat curious fact that these jellyfish have lenses that could handle fine detail, but the positioning of their retinas only gives them a blurry image without fine detail.

The author might have been asking herself, "Why don't these jellyfish have the fine detail vision that their lenses make possible?"

And her answer is, "I guess eyes are adapted only to what an animal needs, rather than to some abstract sense of how a good eye would be designed".

There are multiple New Concepts in the Conclusion, so we should take a sec and think about how they're intended to match up with the conversation.

"adapted only to its needs" is supposed to match up with the jellyfish's actual vision. So the author must be assuming that "the jellyfish only needs a blurry image that reveals prominent features. It doesn't need fine detail."

"an abstract sense of how a good eye would be designed" is supposed to match up with the best case scenario for what this jellyfish's vision could be. So the author must be assuming that "a good eye would be designed in a way that produces sharp images with fine detail".
- **Goal:** We have some clear Missing Links being assumed, based on these New Concepts in the conclusion.

Let's look for an answer defining what the author thinks "adapted only to an animal's needs" means, or what "how a good eye would be designed" means.

**Answer choices**
**A. Box jellyfish are the only kind of jellyfish with retinas that do not focus clearly.** — Too Strong: only / Only Thing Mentioned ≠ Only Thing (1% of students)
The author hasn't committed to this extreme claim that the only jellyfish with retinas that don't focus clearly are box jellyfish.

The argument only *mentions* box jellyfish, but we're never accusing the author of assuming that the only X mentioned is therefore the only X.

Otherwise, we'd accuse people who say "Black lives matter" of thinking that "Black lives are the only kind of lives that matter".

**B. Box jellyfish have a need to detect prominent features of objects but not fine details.** — Correct (91% of students)
This is one of the two missing links we predicted, based on these New Concepts in the conclusion.

The author is concluding that the box jellyfish's vision is adapted only to its needs not to an ideal standard, since its vision can detect prominent features of objects in blurry focus but not in fine detail.

So the author must be assuming that the box jellyfish's needs stop at "being able to see prominent features" and that's why it never evolved all the way to fine detail.

If we negated this and said, "Box jellyfish have a need to detect prominent features and fine details" then that would utterly destroy the argument. There's no way we could conclude that its vision is adapted to its needs, if it needs fine details but its vision doesn't provide fine details.

**C. Box jellyfish would benefit from having retinas that allowed their eyes to focus more sharply.** — Out of Scope: benefit (3% of students)
Nothing in this argument is about whether or not a given thing would benefit an animal.

If we negate this answer and say, "They wouldn't benefit from retinas that allow their eyes to focus more sharply", that wouldn't weaken the argument.

In the author's mind, the fact that the box jellyfish's vision doesn't provide sharp focus shows that the box jellyfish doesn't need sharp focus.

So if our negation were saying, "Hey, author -- the box jellyfish wouldn't even benefit from sharp focus", that wouldn't go against anything she's arguing.

Whether we "need" something is a totally different inquiry than whether something would benefit us.

**D. Box jellyfish developed from jellyfish whose retinas received clear images.** — Opposite, if anything (1% of students)
The notion of what the box jellyfish evolved from is really out of scope. The author is just claiming that creatures evolve according to their needs, not according to ideal design.

This answer is saying, "The author must assume that box jellyfish evolved from a species with more ideal design".

That would actually seem more like a weakening idea than anything. The author thinks that the evolution of the box jellyfish's visual system stopped at "being able to make out prominent objects" and never evolved all the way to sharp focus / fine detail / clear images, because the box jellyfish didn't need vision that good.

So if anything the author would probably think that the evolutionary predecessor of the box jellyfish had similar or worse vision.

**E. Box jellyfish use vision as their main means of detecting prey.** — Out of Scope: detecting prey / Too Strong: main (3% of students)
We have no idea how the box jellyfish detects prey; we never talked about such a thing. There's no reason to think the author has committed to any idea about what is the box jellyfish's #1 means of detecting prey.

## S2 · Q9 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Columnist: Research shows significant reductions in the number of people smoking, and especially in the number of first-time smokers in those countries that have imposed stringent restrictions on tobacco advertising. This provides substantial grounds for disputing tobacco companies' claims that advertising has no significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most undermines the columnist's reasoning?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (this provides grounds for):** Tobacco companies are wrong to claim that advertising has no significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke.

*rephrase:* Advertising **does** have a significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke.
- **Evidence:** Research shows significant reductions in the number of people smoking, especially the number of first-time smokers, in those countries that have imposed stringent restrictions on tobacco advertising.
- **Evaluate:** This argument is presenting a *Correlation* in its evidence:
"in countries where X is true, Y is true"

And the argument is assuming *Causation* in its conclusion:
"X has a significant causal impact on Y"

Hence, we could use our *Explain Curious Fact* mindset.

*Curious Fact ...* How come countries that impose stringent restrictions on tobacco advertising see significant reductions in the number of people smoking?

*Author's Explanation ...* the restrictions on advertising cause the reduction in smoking, because advertising has a significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke.

These types of Explain Curious Fact arguments always have the same two pressure points:
- Alternate Explanations for the curious fact
- the plausibility of the Author's Explanation
- **Goal:** On Weaken questions, it's more common for them to weaken a causal conclusion by suggesting an Alternate Explanation for the curious fact.

So let's look for an answer that gives us a different way to explain why the countries that imposed restrictions on tobacco advertising saw significant reductions in smoking (maybe there's some 3rd factor that explains both the laws against tobacco advertising and the reduction in smoking).

But we'll keep our mind open to any answer that undermines the plausibility of the idea that "ads for cigarettes have a significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke".

**Answer choices**
**A. People who smoke are unlikely to quit merely because they are no longer exposed to tobacco advertising.** — No Impact / Relative vs. Absolute (19% of students)
When we first read this, it somewhat sounds like it's undermining the idea that "tobacco ads have significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke".

After all, it's saying that people are unlikely to quit merely because they no longer see tobacco ads. But we're not concerned about whether they are likely / unlikely (absolute) to quit smoking. We're concerned with whether they are **more** likely to quit smoking than they were when the ads were still allowed.

In other words, if removing ads led to 20% of smokers quitting, that would be a significant reduction in the tendency to smoke, even though 80% of smokers continued smoking, so as a group they were unlikely to quit once the ads stopped.

More importantly, this answer only deals with existing smokers who may / may not quit. The other way that removing ads is thought to reduce smoking is by reducing the number of first-time smokers. This answer doesn't address that group at all.

If removing tobacco ads causes some smokers to quit (even if a minority of them) but also causes many people to never take up smoking in the first place, then the author would still be right to say that removing these ads had a significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke.

**B. Broadcast media tend to have stricter restrictions on tobacco advertising than do print media.** — No Impact: broadcast vs. print (1% of students)
This argument isn't ever differentiating between broadcast media and print media.

It's talking about nations that impose stringent restrictions on [all kinds] of tobacco advertising.

Whether broadcast is more / less / equally stringent compared to print media doesn't affect the argument at all.

**C. Restrictions on tobacco advertising are imposed only in countries where a negative attitude toward tobacco use is already widespread and increasing.** — Correct (75% of students)
Like most correct answers on Weaken when we're doing an Explain Curious Fact argument, this weakens by suggesting an Alternative Explanation for the curious fact.

The author looked at the fact that nations that impose stringent restrictions on tobacco ads have significant reductions in the number of people smoking and assumed that restricting the ads caused the reduction in smoking.

But whenever we have a correlation between X and Y, and then the author assumes that X causes Y, we immediately consider two alternate possibilities:
- Reverse Cause (maybe Y causes X?)
- 3rd Factor (maybe Z causes X and Y?)

This is a 3rd Factor type of answer.

"Increasingly widespread negative attitudes toward tobacco use" are leading to there being restrictions on tobacco ads and are leading to there being reductions in smoking.

By analogy, suppose we said, "Research shows significant reductions in the number of people who have sex before marriage in those families who go to church every week. Thus, going to church every week as a significant causal impact on the tendency towards pre-marital sex."

The 3rd factor alternate explanation would be, "No, no --- being very religious is the causal factor that explains why they're going to church every week and why they're 'saving themselves' for marriage."

**D. Most people who begin smoking during adolescence continue to smoke throughout their lives.** — No Impact (2% of students)
This answer has nothing to do with hurting the plausibility that "tobacco ads have a significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke".

And it's not offering an alternate explanation for why countries with stringent restrictions on tobacco ads have significant reductions in the number of people smoking.

Thus, it's useless to us. It's just a random fact about the sticking power of smoking, if you start in your adolescence.

**E. People who are largely unaffected by tobacco advertising tend to be unaffected by other kinds of advertising as well.** — No Impact (3% of students)
Do we even care about "other kinds of advertising"? Probably now. However, we could hurt the plausibility that "tobacco ads have a significant causal impact on the tendency to smoke" by pointing to other forms of advertising and saying that they have little causal impact on people's tendency to do what's being advertised.

For example, an answer like "advertisements for casino gambling / alcohol have little causal impact on the tendency to gamble or drink alcohol" would weaken somewhat.

But this isn't saying that other types of advertisements tend to have little causal impact. It's saying, for the group of people that are not impacted by tobacco advertising, they are also not impacted by other types of advertising.

It might be that 95% of people are impacted by tobacco advertising and other types. This answer is only talking about those who aren't, but it isn't giving us any sense of whether that's normal or abnormal when it comes to advertising's impact on behavior.

- some pivot/rebuttal idea

i.e., accepting the gift is not problematic

**Argument breakdown**

- **Evidence:** In a recent survey of the last 20 years, the crime rate has held steady, while the % of the population in prison has gone up a ton and spending on prisons has gone up a ton.
- **Evaluate:** When we're dealing with anti-causal conclusions like this one, we're trying to figure out how we can accept the evidence but argue that there IS a causal influence.

*Given That*
spending on prison went up, % of people in prison went up, but crime rate stayed same,
*How Can We Argue That*
putting more people in prison **can** help to reduce crime?

I don't have any great ideas with this one. i.e., maybe there were economic factors over the past 20 years that should have made the crime rate increase, so the fact that it didn't shows that putting people in jail may have reduced crime.

*infers that because X, Y*
then X should match the Premise and Y should match the Conclusion.

Did our evidence say that *the national crime rate has increased*?

Nope. We can stop reading there. The conclusion half of this answer is *also* wrong, but we don't need to ever even reach that half.

**B. ignores the possibility that the crime rate would have significantly increased if it had not been for the greater rate of imprisonment** — Correct (57% of students)

When Obama became President in the midst of the Great Recession, unemployment had been growing for months. The government issue a big ol' stimulus plan, and the unemployment continued to grow for months.

Some people would say, "Look the stimulus did nothing", and others would retort, "What do you mean, do you know how bad things would have been if we *hadn't* injected $800 billion into the economy".

When we see an answer choice saying
*ignores the possibility that X*
we can ask ourselves, "If X were true, would it hurt the argument?"

In this case, heck yeah! If we say to the author, "If it hadn't been for putting more people in prison, the crime rate would have gone way up", then we've basically shown that "putting more people in prison did help to reduce crime".

If the population increases to 800,000 people, then an identical crime crate would involve 8,000 crimes.

When the author says "can't help to reduce crime", it's implied by context that she means the aforementioned crime rate, not any absolute number of crimes.

How would that help us argue that putting people in prisons has reduced crime?

In order to successfully prove that "this key does not fit this lock" you don't have to first prove that you know a different key that does fit the lock. Maybe there are no available keys that fit the lock. Your conclusion about "this key" can be correct either way.

Similarly, our author is just trying to establish that "putting people in jail" doesn't work to reduce crime. He's not obligated to prove that there are other methods that do work.

**E. takes for granted that the number of prisoners must be proportional to the number of crimes committed** — Too Strong: must be proportional (13% of students)
Any time we see
*takes for granted that X*
we can ask ourselves, "Did this author need to assume that X?"

Our author wasn't ever assuming the incredibly extreme idea that number of prisoners must be proportional to number of crimes committed.

*Curious Fact ...* Why are sales down (especially of new products)?
*Author's Explanation ...* The new ad campaign sucked.

We can describe the flaw for any Curious Fact / Explanation type problem a few ways:
1. the author is overconfidently picking one possible explanation as though it's definitely the explanation.
2. the author is failing to consider other possible explanations for the curious fact.
3. the author is failing to consider that her explanation is implausible for some reason.

*given that ...* sales are down, particularly for new products
*how could we still argue that ...* the ad campaign was well conceived?

**Answer choices**
**A. it takes for granted that LRG's sales would not have been lower still in the absence of the competitor's advertising campaign** — Too Strong (22% of students)
Was the author assuming, "sales would **not** have been even lower without the campaign"?

Maybe? This is very tempting. If we negated that assumption, we'd get "sales *would* have been even lower without the campaign". Does that hurt the argument?

Almost. The problem is the author could say, "Of course sales would have been lower with NO ad campaign. I'm not saying the ad campaign did *nothing*. I'm saying it didn't do *as much good* as my ad campaign would have."

We could fix this answer by saying, "it takes for granted that LRG's sales would have been higher had the ad campaign taken the consultant's advice".

The author is assuming a mental comparison in which *his* version of the ad campaign outperforms the actual version that ended up happening.

But we can't say the author is assuming that the actual ad campaign was functionally worthless, that it essentially had no impact and the company's sales would have been just as low with no campaign at all.

If the author's conclusion had been, "Thus, the ad campaign did nothing", then this answer would be correct. But because it's just, "the ad campaign was ill conceived" then it means that the author is only saying "the ad campaign could have done better than it did, were it better designed".

### Prompt

*Conclusion:* If most Q is selected and it's not X, then it must be Y.

*If Q isn't the lowest bidder, then C is. So if the contract goes to the lowest bidder and doesn't go to Q, then it will go to C.*

## S2 · Q18 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Critic: An art historian argues that because fifteenth-century European paintings were generally more planimetric (that is, two-dimensional with no attempt at suggesting depth) than were sixteenth-century paintings, fifteenth-century painters had a greater mastery of painting than did sixteenth-century painters. However, this conclusion is wrong. Fifteenth-century European painters did not have a greater mastery of painting, for the degree to which a painting is planimetric is irrelevant to the painter's mastery.

### Prompt
The argument is flawed in that it

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** (This art historian's conclusion is wrong)
15th century painters did not have greater mastery of painting than 16th century painters.
- **Evidence:** The historian believes this because 15th century were more planimetric, but "how planimetric a painting is" has nothing to do with whether you have greater mastery.
- **Evaluate:** This is one of the top 5 famous flaws, but it's always so subtle when it happens. You have to distinguish between two types of rebuttal conclusions:
- their conclusion is **unwarranted**
- their conclusion is **false**

This author is allowed to conclude the first, but not allowed to conclude the second.

John thinks that Megan likes him because she kissed him on the cheek when he arrived at her apartment. But Megan kisses all her friends and acquaintances on the cheek (because she spent a semester in Europe and came home insufferable).

The valid conclusion here is, "So John's assumption that Megan likes him is unwarranted on the basis of her cheek kissing."

A going-too-far flawed conclusion here is, "So John's assumption that Megan likes him is wrong." (i.e. Megan doesn't like John)

We can't conclude she doesn't like him. Maybe she does. Where's our evidence she doesn't?

We call this famous flaw Unproven vs. Proven False (you may also have heard it called Absence of Evidence or Failure to Prove).

This author showed that the art historian's premise was irrelevant. But that doesn't mean the art historian's conclusion is wrong.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer pointing out the Unproven vs. Proven False move, or raising the objection that there might be some other reason to consider 15th century painters' having more mastery.

**Answer choices**
**A. rejects a position merely because the proponent of the position has other objectionable views** — Wrong Flaw (8% of students)
This describes the famous flaw Ad Hominem, in which someone dismisses another's point of view because that person has some vested interest in their claims' being true, or because that person has some conflicting past behavior.

Our author dismissed a point of view because the support offered for the view was irrelevant.

**B. illicitly relies on two different meanings of the term "mastery"** — Wrong Flaw (10% of students)
This describes the famous flaw Equivocation, in which someone uses the same word/concept two very different ways within their argument. "Mastery" in this argument was used consistently.

**C. takes a necessary condition for an argument's being inadequate to be a sufficient condition for an argument's being inadequate** — Wrong Flaw (18% of students)
This describes the famous flaw Necessary vs. Sufficient, in which a conditional logic premise is presented, and then the author applies it illegally in some backwards or opposite fashion.

There was no conditional logic in this argument, so it can't be this flaw.

**D. bases its conclusion on two claims that contradict each other** — Wrong Flaw (5% of students)
This describes the famous flaw Internal Contradiction, in which something the author says earlier in the paragraph contradicts something he says later.

There was no contradiction here. And there is only one premise (your planimetric skillz are irrelevant to assessing your mastery), so it's not even worth reading past

"bases its conclusion on two claims ..."

**E. rejects a position on the grounds that an inadequate argument has been made for it** — Correct (59% of students)
Does the conclusion reject a position?
Yes, "this conclusion [made by the art historian] is wrong."

Does the evidence establish that an inadequate argument has been made for the position?

Yes, the argument made for the position was that 15th century paintings are more planimetric. The author said that "degree of planimetric" is totally irrelevant to assessing the position (of greater mastery).

If the only premise offered is irrelevant to the topic of the conclusion, then we can call that an inadequate argument.

Inadequate argument = conclusion unwarranted
Rejects a position = conclusion is false

We're trying to assess whether this object was the head of a speaking staff or something else. This answer choice is saying, "IF it's a speaking staff, then that design was meant to symbolize a warrior's mace".

Cool. We don't really care. Are you telling me it is a speaking staff? Because that would strengthen.

This answer is trying to bait people into thinking, "Ohhhhh, so it is a warrior's mace! Those archaeologists were right!"

But this doesn't say it is a mace. It's saying that something is a speaking staff, but it happens to symbolize a warrior's mace.

### Stimulus

As a result, the soil structure in a typical farm field in the region is poor. So to significantly improve the soil structure, farmers will need to abandon the use of chemical fertilizers.

### Prompt
The argument relies on the assumption that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** To improve the soil structure, farmers will have to abandon chemical fertilizers.
- **Evidence:** As chemical fertilizers came to the region, farmers stopped growing "green-manure" crops to rejuvenate the soil. As a result, the soil structure is now poor.
- **Evaluate:** We're thinking, "How can we argue that they can keep chemical fertilizers but still improve soil structure?" Did they tell us that chemical fertilizers ruin the soil structure?

No, not at all. The thing that killed the soil structure was when farmers stopped growing "green manure". Did they tell us that chemical fertilizers cause farmers to stop growing "green manure"?

Kinda. That did happen in the past: the advent of chemical fertilizers *led* them to abandon "green manure".

The author must be assuming that "as long as we still have chemical fertilizers around, they aren't going to bring back green-manure crops."

If we look at this formally, the conclusion is saying
```
Don't abandon   ------------------->   poor soil
chem fertilizers                       structure
```

The evidence provided
```
not growing    ----->   poor soil
green-manure            structure
```

So what the flow of logic is missing is that first piece,
```
Don't abandon   --->   not growing
chem fertilizers       green-manure
```
- **Goal:** Look for an answer connecting "if we don't abandon chem fert, we won't bring back green-manure", or an answer ruling out some way that we could improve soil structure while still using chemical fertilizers.

Examples aren't special or important. They just help the reader understand what we're talking about. But for LSAC, examples give them a way to set up the trap of "the only thing mentioned = the only thing".

This author doesn't need to assume that *any* farmers start growing alfalfa. Any green-manure crop could rejuvenate the soil structure.

The author could happily concede that putting chemical fertilizer onto green-manure crops could have a positive effect on their growth (maybe it speeds up the photosynthetic rate of the green-manure crop by 2%).

That idea by itself isn't an objection. If we said "using chemical fertilizers and green-manure crops in tandem would not have a positive effect on soil structure", that would be a correct answer.

**C. the most important factor influencing the soil quality of a farm field is soil structure** — Too Strong: most important (7% of students)
This is Necessary Assumption at its friendliest, giving us overly loaded language the author never committed to. Would the author's argument be changed or harmed if soil structure was the 2nd most important thing?

No. The negation of really strong ideas rules out one extreme possibility, but it leaves room for tons of still-powerful possibilities.

**D. chemical fertilizers themselves have a destructive effect on the soil structure of farm fields** — Out of Scope: CF's harm soil (34% of students)
The author never committed herself to that idea, although it's easy to feel like she did since otherwise it's confusing why she's drawing such a harsh relationship between "can't improve soil w/o quitting chemical fertilizers".

If someone were trying to improve their lung health by quitting cigarette smoking, but they had a compulsive habit of smoking any time they did a Sudoku out on the porch, you might conclude "to improve her lung health, she's gonna have to stop doing Sudoku's out on the porch".

That doesn't mean you're assuming that "doing Sudoku on the porch itself has a destructive effect on the lungs".

Our author is thinking that "as long as farmers have chemical fertilizers, they won't grow green-manure", so chemical fertilizers are indirectly leading to the degraded soil structure, but they're not the direct/proximate cause.

**E. many, if not all, farmers in the region will not grow green-manure crops unless they abandon the use of chemical fertilizers** — Correct (43% of students)
When Necessary Assumption answers are conditional, we can diagram them and ask ourselves if the author seemed to be making that reasoning move. This one looks like this:

```
Don't abandon             Many, if not all, will not
chemical fertilizers  →   grow green-manure
```

Our prephrase from before looks similar:

```
Don't abandon      --->   not growing
chem fertilizers          green-manure
```

If we negated this answer, it would sound like
"It's possible that farmers don't abandon chemical fertilizers, and yet many, if not all, farmers do grow green-manure crops".

Would that be an objection?

Yes. That allows us to argue that we could improve the soil, while still using chemical fertilizers.

## S2 · Q22 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Because the native salmon in Lake Clearwater had nearly disappeared, sockeye salmon were introduced in 1940. After being introduced, this genetically uniform group of sockeyes split into two distinct populations that do not interbreed, one inhabiting deep areas of the lake and the other inhabiting shallow areas. Since the two populations now differ genetically, some researchers hypothesize that each has adapted genetically to its distinct habitat.

### Prompt
Which of the following, if true, most strongly supports the researchers' hypothesis?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Hypothesis:** The two populations of sockeye salmon differ genetically because they have each adapted genetically to their distinct habitat.
- **Evidence:** In 1940 a genetically uniform group of sockeyes was introduced into the lake. Now there's a group in the deep part of the lake and a group in shallow part, who don't interbreed and now differ genetically.
- **Evaluate:** As soon as we see the word *hypothesis* (a causal explanation for a phenomenon) in the question stem, we know we can use a Curious Fact / Explanation template.

*Curious Fact:* Why are the deep water sockeye now genetically different from the shallow sockeye?
*Author's Story:* They have each adapted genetically to their distinct habitat

We then evaluate this via two lenses:
1. is there some OTHER WAY to explain the curious fact?
2. how plausible is the AUTHOR'S WAY of explaining it?

Usually Strengthen questions deal with plausibility of the author's way. Is it plausible that each sockeye population has adapted genetically to its distinct habitat?

Can evolution actually work that quickly? Normally the genetic drift of a species takes thousands or millions of years, not the 80 years since 1940. The only time that evolution works super quickly is with a very isolated population and a very distinct selection pressure (like, all of a sudden, there's sulfur in the water and only the fish that mutate a gene that tolerates sulfur will survive and pass on their genes, so within a few generations, the sulfur-tolerant fish dominate and effectively the "genes" of that species have been changed).

To strengthens the AUTHOR'S STORY, we would want to know that the deep lake and the shallow lake have very different and very important selection pressures, in order for us to think that over the course of 80 years we would have had a mutation so strongly selected for that the populations of sockeye are now genetically different.

Is there some OTHER WAY to explain how these once genetically identical populations of sockeye are now different? Hmmm, how can you change genetic makeup, other than by mutation / natural selection? The only other way that genes get affected is through mating. When two animals have sex, their offspring is a genetic blend of each of the component animals. So maybe the deep lake sockeye and shallow lake sockeye now differ from each other because at least one of them is mating with some other animals, thereby changing the gene pool.
- **Goal:** We either need something that strengthens the idea that the two different parts of the lake have such different survival needs that it's plausible that evolutionary selection pressure could have changed the genetic makeup of the two populations over just 80 years,
or we need something that rules out an alternate explanation for why the two populations now have different genetic makeup.

**Answer choices**
**A. Neither of the two populations of sockeyes has interbred with the native salmon.** — Correct (48% of students)
This rules out some OTHER WAY that their genetics would have changed. If the deep lake sockeye mated with the native salmon, then the result would be fish that have 1/2 sockeye genes, 1/2 native genes.

If this were happening in the deep lake, but not the shallow lake, then 80 years later we'd see the deep lake sockeye have a different genetic makeup, since there would now be a lot of native salmon genes mixed into their [ahem] gene *pool.*

This answer doesn't totally rule out interbreeding as the explanation for the genetic change (maybe some of the sockeye interbred with an animal other than the native salmon), but it's incredibly unlikely if not impossible that it happened with a different animal. Usually two species are defined as distinct *because* they can't interbreed. So salmon should only be able to interbreed with other salmon, one would think.

If interbreeding didn't change the genetic makeup, then we're more likely to accept the author's explanation that adaptation changed the genetic makeup.

**B. When the native salmon in Lake Clearwater were numerous, they comprised two distinct populations that did not interbreed.** — No Impact (30% of students)
Citing a parallel between sockeye and native in terms of the fact that the deep lake crew doesn't associate with the shallow lake crew isn't doing anything for us in terms of ruling out an Alternate Explanation or boosting the plausibility of the Author's Explanation.

**C. Most types of salmon that inhabit lakes spend part of the time in shallow water and part in deeper water.** — No Impact (8% of students)
We don't really care what most types of salmon do. The fact that most salmon would hang out in both parts of the lake, whereas sockeye are segregating themselves into two camps, doesn't rule out an Alternate Explanation for the genetic change nor does it help the Author's Explanation.

**D. One of the populations of sockeyes is virtually identical genetically to the sockeyes originally introduced in 1940.** — Opposite (if anything) (11% of students)
This answer potentially weakens, since the author's conclusion is that "each population has adapted genetically to its distinct habitat". This answer makes it seem like one of the two populations has not adapted genetically to its habitat. It sounds like their DNA is basically the same as when they were dumped there in 1940.

**E. The total number of sockeye salmon in the lake is not as large as the number of native salmon had been many years ago.** — No Impact (2% of students)
The total number of fish in the lake doesn't help us assess the causal mystery behind why the two populations of sockeye now have different genetic makeups.

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A developing country can substantially increase its economic growth if its businesspeople are willing to invest in modern industries that have not yet been pursued there. But being the first to invest in an industry is very risky. Moreover, businesspeople have little incentive to take this risk since if the business succeeds, many other people will invest in the same industry, and the competition will cut into their profits.

### Prompt
The statements above, if true, most strongly support which one of the following claims?

```
Businesspeople willing        developing country
to invest in 'new'       →    can increase econ
modern industries              growth a lot
```

```

It's hard to predict any specific wording, but the correct answer will likely connect "boosting the economy of a developing country" to "carrying unpalatable risk to investors", since the Overlapping Idea connecting those two things is "investing in modern industries".

That's crazy strong. Maybe it wouldn't contribute to **substantial** increases in growth, but not contribute at all?

**B. In developing countries, there is greater competition within modern industries than within traditional industries.** — Out of Scope: greater competition (7% of students)
This introduces an unknown comparison between *modern* and *traditional* industries. We were told that if a new modern industry succeeds, there will potentially be competing investors in that modern industry.

But we were told nothing about competition within traditional industries, so we have no means to make any comparison.

**C. A developing country can increase its prospects for economic growth by providing added incentive for investment in modern industries that have not yet been pursued there.** — Correct (75% of students)
This Reconciles the Pivot, balancing the idea that developing countries would benefit economically from investment in 'new' modern industries, but investors will see that investment as unpalatably risky. For investors, the risk seems to outweigh the incentive.

The investment in a previously un-pursued modern industry is "very risky", while there is "little incentive" to do it.

So it's supportable to say that if a developing country can tip the scales a little bit more by adding more incentive for investment in these new modern industries, then more investors might be willing to take on the risk (and if they succeed, it would help the developing country's economic growth).

It feels weird to be bringing in this new idea of the developing country adding incentive, but it seems like a plausible takeaway given what we were told about the current imbalance between risk and reward.

**D. A developing country will not experience economic growth unless its businesspeople invest in modern industries.** — Too Strong: will not / unless (7% of students)
The first sentence is saying that a developing country can experience growth from investment in modern industries, but this answer choice is acting like it said that a developing country can only experience growth from investment in modern industries.

**E. Investments in a modern industry in a developing country carry little risk as long as the country has at least one other business in that industry.** — Too Strong: little risk / Out of Scope: established industries (6% of students)
This answer is essentially selling us an "illegal light switch" idea.

Because the passage told us that "within new industries that no one's yet pursued, investment is very risky", it's acting like we can infer that "within industries where at least one has pursued, investment carries little risk".

We don't know anything from this paragraph about industries that already have at least one established business. And it's possible that investments in them are also very risky, but the potential incentives are so high that investors are willing to take on those risks.

## S4 · Q1 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
In an experiment, ten people were asked to taste samples of coffee and rank them. Five of the people were given chocolate with the coffee, and this group subsequently reported that all the coffee samples tasted pretty much the same as one another. Five others tasted coffee only, and they were able to detect differences. Clearly then, chocolate interferes with one's ability to taste coffee.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most undermines the conclusion drawn above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (Clearly then):** Chocolate interferes with one's ability to taste coffee.
- **Evidence:** Five of the ten people in the experiment were given chocolate with their coffee, and this group reported that all the coffee samples tasted pretty much the same as one another.

The other five tasted coffee only, and they were able to detect differences.
- **Evaluate:** Since this author is looking at a Curious Comparison from a study and then arriving at a causal conclusion (X *interferes* with Y), we can use our Explain Curious Fact mindset.

*Curious Fact ...* how come the people who had chocolate didn't taste differences, whereas the people who didn't have chocolate did taste differences?

*Author's Explanation ...* eating the chocolate interfered with people's taste buds so that all the different coffee samples they tasted seemed pretty much the same to them.
- **Goal:** Since we're doing a Weaken question, our answer could come in two flavors:

1. suggest an Alternate Explanation for the curious fact (what could be a different reason the coffee groups didn't find the coffee samples to taste different? Maybe they already had messed up taste buds. Maybe they were all stuffed up or had long covid, so their sense of taste was impaired. Maybe they never drink coffee, so all the coffee just tasted like awful dark liquid to them.

2. undermine the plausibility of the author's explanation (try to make it seem unrealistic to think that chocolate interferes with ability to taste coffee)

**Answer choices**
**A. The ten people were randomly assigned to either the group that tasted only coffee or the group that was also given chocolate, although some people had asked to be in the group that received chocolate.** — Mildly Strengthens (4% of students)
If these people were randomly assigned, it makes the data a little bit more credible. Random assignment helps to rule out certain alternate explanations that result from self-selecting.

**B. Similar results were achieved when the experiment was repeated with a different, larger group of people.** — Strengthens (1% of students)
This corroborates the results of the study, which strengthens the evidence, which strengthens the argument.

**C. Chocolate is normally consumed as a solid, whereas coffee is normally consumed as a liquid.** — No Impact (2% of students)
How each substance is typically consumed has no bearing on solving the causal mystery of why the chocolate group thought all the coffees tasted similar while the no-chocolate group was able to detect differences.

**D. The five people who were originally given chocolate were asked a week later to taste coffee samples without chocolate, and they still detected no differences between the coffee samples.** — Correct (87% of students)
This attacks the plausibility of the author's interpretation of this experiment.

He thinks that the five people in the chocolate group were unable to taste differences between the different coffees because they ate chocolate right beforehand, which interfered with their ability to taste coffee.

If that were true, then we would think that if they came back a week later and didn't have chocolate, they would be able to tell the difference.

Since these people, even when they had no chocolate, could not differentiate the coffee samples, then clearly chocolate wasn't the causal difference-maker preventing them from tasting differences.

We still don't know what the real reason is for why they can't taste the difference, but it clearly wasn't chocolate's doing.

**E. Some subjects who tasted just coffee reported only subtle differences between the coffee samples, while others thought the differences were considerable.** — No Impact (6% of students)
This is trying to weaken by blurring the lines a little between the chocolate group (they all taste pretty much the same) and the non-chocolate group (they could detect differences).

Learning that there was variation within the group that noticed differences (some noticed small, some noticed big) doesn't do anything for this argument.

It doesn't provide an alternate explanation for why the chocolate group tasted no differences, and it doesn't attack the plausibility that chocolate interfered with that group's taste buds.

## S4 · Q2 — Principle (Strengthen)

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Residents of a coastal community are resisting the efforts of one family to build a large house on the family's land. Although the house would not violate any town codes, the land in question is depicted in a painting by a famous and beloved landscape painter who recently died. Residents argue that the house would alter the pristine landscape and hence damage the community's artistic and historic heritage.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning of the residents opposed to building the house?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (implied):** That family shouldn't build a large house on the iconic land depicted in that famous painting from the recently deceased local artist.
- **Intermediate Conclusion (hence):** Building a house there would damage the artistic and historic heritage of the community.
- **Evidence:** The house would alter the pristine landscape.
- **Evaluate:** On Strengthen + Principle, we primarily pre-phrase a linking idea that feels like "If Premise, Then Conclusion".

There are two moves in this argument we could prep a linking idea for:

1. If it alters the pristine landscape, then it damages the community's artistic and historic heritage.

2. If it damages the artistic and historic heritage, then (even though it's within the families legal rights to do it), they shouldn't do it.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that strengthens the idea that building this large house would be tainting our local artistic heritage and thus we shouldn't do it.

**Answer choices**
**A. Every possible effort should be made to preserve historic buildings that are well known and loved.** — Irrelevant: preserve buildings (7% of students)
There's no building here that residents are trying to preserve. They are trying to preserve an absence of building.

**B. Communities that seek to preserve undeveloped areas of landscape or historic neighborhoods should purchase those properties for the public trust.** — Bad Conclusion Match (2% of students)
This principle would help us to conclude that the concerned residents should rally together money and buy this land from this family.

That's not a match for either conclusion. We're trying to justify that "the family shouldn't build the house" or that "doing so would damage our local artistic / historic heritage".

**C. Artists who choose to represent actual landscapes in their paintings have the right to demand that the owners of the land represented do not significantly alter the landscape.** — Unrelated to Goal (3% of students)
This is a principle about what rights artists have. The artist in this argument is deceased. We're looking for a principle that would address what rights the local residents have to block construction of a house on private land.

**D. The right to build on one's own property is constrained by the artistic and historical interests of the community at large.** — Correct (78% of students)
This helps us build the case that family, even though they are well within their rights to build the house, should still be factoring in the artistic / historical interests of the community.

It's a weak strengthener, because it really just affirms a necessary assumption. But it's the strongest thing offered.

A minority of Strengthen + Principle questions have this feel of Weighing Tradeoffs. This argument is all about stopping the construction of the house, because the community's artistic and historic heritage would be damaged.

But the argument supplied some counterpoints -- this house would be on the family's land and would not violate any town codes.

So if we were objecting, "The family should go forward with the construction. After all, it's perfectly within their legal rights. Don't worry about those whiny residents", this principle would resist that line of thinking.

### Stimulus

### Prompt
The reasoning in Moore's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Sunscreen lotions do not effectively block skin-cancer-causing UV radiation.
- **Evidence:** Many studies show that people who have consistently used these lotions develop, on average, as many skin cancers as those who have rarely, if ever, used them.
- **Evaluate:** This is an *Anti-Causal* argument, because the conclusion is saying "X does **not** cause Y". The evidence is that people who use sunscreen tend to get as much skin cancer as do people who don't. The classic *Anti-Causal* objection is to say, "yes but those two groups aren't fair to compare".

Consider this *Anti-Causal* analogy:
Contact lenses, which are designed to improve people's vision, do not do so effectively. Studies show that people who wear contact lenses are able, on average, to see as far down the road as are people who don't wear contact lenses.

We would shout, "That's because the first group of people is wearing their contact lenses! Compare these two groups with no one wearing contact lenses and you'll see that the first group can barely focus 10 feet in front of them, while the group of people who doesn't wear contacts can see 200 feet in front of them. Since wearing contact lenses allows this first group to *also* see 200 feet in front of them, clearly these contact lenses *are* effectively improving their vision."

Similarly, maybe the people who wear sunscreen are people who really need it (they are super fair-skinned or they spend tons of time out in the open sun) whereas people who don't wear it are darker-skinned, indoor types.

If the first group weren't wearing sunscreen, then their skin cancer levels would be through the roof. The fact that wearing sunscreen brings their skin cancer numbers down to the same level as those of darker-skinned, indoor types makes it seem like the sunscreen is effectively blocking some cancer-causing radiation.
- **Goal:** We need to defend the idea that sunscreen DOES help block our cancerous UV radiation, so we need to "make an excuse" for the evidence. We need a way to explain why sunscreen users have similar skin cancer rates to non-sunscreen people, such that we can still argue that the sunscreen IS helping the sunscreen users to block out cancerous UV rays.

**Answer choices**
**A. takes for granted that there are no other possible health benefits of using sunscreen lotions other than blocking skin-cancer-causing ultraviolet radiation** — Not Assumed / Too Strong: no other (2% of students)
Since this starts with *takes for granted / presumes*, we ask ourselves whether this idea qualifies as a Necessary Assumption.

It doesn't. It's way too strong and irrelevant. The author doesn't need to assume that **there are no possible** benefits to using sunscreen; if we say that sunscreen at least has the health benefit of moisturizing one's face, that wouldn't hurt the argument at all.

The conclusion is strictly about whether or not sunscreen blocks cancer-causing UV radiation. Anything else it happens to do is out of scope.

**B. fails to distinguish between the relative number of cases of skin cancer and the severity of those cases in measuring effectiveness at skin cancer prevention** — Weak Objection (17% of students)
This answer is pretty tempting since we might have thought about objecting to the anti-causal conclusion by saying, "Sure, people who use sunscreen have a similar rate of **getting** skin cancer, but the **type** of skin cancer is less severe, so therefore the sunscreen is still doing something."

This isn't terrible, but the conclusion is saying that sunscreen doesn't effectively block skin-cancer-causing UV radiation. If someone wears sunscreen and gets skin cancer, even a mild case, then isn't it fair to say that their sunscreen did not effectively block skin-cancer-causing UV radiation?

If the verb were "does not effectively reduce UV radiation" then we could probably point to a mild case of skin cancer and say, "This is still a sunscreen success story. The skin cancer would have been even worse without the sunscreen."

But because of the definitive verb block, if someone wearing sunscreen gets any form of skin cancer, then it's fair to say that the sunscreen didn't effectively block the cancer-causing radiation.

**C. fails to consider the effectiveness of sunscreen lotions that are not specifically designed to block skin-cancer-causing ultraviolet radiation** — Irrelevant Objection (2% of students)
Since this starts with *fails to consider / ignores the possibility*, we can ask ourselves whether this idea qualifies as a weakening Objection.

It doesn't, because this conclusion is strictly about the effectiveness of sunscreens that are designed to effectively block cancer-causing UV radiation.

The effectiveness of sunscreens that aren't designed to block that radiation is totally irrelevant to this conversation.

**D. relies on evidence regarding the probability of people in different groups developing cancer that, in principle, would be impossible to challenge** — Bad Evidence Match (2% of students)
The evidence in this argument isn't about the probability of people in different groups developing cancer (that's a future-looking guess).

The evidence is data from scientific studies tabulating the rate at which people did get skin cancer.

**E. overlooks the possibility that people who consistently use sunscreen lotions spend more time in the sun, on average, than people who do not** — Correct (76% of students)
Since this starts with *fails to consider / ignores the possibility*, we can ask ourselves whether this idea qualifies as a weakening Objection.

It does, because if the people who use sunscreen spend more time in the sun than people who don't, it's not fair to compare these two groups. The former group is at a much higher risk for skin cancer (since they get more exposure to the sun).

If none of them wore any sunscreen, then the people exposed to more sun would have higher rates of skin cancer. The fact that wearing sunscreen brings their cancer rates down to the level of people who have less exposure to the sun shows that the sunscreen is working.

On Anti-Causal arguments, we're typically lodging one of two (often related) objections:
1. It would be even worse/better without X
2. These two groups/situations aren't fair to compare

Here, we're sort of doing both. These two groups aren't fair to compare because the sunscreen group has a much higher underlying risk of skin cancer due to their greater risk of exposure. Thus, their rate of skin cancer would be even higher if they weren't wearing sunscreen.

We can also think of this as offering an Alternate Explanation for the Curious Fact. The Curious Fact was, "How come sunscreen users have the same cancer rate as non-sunscreen users?", and the author's Explanation was, "Because sunscreen doesn't actually help block cancer causing radiation", and here our Alternate Explanation is, "no, the reason they have the same rate is because sunscreen users start out at a much higher risk of skin cancer".

- **Evidence:** A financial planner would be guilty of gross malpractice if they said to a 30 year old client, "Don't worry about saving for retirement now ... you won't have to deal with that problem for 35 years".
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we do Main Conclusion or Role, we prime our brains before reading the paragraph with the reminder that the Conclusion (on these questions) will almost always be
- First Sentence, or
- Rebuttal

This first sentence sounds a bit like it could be the Main Conclusion. It's a claim about the future so it's inherently opinionated. If we read on and feel like the author is giving us supporting reasons why failing to make changes to our vendor contracts could imperil our supply chain, then we'd know the first sentence was the conclusion.

Instead, the second sentence gives us an attribution, "Some will argue". This feels like a setup for a Rebuttal, and then sure enough the following claim starts with a classic rebuttal Pivot word (but, yet, however). The final two sentences are supporting that rebuttal with an analogy.
- **Goal:** We want an answer that matches "That is an irresponsible approach". Since our conclusion has a pronoun, "That", we need to import the referential text:

"Kicking the can down the road / not worrying about changing vendor contracts now because we won't see problems in our supply chain until far into the future" is an irresponsible approach.

An equivalent way of saying "X is an irresponsible approach" could be to say "we shouldn't do X".

**Answer choices**
**A. Some people argue that the supply-chain problem is so far in the future that there is no need to address it now.** — Opposing Position (1% of students)
This is not the 3rd sentence. This is the 2nd.

This is an attribution to someone else's beliefs. The Main Conclusion will always be the author's belief. This claim is actually what the Main Conclusion is calling irresponsible.

**B. It would be irresponsible to postpone changes to the vendor contracts just because the supply chain will not develop weaknesses for a long time.** — Correct (79% of students)
This is a good match for the meaning of the 3rd sentence. This is the approach that the author is labeling 'irresponsible'.

It's actually the only answer saying something is 'irresponsible', so if we were clear on the structure but fuzzy on the meaning, this is the only answer that resembles the key word in the 3rd sentence.

**C. If no changes are made to the vendor contracts, the supply chain will eventually develop significant weaknesses.** — Background (17% of students)
This is not the 3rd sentence. This is the 1st.

This is a just a background fact.

**D. In planning to meet its future obligations, a company should follow the same practices that are appropriate for an individual who is planning for retirement.** — Assumption (3% of students)
This is not the 3rd sentence. This isn't any claim that was said, so we know it's wrong.

This might appeal to people because it's something this argument is assuming, but we're not being asked for what invisible idea lurks in the author's mind. We're being asked for which explicit idea the author spoke out loud as her conclusion.

**E. Financial planners should advise their clients to save money for retirement only if retirement is many years away.** — Unstated (0% of students)
This is not the 3rd sentence. This isn't any claim that was said, so we know it's wrong.

It's not even something implied or assumed. It's just an overly strong conditional that the author never said or suggested.

Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:** *Math-y Comparison*
Last year > any previous year
**# of books sold**

Last year > any previous year
**# of cookbooks sold**

Last year, 1st time ever, cookbooks sold
intended for beginners < 50%

**# of cookbooks bought by pros**

*Distinction (one of the few)*

*Problem-Free Cooking* is a cookbook, written for beginners, and, unlike almost all other books, it's available on every continent.
- **Evaluate:** Is there any way to combine any of these ideas?

Typically, if we're handed a few general statements/rules and then given a specific fact (like Problem-Free Cooking), we want to see if any of those rules/statements apply to that specific fact.

The other place we'd look for combining ideas is with the multiple Mathematical Comparisons. Is there something we can mathematically derive by combining those?

**Answer choices**
**A. Last year there were more cookbooks sold that were not intended for beginners than any previous year.** — Correct (60% of students)

The 2nd claim says
*number of cookbooks sold was highest ever*

The 3rd claim says
*the % of cookbooks sold that were not intended for beginners was highest ever*

So if last year's slate of cookbooks had the highest percentage ever of "advanced" cookbooks, and last year's slate of cookbooks was the biggest raw total of cookbooks ever, then that would have to output the greatest number of advanced cookbooks sold in one year.

How do we know last year's % for advanced (i.e. not intended for beginner) was highest ever? We know it's the all time high, because last year was the first time it ever passed the **50% most** threshold.

| % of cookbooks sold that were not for beginners | > 50% | < 50% |

- it was a bad year for beginners' cookbooks, because a lower percentage than ever of cookbooks sold were for beginners.

but

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
In early 2003, scientists detected methane in the atmosphere of Mars. Methane is a fragile compound that falls apart when hit by the ultraviolet radiation in sunlight. So any methane in the Martian atmosphere must have been released relatively recently.

### Prompt
The argument relies on the assumption that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Any methane in the Martian atmosphere must have been released relatively recently.
- **Evidence:** Methane falls apart when it's hit by UV radiation from sunlight.
- **Evaluate:** This argument makes sense, as long as UV radiation is constantly hitting the methane on Mars. Since UV breaks it apart into its constituent atoms, a methane compound's lifespan is only as long as it takes to get hit by UV radiation.

Missing Link:
*if Martian atmosphere → continually hit by UV*

The whole argument is thinking
**1.** when you're in Martian atmosphere, you're continually hit by UV.
**2.** methane dies whenever its hit by UV.
**infer:** methane in the Martian atmosphere is constantly dying.
**conc:** any methane you find is near the beginning of its very short life span (cuz all this methane's gonna get zapped by UV in no time).

If a linking answer doesn't occur to us, we should be looking for an Objection.

GIVEN THAT methane falls apart whenever it's hit by the Sun's UV,
HOW COULD WE ARGUE it's possible to find methane in Mars' atmosphere that was released a really long time ago?

We would just say, that old-ass Martian methane never got hit by the Sun's UV. Maybe this methane was released into some ridged crater that provided permanent shade from the sunlight.

Or maybe there's a "Dark Side of the Mars" that never faces the Sun.
- **Goal:** Since our main way to object is to say, "What if there's some methane that just hasn't been hit by UV yet?", we can say the author must assume "There is not methane that has been on Mars for a long time but has never been hit by UV."

**Answer choices**
**A. Mars had no methane in its atmosphere prior to 2003** — Too Strong: nothing prior to 2003 (13% of students)
The author isn't thinking that methane suddenly burst on the scene in 2003. He just thinks that as methane leaks out of Mars, it gets zapped by UV radiation shortly thereafter, which dissolves it into its constituent bits.

**B. all methane in the Martian atmosphere is eventually exposed to sunlight** — Correct (59% of students)
This actually does resemble our Missing Link:
*if Martian atmosphere → constantly hit by UV*

If we negated this, would it Weaken?

Sure! "Hey, author, **some** methane in the Martian atmosphere is **never** exposed to sunlight. So we might discover methane in the Martian atmosphere that's a million years old. If it was never exposed to sunlight, then we have no reason to think that something made it fall apart."

**C. methane cannot be detected until it has started to fall apart** — Too Strong: cannot be detected (12% of students)
The conclusion and evidence are not really about "detection" at all, so there's no reason for the author to assume anything about detection.

This answer also sort of offends common sense, like "I couldn't taste the soup until you spilled it on the floor". It should be easier to detect intact methane then crumbling methane, right?

**D. the methane that scientists had detected had been exposed to ultraviolet radiation** — Opposite (15% of students)
Had the methane the scientists detected been exposed to UV, it wouldn't have been methane; it would have just been loose hydrogen and carbon atoms (the building blocks of methane).

The author thinks that once methane is exposed to UV, it falls apart (its hydrogen and carbon atoms falls apart into disconnected atoms).

So the author is assuming that the methane the scientists detected had not been exposed to UV.

**E. methane in Earth's atmosphere does not fall apart as a result of exposure to ultraviolet radiation** — Opposite, if anything / Out of Scope: Earth's atmosphere (1% of students)
The author would believe that anywhere in the solar system that methane gets hit by the UV radiation in sunlight is a place where methane will fall apart.

If we negated this and objected, "Not in Earth's atmosphere, it doesn't!", we wouldn't be weakening this conclusion, which is exclusively about the Martian atmosphere.

Environmentalist: Pollution from gasoline burned by cars contributes to serious environmental problems. The environmentalist's statements, if true, most strongly support which one of the following?

Pollution from gas burned by cars contributes to big environmental problems.

*pivots (But, however)*

### Stimulus
Stress is a common cause of high blood pressure. By calming their minds and thereby reducing stress, some people can lower their blood pressure. And most people can calm their minds, in turn, by engaging in exercise.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Facts:** (1) Stress is a common cause of high blood pressure. (2) Calming the mind reduces stress, and for some people this lowers their blood pressure. (3) Most people can calm their minds by exercising.
- **Evaluate:** This is a chain: exercise → calmer mind → less stress → lower blood pressure for some. The logical move is "for at least some people." The strongest direct chain is exercise → calmer mind → reduced stress, since "most people can calm their minds by exercising" applies broadly enough that at least some of those people are covered. The blood-pressure end of the chain only works for "some people," which is a weaker bridge.
- **Goal:** Find an answer that is directly supported by the chain of facts — most likely a "for at least some" claim that links exercise to one of the closer steps in the chain (calming, stress reduction).

**Answer choices**
**A. For at least some people, having lower blood pressure has at least some tendency to cause their stress levels to be reduced.** — Reversal / Negation (2% of students)
The stimulus says reducing stress lowers blood pressure for some — the direction is stress → blood pressure. This answer reverses it, claiming lower blood pressure causes lower stress. Nothing in the stimulus supports that backwards causal direction.

**B. Most people with high blood pressure can lower their blood pressure by reducing their stress levels.** — Too Strong (9% of students)
The stimulus says "some people can lower their blood pressure" by reducing stress — not most. So we can't conclude that most people with high blood pressure can lower it through stress reduction. The leap from "some" to "most" is unsupported.

**C. Most people who do not exercise regularly have higher stress levels as a result.** — Too Strong (2% of students)

**D. Engaging in exercise can directly lower one's blood pressure.** — Too Strong (11% of students)
The stimulus describes an indirect chain: exercise → calmer mind → reduced stress → lower blood pressure (for some). This answer says exercise can directly lower blood pressure — skipping all the intermediate steps. The stimulus doesn't support a direct mechanism; it supports an indirect one routed through stress.

**E. For at least some people, engaging in exercise can cause their stress levels to be reduced.** — Correct (76% of students)
This is the conservative inference the chain supports. The stimulus says (a) calming the mind reduces stress and (b) most people can calm their minds by exercising. So at least some of those "most people" have their stress reduced by exercising. The "for at least some" hedge keeps the inference safely within what the stimulus guarantees.

- **Evaluate:** The chain supports: at least some people have their stress reduced by exercise.

**Answer choices**
**A. For at least some people, having lower blood pressure has at least some tendency to cause their stress levels to be reduced.** — Reversal / Negation (2% of students)
Reverses the direction: the stimulus has stress causing blood pressure issues, not the other way.

**B. Most people with high blood pressure can lower their blood pressure by reducing their stress levels.** — Too Strong (9% of students)

**D. Engaging in exercise can directly lower one's blood pressure.** — Too Strong (11% of students)

A positive correlation has been found between the amount of soot in the atmosphere of cities and the frequency of a certain ailment among those cities' populations. Which one of the following statements, if true, most weakens the argument?

- **Conclusion (probably):** The soot itself probably doesn't cause this ailment.

- **Evaluate:** This argument is an Anti-Causal spin on the normal Explain the Curious Fact template.

*Curious Fact ...* why is there a correlation between atmospheric soot and ailment X?

*Potential Explanation ...* the soot causes ailment X.

*Author's Anti-Causal Alternate Explanation ...* No, the soot probably isn't causing ailment X. The ailment is probably being caused by other air pollutants, because the cities that have lots of atmospheric soot also have these other air pollutants.
- **Goal:** If we're trying to weaken this argument, we'd like to either find an answre that helps us argue:

2. It's not these other pollutants that are causing the ailment
(or both)

**Answer choices**

**B. If the ailment rarely occurs except in cities in which there are large amounts of soot in the air, then the soot is probably the cause of the ailment.** — Untriggered Conditional (22% of students)
The outcome of this conditional would be the perfect counterpunch to this author's conclusion, but we only get that outcome if we establish that the trigger is true.

Do we know that the ailment rarely occurs except in cities in which there's lots of soot in the air?

No, we've never been told that. Thus, this hypothetical idea has no impact.

**C. In each of the cities where there are large amounts of soot in the air but little other air pollution, the frequency of the ailment is at least as high as it is anywhere else.** — Correct (60% of students)
This both strongly suggests that soot is the causal difference-maker and that the other pollutants are not.

There are cities where there is high soot, but not the "other pollutants" that the author is thinking cause ailment X, and yet the incidence of ailment X is just as high as anywhere else. So that suggests that soot is what's causing the incidence of ailment X.

If you believe that soot causes X, then we would say this data point strengthens by providing a "Yes Cause, Yes Effect" example.

If you believe (as the author does) that these other pollutants cause X, then we would say this data point weakens by providing a "Effect without Cause" data point, since the effect is present (high incidence of X) even though the cause is absent (the other pollutants aren't there).

**D. If high concentrations of many different pollutants in a city's air are correlated with a high frequency of the ailment among that city's population, then it is possible that two or more of those pollutants each causally contributes to the ailment.** — Strengthens, if anything (5% of students)
This is also a conditional, so it doesn't have any value unless we can establish the trigger.

If we do trigger the conditional, the outcome would actually help the author to argue that it's these other air pollutants that are causing the incidence of ailment X.

The argument mostly establishes the trigger.

Higher rates of ailment X are correlated with high levels of soot, and high levels of soot are correlated with high levels of these other pollutants.

If A is correlated with B, and B is correlated with C, that doesn't necessarily mean that A is correlated with C, but it suggests it probably is. Luckily we don't need to dig deep on whether the trigger is established because the outcome wouldn't be helpful in terms of weakening the argument.

**E. In cities in which there are high concentrations of many air pollutants, there are generally also high concentrations of other forms of pollution that are very likely to contribute causally to the ailment.** — Somewhat Strengthens & Weakens (4% of students)
This is an interesting answer, because it suggests that ailment X may actually be caused by some other form of pollution (not airborne).

That somewhat goes against the idea the author is suggesting, which is that "other air pollutants" are causing ailment X, but it strengthens the author's conclusion that "the soot itself is probably not causing ailment X".

If an answer is a mixed bag, it will almost always lose out to an answer that purely strengthens or weakens.

The fact that this answer is saying "other forms of pollution very likely contribute causally to the ailment" also means that it's not actively denying the author's idea that air pollutants cause X. It can just be adding to that idea, saying "Yes and these other forms of pollution also contribute to X".

## S4 · Q18 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
So far this summer there has been no rain in the valley. But usually a few inches of rain fall there each summer. Since only one week of summer is left, it will probably rain in the valley within the next week.

### Prompt
The flawed pattern of reasoning in the argument above is most similar to that in which one of the following arguments?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (probably):** It will probably rain in the valley within the next week.
- **Evidence (since):** There are usually a few inches of rain each summer.

Only one week of summer is left, and so far there's been no rain.
- **Evaluate:** There isn't any conditional logic here, but there are some repeating ideas:

"rain / summer / last week" are mentioned a couple times, so we could potentially assign them symbols.

The author is predicting rain in the coming week because we normally have rain each summer, we haven't so far, and there's only one week of summer left. This is a *Math-y* flaw where an author interprets the concept of average or probability too literally.

The conclusion has a "probably" strength, so we can start reading each answer by verifying it has the same type of conclusion.
- **Goal:** Let's look for something like
- We usually see X happen by marker Y.
- X hasn't happened yet and we're almost at marker Y.
- Thus, X will probably happen in the remaining time before we get to marker Y.

Or more conversationally, "since a thing we'd usually see by now hasn't happened, it will probably happen soon".

**Answer choices**
**A. Aisha has finished proofreading all but the last two pages of an issue of the journal *Periodos* and has encountered no errors. However, there are sometimes a few errors in an issue of the journal *Periodos*. So there may be errors in the pages that Aisha has not yet checked.** — Weak Conclusion/Premise Match (16% of students)
The original argument played off the idea that there is **usually** rain by time X, so there will **probably** be rain in the remaining time leading up to time X.

This argument is saying there are **sometimes** a few errors, so there **may** be errors we find before we're done.

*Probably/usually* are of a different strength than *sometimes/may* (the former means it's likely, the latter means it's possible).

It's possible this answer would turn out to be the best available, and we would have to forgive that difference, but on a first pass we wouldn't be receptive to it.

**B. There are generally few errors in an issue of the journal *Periodos*. Aisha has finished proofreading all but the last two pages of an issue of this journal but has encountered no errors. Hence, there are probably no errors in the pages that Aisha has not yet checked in this issue of the journal.** — Bad Premise Match (4% of students)
This is fairly tempting, but to match the original we would want to hear, "There usually are a few errors. We haven't found any so far, so there will probably be some in the remaining pages."

In the original argument, there is a mismatch between the usual (there's usually a few inches of rain each summer) and the observed (there hasn't been any rain so far).

In this argument, there isn't a mismatch. The usual (there are usually few errors) and the observed (there haven't been errors so far) are aligned.

**C. On average, there are a few errors in an issue of the journal *Periodos*. Aisha has finished proofreading all but the last two pages of an issue of this journal but has encountered no errors. So there are probably errors in the pages she has not yet checked in this issue of the journal.** — Correct (74% of students)
There is usually a few inches of rain each summer.
There are a few errors in every issue, on average.

One week left, and no rain so far this summer.
Two pages left, and no errors so far.

Thus, it will probably rain in the next week.
Thus, there will probably be errors in the last two pages.

**D. Aisha has proofread several issues of the journal *Periodos* and has encountered no errors. But there are seldom any errors in an issue of this journal. So there will probably be no errors in the next issue of the journal *Periodos* that she proofreads.** — Bad Premise Match (4% of students)
Just like (B), there's no mismatch here between the usual and the observed.

The original argument was saying, "Since something we usually see occur hasn't yet occurred, it will probably occur soon".

This argument is saying, "We usually don't see errors in this journal. And Aisha hasn't seen errors in this journal".

**E. There usually are errors in each issue of the journal *Periodos*. Since Aisha has finished proofreading the latest issue of this journal and has detected no errors, Aisha has probably made a mistake in her proofreading.** — Bad Conclusion Match (1% of students)
The conclusion has to be related to the "usual" thing we see. In the original argument, there usually is rain during the summer, and so the conclusion is predicting there will probably be rain (during the remaining week of summer).

Here, there are usually errors in each issue of the journal, so the conclusion should be predicting that there will be an error in the remaining part of the journal.

Instead, the conclusion is saying that Aisha probably messed up.

For the action to serve the goal, enabling that belief must actually prevent the loss of motivation. It just needs the recommended action to address the motivation problem. The argument doesn't need belief in better futures to eliminate pollution, poverty, and war. The conclusion is more modest: we must enable that belief in order to preserve motivation. **E. The current prevalence of such problems as pollution and poverty stems from previous generations' inability to believe that futures can be better.** — Out of Scope (3% of students)
The argument is forward-looking — about preventing the current generation's loss of motivation. It doesn't need any claim about what caused current problems. - **Conclusion:** Glutamate leaking from damaged or oxygen-starved nerve cells is a cause of long-term brain damage resulting from strokes.

*Curious Fact*
Why did the stroke patients with the long term damage to their brain's nerve cells have the highest levels of glutamate in their blood?

- how plausible is the Author's Explanation?

We didn't need to be convinced that glutamate will damage surrounding nerve cells (we had already been told that), so this is really only opening up the possibility of **alternate causes**, if there are other neurotransmitters that also could be the culprit in causing this brain damage.

A "wide variety of abnormal chemicals in their blood" is too unspecific to do anything. At worst, this is actually nudging in a weaken direction by suggesting that there might be other possible chemical levels that are off, and one of those might be causing the brain damage.

**C. Glutamate is the only neurotransmitter that leaks from oxygen-starved or physically damaged nerve cells.** — No Impact (31% of students)
This is better than nothing, kind of, although we really don't care if other neurotransmitters leak. We'd only care if the other neurotransmitters were known to kill nerve cells (then they would be a potential alternate cause).

**D. Leakage from damaged or oxygen-starved nerve cells is the only possible source of glutamate in the blood.** — Correct (50% of students)

```
Glutamate           it leaked from damaged or
in the blood   →    oxygen-starved nerve cells
```

Wait, didn't we already know that? [Check the text]

## S4 · Q21 — Parallel Reasoning

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
The only songs Amanda has ever written are blues songs and punk rock songs. Most punk rock songs involve no more than three chords. So if the next song Amanda writes is not a blues song, it probably will not involve more than three chords.

### Prompt
The reasoning in which one of the following arguments is most similar to that in the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (So):** If the next song Amanda writes is not a blues song, it probably will not involve more than three chords.
- **Evidence:** The only songs Amanda has ever written are blues songs and punk rock songs. Most punk rock songs involve no more than three chords.
- **Evaluate:** This argument combines several different reasoning patterns.

At the heart of this argument is a "**Past = Present**" type assumption. In the past, Amanda has only written B or P (blues or punk). And so the author is assuming that in the future, Amanda will only write B or P.

The argument also has the structure of a "**ruling out competing alternatives**". It's basically arguing, "Every song is B or P. So if the next song is not B, it will be P."

The argument is also using a "**most = probably**" type move. The author is thinking, "<u>Most</u> P songs involve no more than three chords. Thus, if this next song is P, then it will <u>probably</u> involve no more than three chords."

Since there lots of repeating concepts and there are also conditionals / quantifiers, it probably makes sense to try diagramming this argument.
- **Goal:** Let's look for something with this structure.

Premise 1: The only X's in the past have been A or B.
Premise 2: Most B's have trait Z.
Conclusion: If the next X is not A, it will probably have trait Z.

We'd expect the conclusion to be a conditional, with a probable outcome, so that might be an easy way to get rid of some answers.

**Answer choices**
**A. The only pets the Gupta family has ever owned are fish and parrots. Most parrots are very noisy. So if the next pet the Gupta family owns is a parrot, it will probably be very noisy.** — Bad Conclusion Match (6% of students)
This one is pretty close.

Premise 1: The only X's in the past have been A or B.
The only Gupta pets in the past have been fish or parrots.

Premise 2: Most B's have trait Z.
Most parrots have trait "very noisy".

Conclusion: If the next X is not A, it will probably have trait Z.
If the next Gupta pet is a parrot, it will probably be very noisy.

This would have worked if only the conclusion had said, "If the next pet is **not a fish**, it will probably be very noisy".

**B. Most parrots are very noisy. The Gupta family has never owned any pets other than fish and parrots. So if the Gupta family has ever owned a noisy pet, it was probably a parrot.** — Bad Conclusion Match (2% of students)
This conclusion is past-looking. Thus, it won't be able to replicate that "Past = Present/Future" aspect of the original argument.

This argument also has a flaw in that it's possible that most fish are also very noisy. Unless the argument rules out that possibility, we can't conclude that any past noisy pet was probably a parrot.

**C. All the pets the Gupta family has ever owned have been fish and parrots. Most parrots are very noisy. So any pet the Gupta family ever owns that is not a fish will probably be very noisy.** — Weak Conclusion Match (28% of students)
This one is also very close.

Premise 1: The only X's in the past have been A or B.
All the Gupta pets in the past have been fish or parrots.

Premise 2: Most B's have trait Z.
Most parrots have trait "very noisy".

Conclusion: If the next X is not A, it will probably have trait Z.
If any future Gupta pet is not a fish, it will probably be very noisy.

The only difference here is that the conclusion isn't about "the next pet", it's about all future pets.

This answer would work if the original argument had concluded, "So any future songs Amanda writes that aren't blues will probably not involve more than three chords."

**D. Every pet the Gupta family has ever owned has been a fish or a parrot. Most parrots are very noisy. So if the next pet the Gupta family owns is not a parrot, it will probably not be very noisy.** — Bad Conclusion Match (27% of students)
This one works until the conclusion.

Premise 1: The only X's in the past have been A or B.
Every Gupta pet in the past has been fish or parrots.

Premise 2: Most B's have trait Z.
Most parrots have trait "very noisy".

Conclusion: If the next X is not A, it will probably have trait Z.
If the next Gupta pet is not a parrot, it will probably not be very noisy.

This conclusion, symbolically, is saying,
Conclusion: If the next X is not B, it will probably not have trait Z.

**E. The Gupta family has never owned any pets other than fish and parrots. Most parrots are very noisy. So the next pet the Gupta family owns will probably be very noisy if it is not a fish.** — Correct (36% of students)
This one works top to bottom.

Premise 1: The only X's in the past have been A or B.
The only Gupta pets in the past has been fish or parrots.

Premise 2: Most B's have trait Z.
Most parrots have trait "very noisy".

Conclusion: If the next X is not A, it will probably have trait Z.
If the next Gupta pet is not a fish, it will probably be very noisy.

LSAC tried to fool us by burying the "if" trigger in the conclusion later in the sentence, and by forcing us to rephrase the first sentence into "the only X's have been A or B" form.

to honesty
```
- **Problem:**
```
S congratulates       →   she'll be insincere
S not congratulate    →   hurt D's feelings
```
- **Evaluate:** Since the question stem wants us to justify a conclusion using the principle, we'd have to be justifying the conclusion that "Shayna shouldn't be insincere". You can only prove/conclude what's on the right side of the Principle, so we can only use this principle to justify a conclusion that sounds like, "Thus, Shayna shouldn't be insincere" / "Shayna should be sincere" / "Shayna shouldn't congratulate Daniel" (since congratulating would be insincere)

"Except possibly" works like Unless / Without / Until. It's an "if-not" trigger.

When we say "Todd ... Melanie will never go out with you, except possibly if you invite her to a Cardi B concert", we're definitely not guaranteeing Todd that Melanie would agree to a date even if it were a Cardi B show. We're saying he has a chance (possibly) in that scenario, whereas he has no chance in any other scenario. This is the only possible exceptional case. Thus the way to get certainty with "except possibly / unless / without / until" is to say, "If we're not in that exceptional case, then you definitely won't get a date".
- **Goal:** Since we want to apply this principle to this problem, we want to
1. establish that Shayna doesn't believe that Daniel prefers kindness to honesty
and then
2. conclude that Shayna shouldn't be insincere (i.e. that she shouldn't congratulate him on his award)

**Answer choices**
**A. If Shayna congratulates Daniel, she will avoid hurting his feelings, so she should congratulate him.** — Bad Conclusion Match (10% of students)
This argument is saying that she *should* congratulate, which means she *should* be insincere. This principle has no power to prove *should be* insincere, only *shouldn't be* insincere (since you can only prove the idea on the right side of the principle).

**B. Daniel might prefer for Shayna to congratulate him—even if insincerely—rather than for her to express her true feelings, and so Shayna would be doing nothing wrong in insincerely congratulating Daniel.** — Bad Outcome Match / Bad Trigger Match (45% of students)
This argument is saying that she *would be doing nothing wrong* by congratulating, but this principle only has the power to prove that Shayna *would be* doing something wrong by congratulating, since we can only conclude the right side of the principle.

Additionally, this doesn't establish that Shanya **believes** that Daniel prefers kindness to honesty; it just informs us that Daniel might prefer kindness to honesty in this situation. The rule is based on a *belief* about another person's preference, not about their actual preference.

**C. Shayna believes that kindness should be preferred to dishonesty when speaking to others, so she should not tell Daniel her true feelings.** — Bad Premise/Conclusion Match (8% of students)
We don't care whether Shayna prefers kindness to honesty. The principle cares about whether she believes the person with whom she's speaking (Daniel) prefers kindness to honesty.

And the conclusion is saying "she should not tell her true feelings", which is drifting towards "she should lie / be insincere". Our principle only allows us to conclude that you shouldn't be insincere, that you should tell your true feelings.

**D. Daniel's feelings would be hurt if he knew that congratulations from Shayna were insincere, so Shayna should not congratulate him.** — Bad Premise Match (8% of students)
This does conclude what the principle has the power to prove: don't be insincere, so don't congratulate him.

But the basis for this conclusion doesn't match the trigger of the principle -- it says nothing about whether Shayna believes that Daniel prefers kindness to honesty.

**E. Shayna has no opinion about whether Daniel would prefer kindness to honesty, so she should not congratulate him.** — Correct (29% of students)
This establishes the trigger of the principle:
- it is not the case that Shayna believes Daniel prefers kindness to honesty (she has no opinion on the matter)

And it concludes the outcome of the principle:
- she should not be insincere; thus, she shouldn't congratulate Daniel.

Clearly, a democracy cannot thrive without effective news media. After all, a democracy cannot thrive without an electorate that is knowledgeable about important political issues, and an electorate can be knowledgeable in this way only if it has access to unbiased information about the government.

## S4 · Q25 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Roberta is irritable only when she is tired, and loses things only when she is tired. Since she has been yawning all day, and has just lost her keys, she is almost certainly irritable.

### Prompt
The reasoning above is flawed in that it

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Roberta is almost certainly irritable.
- **Evidence (since):** Roberta has been yawning all day and has lost her keys.

She is irritable only when she is tired, and loses things only when she is tired.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we're doing Flaw and see any conditional logic in the premises, we immediately suspect that we're headed into the #1 most famous flaw, **Necessary vs. Sufficient**.

Here, the conditional logic is signified by "only when", which is the same as "only if", a *necessary* indicator (right side of the arrow). So here are the two rules:

```
Irritable       →   Tired
Loses Things    →   Tired
```

Since Roberta has lost her keys, we know from the 2nd rule that she is definitely tired. The author mentions that she's been yawning all day, but we don't actually need that idea at all as a "premise" (we don't have to worry whether all-day yawning = tired, because the 2nd rule tells us that if she lost her keys, then she's tired).

The author is then moving from proving that Roberta is tired to concluding that she must be irritable. That, however, is an illegal reversal. The author is arguing like this:

if she's Tired, then she must be Irritable

But the conditional goes the other way:
Irritable → Tired
- **Goal:** Look for an answer complaining about the illegal reversal (aka 'confusing necessary with sufficient'). The author does soften the conclusion by saying almost-certainly, so the answer may reflect that. But the argument is failing to consider that Roberta might frequently be tired but not irritable.

**Answer choices**
**A. infers from a correlation between tiredness and yawning that tiredness causes yawning** — Not Causal (17% of students)
This answer describes one of the 10 famous flaws, *Causal*, in which the author overconfidently assumes a causal relationship between two things that were correlated in the premise.

Whenever an answer says "infers from X that Y", then X should match evidence and Y should match a conclusion the author drew.

Did the author conclude that "tiredness causes yawning"?

Nope. There also wasn't a correlation between tiredness and yawning presented (that would have sounded like, "People who are frequently tired are more likely than those who aren't to yawn").

**B. assumes the conclusion that it sets out to prove** — Not Circular (5% of students)
This answer describes one of the 10 famous flaws, *Circular Reasoning*, in which the premise restates the conclusion or requires the truth of the conclusion.

This answer is almost always wrong.

The conclusion here is that Roberta is irritable, and none of the premises require that she is currently irritable.

**C. generalizes on the basis of a single instance** — Not Sampling (2% of students)
This answer describes one of the 10 famous flaws, *Sampling*, in which we critique the argument for using a sample that is too small, unrepresentative, or biased in some way.

This argument doesn't use any sample at all.

**D. takes a necessary condition for Roberta's losing things to be a sufficient condition** — Not the Broken Conditional (18% of students)
There were two conditionals but the author only interpreted one of them illegally, and it was the one about being "irritable" not about "losing things".

The "losing things" conditional was:
If R loses something, then she's tired

The premise establishes that Roberta did lose something (her keys), and so according to this rule she is tired. There's nothing wrong with thinking that.

The problem is that the author thinks, "If she's tired, she must be irritable".

**E. takes a necessary condition for Roberta's being irritable to be a sufficient condition** — Correct (57% of students)
This points to the conditional that the author interpreted backwards.

Because Roberta lost her keys, we know that she is tired. But her being tired doesn't prove she's almost certainly irritable.

In order for Roberta to be irritable she has to be tired (it's necessary for her to be irritable). But being tired isn't sufficient to show she's irritable. She might be really good at being tired but not getting cranky.

## S4 · Q26 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Farmer: Crops genetically engineered to produce toxins that enable them to resist insect pests do not need to be sprayed with insecticides. Since excessive spraying of insecticides has harmed wildlife populations near croplands, using such genetically engineered crops more widely is likely to help wildlife populations to recover.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption the farmer's argument requires?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence (Since):** Too much insecticide has harmed the wildlife that live near the crops.

But ... we have new GMO crops that you don't need to spray with insecticide.
- **Conclusion:** Using GMO crops more would be good for wildlife near crops.
- **Evaluate:** This makes sense, superficially. If insecticides were harming wildlife, then GMO crops with no insecticides should be better, right?

This is an **Incomplete Comparison**.

When an author is pitching a Plan, she's implicitly making a comparison between the Current World and the hypothetical Plan World.

When we go from normal crops to GMO crops, **one difference** is that with the GMO crops won't need insecticide.

But there could potentially be other differences. How could the Plan fail to achieve its Goal?

*We grow more GMO crops (w/o insecticide),*
*but it doesn't help the local wildlife to recover.*

What if people don't want to buy the GMO crops? Would the farms go out of business? Maybe, but the conclusion is only making a claim about the effects of GMO crops on helping wildlife to recover.

If farms went out of business would that negatively impact local wildlife? Would something else about GMO negatively impact local wildlife?
- **Goal:** Find an answer that, when negated, sounds like a way to argue that this plan, if enacted (doing more GMO crops), wouldn't help wildlife to recover (maybe would even be harmful to them).

**Answer choices**
**A. Use of the crops that have been genetically engineered to resist insect pests in place of crops that have been sprayed with insecticides will cause less harm to wildlife populations.** — Correct (52% of students)
This correct answer is a language connector (Missing Link) between the premise and the conclusion.

Premise: the GMO crops don't need insecticide
Conclusion: the GMO crops will let wildlife recover

This answer is essentially saying, "If we use the GMO crops instead of the insecticide-laden normal crops, it'll be better for the local wildlife."

If we negated this, it would be saying, "Using the GMO crops will cause **at least as much** harm to local wildlife as the normal crops w/ insecticide do."

That would blow up the conclusion. The correct answer, when negated, just has to weaken, but in this case it pretty much contradicts the conclusion.

Generally, there are three ways to feel about a correct Necessary Assumption answer, and this is the 3rd way (the least common).

1. This answer is right, because the author *did* make a move from **this** concept to **that** concept.

2. This answer is right, because **if we negated it, it would weaken**.

3. This answer is right, because the **author was clearly thinking this**.

**B. Wildlife populations that have been harmed by the excessive spraying of insecticides on croplands are likely to recover if the amount of insecticides sprayed on those croplands is reduced even slightly.** — Too Strong: even slightly / likely (21% of students)
The author's conclusion is more squishy than this level of precision. She's saying "using it more widely" is likely to help. As we use GMO crops more widely, the amount of insecticides on those newly switched crops are reduced completely, not slightly.

When we switch from traditional crop to GMO crop, we turn pesticides down to 0. The author is predicting that turning some croplands down to 0 pesticides will likely help wildlife recover.

We don't know that she clearly thinks even a slight reduction in pesticide would lead to recovery.

**C. Crops that have been genetically engineered to resist insect pests are never sprayed with insecticides that harm wildlife populations.** — Too Strong: never (15% of students)
The author doesn't have to assume pesticides are never used on GMO crops. She's just assuming that as these GMO crops would increase in usage, some if not most of them would be pesticide free.

When you negate "never", you get a puny objection of, "Hey, author, there was at least one time when someone sprayed pesticides on GMO crops".

**D. Use of crops that have been genetically engineered to resist insect pests is no more costly to farmers than the use of insecticides on crops that are not genetically engineered.** — Out of Scope: cost (3% of students)
The issue of cost is out of scope, because we're only analyzing what effect this switch would have on local wildlife populations' recovering.

**E. If a wider use of certain crops that have been genetically engineered to resist insect pests is likely to help at least some wildlife populations to recover, it is likely to have that effect only because its use will prevent excessive and ineffective spraying of insecticides on croplands.** — If-Conclusion (9% of students)
The "if" part of this conditional is the argument's conclusion. That is always wrong. (The "if" part can be the negation of the conclusion)

An author's argument is only trying to get to its Main Conclusion. That's the final destination. The truth value of the claim in the Conclusion is the only verdict we're interested in.

Any answer saying "If Conclusion, then ..." is wrong since it's saying the author made a move from the conclusion to something else.

It's either gonna give us an Illegal reversal, going from Conclusion to Evidence, or it's just going to give us some bogus extra move, as this answer does.

The author doesn't need to assume that the benefit of GMO crops is only the pesticide thing. If you negate that answer and say, "Hey, author, the local wildlife from the GMO crops in another way too", it only strengthens the likelihood of her conclusion being true.
