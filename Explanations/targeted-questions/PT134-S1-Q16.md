# PrepTest 134 · Section 1 · Question 16

**Question type:** Flaw  
**Correct answer:** A  
**LSAT U order:** PT134-S1-Q16  
**Question bank file:** `public/data/PT134-S1-LR.json`

## Stimulus

The view that every person is concerned exclusively with her or his own self-interest implies that government by consent is impossible. Thus, social theorists who believe that people are concerned only with their self-interest evidently believe that aspiring to democracy is futile, since democracy is not possible in the absence of government by consent.

## Prompt

The reasoning in the argument is flawed in that the argument

## Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** Social theorists who believe that people are concerned only with self-interest evidently believe that aspiring to democracy is futile.
- **Evidence:** The self-interest view implies that government by consent is impossible, and democracy is not possible without government by consent.
- **Evaluate:** The argument moves from "this belief has an implication" to "the people who hold the belief also believe that implication." That is the bad jump. Knowing that a view points somewhere does not prove that the person holding the view sees, accepts, or believes every place it points.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that says the argument confuses believing a view with believing an implication of that view.

## Written explanation

Let's separate three things that the argument mashes together like a toddler making soup.

There is the **view itself**: people care only about their own self-interest. There is the **implication of that view**: government by consent is impossible. Then there is the **belief of the social theorists**: what those theorists actually think in their own heads.

The author treats those as if they automatically travel together. They do not.

Maybe the view really does imply that democracy is impossible. Fine. But that only tells us what follows logically from the view. It does not tell us that every social theorist who holds the view has noticed that implication, accepted it, and now walks around muttering "democracy is doomed" into their sad little political-theory coffee.

That is the flaw: **the argument attributes an implication of a belief to the believer.**

If Mark is secretly a three-time pig racing champion and Samika had dinner with Mark, we can say Samika had dinner with a three-time pig racing champion. But we cannot say Samika *knows* she had dinner with a three-time pig racing champion unless we know she knows that fact about Mark.

Same move here. The theory may imply something. The theorist may not believe that thing.

## Answer choice explanations

**A. infers merely from the fact of someone's holding a belief that he or she believes an implication of that belief**: **Correct.** This is the flaw exactly. The author says the self-interest view implies democracy is futile, then concludes that theorists who hold that view believe democracy is futile. But holding a belief is not the same as believing every implication of that belief. People miss implications all the time. People are walking contradiction museums.

**B. infers that because something is true of a group of people, it is true of each individual member of the group**: *Wrong flaw.* This is the group-to-member flaw. The argument is not saying, "This is true of social theorists as a group, so it is true of each theorist." The problem is not group math. The problem is belief math.

**C. infers that because something is true of each individual person belonging to a group, it is true of the group as a whole**: *Wrong direction.* This is the member-to-group flaw, the mirror image of B. The argument is not building from individuals to a group conclusion. Toss it.

**D. attempts to discredit a theory by discrediting those who espouse that theory**: *Ad hominem trap.* The argument does not attack the character, intelligence, or motives of the social theorists. It tries to infer what they believe from what their theory implies. That is a different bad move.

**E. fails to consider that, even if an argument's conclusion is false, some of the assumptions used to justify that conclusion may nonetheless be true**: *Not the issue.* The argument's problem is not about false conclusions and true assumptions. The issue is much cleaner: the author takes an implication of a belief and assigns it to the believer.

## Student takeaway

When a flaw question uses belief language, keep three boxes separate:

- what the view says
- what follows from the view
- what the person holding the view actually believes

If the argument slides from box two to box three, that is the trick.
