# PrepTest 158 · Section 4 · Question 24

**Question type:** Weaken  
**Correct answer:** D  
**LSAT U order:** PT158-S4-Q24  
**Question bank file:** `public/data/PT158-S4-LR.json`

## Stimulus

Consumer advocate: Some agricultural crops are now being genetically engineered to produce important pharmaceuticals. However, this development raises the possibility that the drugs will end up in the general food supply, since if pollen from a drug-producing crop drifts into a nearby field in which an ordinary, non-drug-producing crop of the same species is being grown, the pollen could fertilize that crop and turn it into a drug-producing crop as well.

## Prompt

Which one of the following, if true, would most weaken the consumer advocate's argument?

## Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** The development of pharmaceutical-producing crops raises the possibility that drugs will end up in the general food supply.
- **Evidence:** Pollen from a drug-producing crop could drift into a nearby field, fertilize an ordinary crop of the same species, and turn that ordinary crop into a drug-producing crop.
- **Evaluate:** The argument assumes that if an ordinary food crop becomes drug-producing, then the drug will be present in the part of the plant people actually eat. That is the bridge. If the drug-producing trait never reaches the edible part, the scary pollen story loses its bite.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that blocks the move from "cross-pollinated crop becomes drug-producing" to "drugs enter the food supply."

## Written explanation

This argument is trying to scare us with a chain.

Drug crop exists. Pollen drifts. Ordinary crop gets fertilized. Ordinary crop becomes drug-producing. Then, apparently, the drug sneaks into the food supply wearing a tiny trench coat.

But slow down. The conclusion is not merely that cross-pollination could happen. The conclusion is that the drugs could end up in the food supply. Those are different claims, and the space between them is where the question lives.

The author needs the drug to be in the part of the plant people eat. If the crop is harvested for grain, fruit, or leaves, but the pharmaceutical compound is only in some other non-food part of the plant, then the pollen chain may happen without putting the drug on anyone's plate.

That is why D weakens the argument. **It lets the genetic story happen, but blocks the food-supply ending.**

This is the clean weaken move: do not argue with every link. Break the link that matters.

## Answer choice explanations

**A. As far as scientists know, none of the pharmaceuticals produced by genetically engineered crops would present any danger to public health if they were present in the general food supply.**: *No impact.* This talks about whether the drugs would be dangerous. But the argument's conclusion is about whether the drugs could end up in the food supply. "It would not hurt anyone" does not weaken "it could get there." Different fight.

**B. If pollen from a genetically engineered crop is prevented from drifting into fields in which ordinary crops of the same species are being grown, then there is no risk of the pollen fertilizing the latter crops.**: *Validates the concern.* This basically says, "If you stop the pollen from drifting, you stop the problem." Sure. But that confirms pollen drift is the mechanism we care about. It gives a prevention strategy, not a reason the argument is weak.

**C. The genetically engineered crops that produce pharmaceuticals are not among the crop species that comprise the largest portions of the general food supply.**: *Reduces scale, does not eliminate.* Maybe these crops are not corn, wheat, or rice. Fine. But smaller food crops still enter the food supply. The advocate only needs a possibility of contamination, not a nationwide grocery-store apocalypse.

**D. In crops genetically engineered to produce pharmaceuticals, the drugs are not present in any part of the plant used for food in ordinary crops of the same species.**: **Correct.** This breaks the causal chain at the most important spot. Even if pollen drifts and transfers the drug-producing trait, the pharmaceutical does not appear in the edible part of the crop. So the crop may be "drug-producing" technically, while the food part stays drug-free. The mechanism exists, but it does not reach your plate.

**E. If pollen from a drug-producing crop turned an ordinary crop of the same species into a drug-producing crop, it would be possible for scientists to identify the latter crop.**: *After-the-fact detection.* Detection is not prevention. If scientists can identify contaminated crops, that may help manage the problem, but it does not weaken the claim that contamination could occur. The answer basically says, "We can find the raccoon after it gets into the kitchen." Great. Still a raccoon in the kitchen.

## Student takeaway

For weaken questions, do not just react to the topic. Find the exact bridge.

Here, the bridge is:

> crop becomes drug-producing -> drug enters the food supply

D attacks that bridge. The drug can exist somewhere in the plant without existing in the part people eat.

That's the move.
