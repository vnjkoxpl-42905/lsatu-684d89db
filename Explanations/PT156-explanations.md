# Section 2 (Logical Reasoning)

## S2 · Q1 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus
When primatologist Akira Suzuki began studying snow monkeys in the 1950s, he found that they often roamed out of the mountains to feed in apple orchards. After a decade of observing this behavior, Suzuki began to feed the monkeys in their mountain habitat by providing them with soybeans to eat. The monkeys no longer raided the orchards. When Suzuki began his work, 23 snow monkeys lived in the region he studied. The population today is 270 snow monkeys and is expected to continue growing.

### Prompt
Which one of the following claims is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Anticipate:** The strongest pattern here is that the snow monkeys stopped leaving the mountain to raid orchards as soon as Suzuki started providing soybeans in the mountain habitat. The most strongly supported inference will draw on that before-and-after change.
- **Evidence:** Before food was supplied: monkeys roamed out to apple orchards. After Suzuki provided soybeans in the mountain habitat: monkeys stopped raiding orchards.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that follows directly from the stimulus — most likely something tying the monkeys' decision to leave the mountain habitat to whether food is available there.

**Answer choices**
**A. Snow monkeys do not feed outside of their mountain habitat when food is readily available within it.** — Correct (94% of students)
This follows directly from the before-and-after pattern. When food was not readily available in the mountain habitat, the monkeys roamed out to feed in apple orchards. Once Suzuki started providing soybeans in the mountain habitat — making food readily available there — the monkeys stopped raiding the orchards. That is exactly what this answer claims: snow monkeys do not feed outside their mountain habitat when food is available within it.

**B. For snow monkeys, soybeans provide more complete nutrition than other beans.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
The stimulus says nothing about how soybeans compare to other beans nutritionally. The monkeys ate soybeans because Suzuki provided them; we have no information about which beans are most nutritious or whether the monkeys would behave the same way with a different bean.

**C. In feeding soybeans to the monkeys, Suzuki did not intend to provoke the phenomenal population growth that resulted.** — Unsupported (4% of students)
The stimulus tells us what Suzuki did but not what he intended. Whether the population growth was a goal, a surprise, or a regret to him is simply not addressed. Most-strongly-supported answers cannot rely on the unstated motivations of people in the stimulus.

**D. Snow monkeys eat apples only if there is no other fruit to eat.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
The stimulus says monkeys roamed to apple orchards before being fed soybeans. It does not tell us anything about other fruit or rank apples among other foods the monkeys might eat. This answer makes a much sharper claim — that monkeys eat apples only when no other fruit is available — that the stimulus simply does not establish.

**E. Feeding soybeans to snow monkeys has proved to be an environmentally unsound policy.** — Unsupported (0% of students)
Whether feeding the monkeys was environmentally unsound is a value judgment the stimulus does not make. The population growth is described neutrally; the stimulus does not call it harmful or weigh costs against benefits.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Anticipate:** Monkeys stopped raiding orchards once soybeans were provided in the mountain habitat. The supported inference will tie food availability to where monkeys feed.
- **Evidence:** Before mountain food was provided, monkeys roamed to orchards; after, they stopped.
- **Goal:** Pick the answer that says, in effect, monkeys do not roam out for food when food is readily available at home.

**Answer choices**
**A. Snow monkeys do not feed outside of their mountain habitat when food is readily available within it.** — Correct (94% of students)
The monkeys stopped raiding orchards once soybeans were provided in the mountain — which is exactly what this claim describes.

**B. For snow monkeys, soybeans provide more complete nutrition than other beans.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
The stimulus says nothing about soybean nutrition relative to other beans.

**C. In feeding soybeans to the monkeys, Suzuki did not intend to provoke the phenomenal population growth that resulted.** — Unsupported (4% of students)
The stimulus does not address Suzuki's intentions about population growth.

**D. Snow monkeys eat apples only if there is no other fruit to eat.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
The stimulus does not address the monkeys' fruit preferences or other-fruit availability.

**E. Feeding soybeans to snow monkeys has proved to be an environmentally unsound policy.** — Unsupported (0% of students)
The stimulus describes population growth neutrally; no environmental judgment is made.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Anticipate:** The cleanest pattern in the stimulus is the before-and-after: monkeys went to orchards before, and stopped going once Suzuki put soybeans in their mountain habitat.
- **Evidence:** That sequence tells us a lot. The monkeys' trips out of the mountains looked like food-seeking — and once food was easy to get at home, the trips stopped.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that follows from this: the monkeys leave the mountain when food is scarce there, and stay home when it is plentiful.

**Answer choices**
**A. Snow monkeys do not feed outside of their mountain habitat when food is readily available within it.** — Correct (94% of students)
This is the most natural reading of the stimulus. Before Suzuki started feeding the monkeys, they had to go out to apple orchards to find food. Once Suzuki put soybeans in their mountain habitat, the orchard raids stopped.

Put those two facts together and the inference falls out: the monkeys went to the orchards because they needed food, and they stopped going once food was available at home. That is exactly what this answer says.

**B. For snow monkeys, soybeans provide more complete nutrition than other beans.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
The stimulus mentions soybeans only because Suzuki happened to feed soybeans. It says nothing about the nutritional value of soybeans compared to other beans. We have no basis for ranking beans by nutrition.

**C. In feeding soybeans to the monkeys, Suzuki did not intend to provoke the phenomenal population growth that resulted.** — Unsupported (4% of students)
Notice the answer is about what Suzuki intended. The stimulus says what he did and what happened, but says nothing at all about his intentions, expectations, or surprise. So we cannot infer this either way.

**D. Snow monkeys eat apples only if there is no other fruit to eat.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
The stimulus says the monkeys ate apples in orchards. It does not say apples are a fallback or that monkeys prefer some other fruit when available. This answer adds an "only" that the stimulus does not support.

**E. Feeding soybeans to snow monkeys has proved to be an environmentally unsound policy.** — Unsupported (0% of students)
The stimulus reports the population growth as a fact but does not label it harmful or unsustainable. To conclude the policy is environmentally unsound, we would need information about ecological consequences that the stimulus simply does not provide.

## S2 · Q5 — Evaluate

**Correct answer:** B

### Stimulus
Zobel: Peterson's analytic concepts are wrong and should be rejected. As a psychoanalyst myself, however, I can understand why certain psychoanalysts adhere to them. These psychoanalysts acquired their "emotional certainty" that Peterson's views are correct while training under her. This training includes one's own psychoanalysis, in which the teacher interprets the actions, dreams, and fantasies of the student in analytic terms. Strong emotional bonds with the teacher are formed, bonds that predispose the student to accept the teacher's analytic concepts as correct. It is thus impossible for a student to make unbiased judgments about the value of the teacher's analytic concepts.

### Prompt
Based on the information in the passage, which one of the following is the most accurate assessment of Zobel's claim that Peterson's analytic concepts are wrong and should be rejected?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Zobel claims Peterson's analytic concepts are wrong and should be rejected.
- **Intermediate Conclusion (thus):** It's impossible for a student to make unbiased judgments about the value of the teacher's analytic concepts.
- **Evidence:** The teacher interprets the actions, dreams, and fantasies of the student in analytic terms, which leads to strong emotional bonds with the teacher that predispose the student to accept the teacher's analytic concepts.
- **Evaluate:** The evidence is about Peterson's students, that they are biased in their assessments. That tells us nothing about whether Peterson's concepts are themselves wrong. Even if every one of her students is hopelessly biased, her concepts could still be correct.

  This is an Unproven vs. Proven False type argument. Zobel is basically showing how the students' faith in Peterson is a faulty way of proving Peterson right (they're too biased to be trusted). But that doesn't mean that Zobel has proven Peterson wrong.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer saying that Zobel has only established that we should be suspicious of students who say (because of bias) that Peterson's concepts are correct, but has provided no evidence to make us think that Peterson's concepts are wrong.

**Answer choices**
**A. The claim is dubious because Zobel assumes without justification that she is qualified to judge Peterson's analytic concepts.** — Bad Objection (2% of students)
Does she really not have justification to think she's qualified? She does briefly mention that she is "a psychoanalyst myself", so that would be a weird way for us to deny her standing.

"We're not even going to evaluate the merits of your argument, Zobel, because you're not even qualified to make an argument on this matter."

That would seem like an LSAT flaw, not an LSAT correct answer.

**B. The claim has not been established because Zobel provides evidence that could show only that Peterson's students are biased in the evaluation of Peterson's analytic concepts.** — Correct (84% of students)
This gets at our objection that all Zobel has done is given us reasons to not put blind faith in Peterson's supporters.

The fact that they're biased shows we should take their endorsement of Peterson with a grain of salt.

But it in no way proves that Peterson is actually wrong about his ideas. By saying, "Z's evidence could show only X", they're saying "Z's evidence did not prove the actual conclusion".

**C. The claim cannot be evaluated because it is not possible for any psychoanalyst to provide an objective assessment of another psychoanalyst's analytic concepts.** — Too Strong (impossible) (3% of students)
Why are we saying it's impossible for a psychoanalyst to provide an objective assessment? Couldn't it be a blind taste test? If we withhold the person responsible for the analytic concepts and just write out the analytic concepts, couldn't a psychoanalyst provide an objective evaluation?

Like (A), this would be a weird correct answer for LSAT, since it's saying that we should disqualify someone's view based on personal characteristics (such as professional bias). That's how ad hominem flaw works, not how a correct answer works.

The point of assessing an argument is to talk about how the evidence falls short of proving the conclusion, not to disqualify the speaker from even making an argument.

**D. The claim is questionable because it is obvious that Zobel has a professional rivalry with Peterson and cannot judge Peterson's concepts fairly.** — Out of Scope (rivalry) (1% of students)
How is it obvious that Zobel has a rivalry with Peterson? Because she disagrees with Peterson? That doesn't show there's an obvious rivalry.

Like (A) and (C), this would be a weird correct answer for LSAT, since it's saying that we should disqualify someone's view based on personal characteristics (such as having a rivalry with someone). That's how ad hominem flaw works, not how a correct answer works. If X has a rivalry with Y, then we take X's comments about Y with a little grain of salt. We'd want some unbiased corroboration. But we don't totally disqualify its legitimacy.

The point of assessing an argument is to talk about how the evidence falls short of proving the conclusion, not to disqualify the speaker from even making an argument.

**E. The claim is acceptable because Zobel has effectively shown that Peterson's analytic concepts are biased and based on emotion rather than rational thinking.** — Opposite (10% of students)
Zobel's argument was badly flawed, not acceptable. She didn't show that Peterson's concepts are biased, only that her supporters are biased in favor of her.

She also didn't say that Peterson's concepts were based on emotion, only that emotional bonds with her students is what makes her students have a bias in favor of her.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Zobel claims Peterson's analytic concepts are wrong and should be rejected.
- **Evidence:** Peterson's students are biased toward her concepts because of emotional bonds formed during training.
- **Evaluate:** Bias in students says nothing about whether the concepts themselves are correct.
- **Goal:** Pick the answer pointing out that Zobel's evidence supports only student bias, not the falsity of the concepts.

**Answer choices**
**A. The claim is dubious because Zobel assumes without justification that she is qualified to judge Peterson's analytic concepts.** — Bad Description (2% of students)
Zobel's qualifications are not the issue; the gap is between her evidence and her conclusion.

**B. The claim has not been established because Zobel provides evidence that could show only that Peterson's students are biased in the evaluation of Peterson's analytic concepts.** — Correct (84% of students)
Zobel's evidence shows only that Peterson's students are biased — not that Peterson's concepts are wrong.

**C. The claim cannot be evaluated because it is not possible for any psychoanalyst to provide an objective assessment of another psychoanalyst's analytic concepts.** — Too Strong (3% of students)
The stimulus speaks only of Peterson's students, not all psychoanalysts.

**D. The claim is questionable because it is obvious that Zobel has a professional rivalry with Peterson and cannot judge Peterson's concepts fairly.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
No professional rivalry is mentioned in the stimulus.

**E. The claim is acceptable because Zobel has effectively shown that Peterson's analytic concepts are biased and based on emotion rather than rational thinking.** — Opposite (10% of students)
Zobel did not show the concepts themselves are biased — only that students may be.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Zobel says Peterson's ideas are wrong.
- **Evidence:** Her support: Peterson's students get emotionally attached during training and therefore cannot fairly judge her ideas.
- **Evaluate:** Watch this carefully — Zobel is talking about students, not the ideas themselves. Even if every student is biased toward Peterson, that does not show Peterson's ideas are wrong. It just shows the students are not in a position to judge.

  Think of it this way: a math teacher's students might worship her too much to judge her work fairly. That has nothing to do with whether her math is right.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that says Zobel's evidence shows student bias, not the falsity of Peterson's concepts.

**Answer choices**
**A. The claim is dubious because Zobel assumes without justification that she is qualified to judge Peterson's analytic concepts.** — Bad Description (2% of students)
The problem with Zobel's argument is not whether she is qualified — she is also a psychoanalyst. The real issue is that her evidence is about students' bias, not about whether the ideas themselves are wrong. This answer points at the wrong target.

**B. The claim has not been established because Zobel provides evidence that could show only that Peterson's students are biased in the evaluation of Peterson's analytic concepts.** — Correct (84% of students)
This nails it. Zobel's conclusion is about the *concepts*: they're wrong, reject them. But every piece of evidence she offers is about *students*: they form emotional bonds, they cannot judge fairly, they are biased.

Those are different things. A teacher's students could be hopelessly biased while the teacher's ideas are perfectly correct. So Zobel's evidence at best establishes the student-bias point. It does not get her to her actual conclusion that the concepts are wrong.

That is exactly what this answer says, which is why it is the most accurate assessment.

**C. The claim cannot be evaluated because it is not possible for any psychoanalyst to provide an objective assessment of another psychoanalyst's analytic concepts.** — Too Strong (3% of students)
This goes way too far. Zobel's point is just about Peterson's own students — they are biased because of training relationships. She does not say any psychoanalyst is incapable of evaluating any other. That is a much bigger claim than the stimulus supports.

**D. The claim is questionable because it is obvious that Zobel has a professional rivalry with Peterson and cannot judge Peterson's concepts fairly.** — Unsupported (1% of students)
This invents a motive for Zobel that the stimulus does not mention. Even if she had a rivalry with Peterson, the actual problem with her argument is that her evidence does not support her conclusion. We would not need to speculate about her motives to see that.

**E. The claim is acceptable because Zobel has effectively shown that Peterson's analytic concepts are biased and based on emotion rather than rational thinking.** — Opposite (10% of students)
This says Zobel succeeded. But she did not show Peterson's concepts are biased; she showed Peterson's students are biased. Those are different claims, and only the second one is actually supported by what Zobel says. So endorsing Zobel's conclusion as established is exactly the mistake the question is testing.

## S2 · Q6 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** D

### Stimulus
Some psychologists claim that empathic responses are forms of moral behavior. Having observed that young children who witness another's distress respond by expressing sadness and offering help, these psychologists believe that moral behavior begins early in life. A second group of psychologists claims that empathic response is not, by itself, moral behavior and that in order to count as moral, behavior must be based on a clear understanding of moral principles and a certain degree of moral reasoning skill. On the basis of children's unsophisticated verbal responses to hypothetical moral dilemmas, these psychologists conclude that children lack the degree of moral reasoning skill necessary for their behavior, however compassionate, to be considered moral.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most seriously undermines the conclusion drawn by the second group of psychologists?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Children lack the moral reasoning skill necessary for their behavior to count as moral.
- **Evidence:** Children provided unsophisticated verbal responses to hypothetical moral dilemmas.
- **Evaluate:** If we were going to try to argue that "children DO have the moral reasoning skill necessary for their behavior to count as moral", how would we respond to the fact that they provided unsophisticated verbal responses to hypothetical moral dilemmas?

  We might say the hypothetical moral dilemmas are dumb / irrelevant / exotic / unlikely to be something a child could weigh in on based on their deficient knowledge of the world (not their moral deficiency).

  We might say that children aren't verbally talented enough to provide a sophisticated verbal response, even though their moral intuitions are still sophisticated. In other words, they aren't responding poorly out of a lack of moral prowess but rather out of a lack of verbal prowess.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that gives us a way to argue that kids do have moral reasoning skills or one that allows us to "make an excuse" for kids' poor verbal answers to these hypothetical moral dilemmas.

**Answer choices**
**A. The children studied by the second group of psychologists displayed a slightly higher level of moral reasoning when they were well rested than when they were tired.** — Weaker / Unclear Impact (2% of students)
When these kids gave their unsophisticated responses, where they tired or well rested? We don't know, which makes it hard to judge the impact of this answer.

But even if they were tired, this answer is only saying that they would've performed "slightly better" if rested. That doesn't fundamentally challenge the conclusion.

**B. Adults who respond to hypothetical moral dilemmas display a much higher level of moral reasoning than do children who responded to the same hypothetical moral dilemmas.** — Opposite (1% of students)
This basically strengthens by ruling out the potential objection that the hypothetical moral dilemmas are a stupid / illegitimate way to test moral reasoning.

If adults can do much better at these, then it helps the psychologists argue that the children's poor performance is due to their lack of moral reasoning skill.

**C. The children studied by the second group of psychologists displayed a slightly higher level of moral reasoning in response to hypothetical dilemmas involving adults than in response to hypothetical dilemmas involving children.** — Irrelevant Comparison (3% of students)
We know overall that the children's responses to the hypothetical dilemmas were unsophisticated.

The fact that some responses were slightly better than others doesn't change anything. The average of those responses was "unsophisticated".

If a basketball player had a poor shooting night, the fact that their shooting percentages were slightly higher if they shot from this spot as opposed to that spot doesn't change the fact that overall they had a poor shooting night.

**D. In actual situations involving moral dilemmas, children display a much higher level of moral reasoning than did the children who, in the study by the second group of psychologists, responded only to hypothetical dilemmas.** — Correct (78% of students)
This helps us argue that kids DO have moral reasoning skill, while giving us a way to respond to the author's evidence.

If kids reason at a much higher level in actual moral situations than they do when responding to hypotheticals, then their weak performance on hypothetical dilemmas doesn't fairly represent children's real moral reasoning ability.

The hypothetical test was undercounting their skill. Their poor performance seems more related to the test itself than to their moral reasoning.

**E. Some adults who respond to hypothetical moral dilemmas reason at about the same level as children who respond to the same hypothetical moral dilemmas.** — Unclear Impact (16% of students)
This is a very weak idea (some = at least one), so it's very unlikely to be the answer with the most impact.

The fact that some adults are tied with the kids could maybe suggest that the kids did well (they tied an adult!).

But it could just as easily mean that this adult sucks at moral reasoning (they tied with children!)

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Children lack the moral reasoning needed for their behavior to count as moral.
- **Evidence:** Children's weak verbal responses to *hypothetical* moral dilemmas.
- **Evaluate:** Hypothetical performance might not reflect actual reasoning ability in real situations.
- **Goal:** Find an answer showing children reason at a higher level in real moral situations than in hypotheticals.

**Answer choices**
**A. The children studied by the second group of psychologists displayed a slightly higher level of moral reasoning when they were well rested than when they were tired.** — No Impact (2% of students)
A slight rest-related improvement doesn't challenge the broader conclusion.

**B. Adults who respond to hypothetical moral dilemmas display a much higher level of moral reasoning than do children who responded to the same hypothetical moral dilemmas.** — No Impact (1% of students)
Adult-vs-child comparison on hypotheticals doesn't challenge whether hypotheticals measure children's ability fairly.

**C. The children studied by the second group of psychologists displayed a slightly higher level of moral reasoning in response to hypothetical dilemmas involving adults than in response to hypothetical dilemmas involving children.** — No Impact (3% of students)
Slight variation within hypothetical tests doesn't address real vs. hypothetical performance.

**D. In actual situations involving moral dilemmas, children display a much higher level of moral reasoning than did the children who, in the study by the second group of psychologists, responded only to hypothetical dilemmas.** — Correct (78% of students)
Children reason much better in real situations than on hypotheticals, so the test undercounted their skill.

**E. Some adults who respond to hypothetical moral dilemmas reason at about the same level as children who respond to the same hypothetical moral dilemmas.** — No Impact (16% of students)
Some adults scoring like children doesn't affect what children can do.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (second group):** The second group concludes that children don't have enough moral reasoning skill for their behavior to count as moral.
- **Evidence:** Their evidence is how children performed on hypothetical moral dilemmas — verbal responses to imaginary scenarios. The kids gave unsophisticated answers, so the second group concluded they lack the reasoning skill.
- **Evaluate:** Watch the gap. The conclusion is about children's moral reasoning in general. The evidence is performance on a specific kind of test — verbal answers to fake situations. Maybe kids are bad at articulating moral reasoning in the abstract but actually reason carefully when faced with real situations.

  If real-situation reasoning is much better than hypothetical-test reasoning, then the evidence isn't a fair sample of what children can actually do, and the conclusion overreaches.
- **Goal:** Find an answer showing children do better with real moral situations than with hypothetical ones — making the second group's evidence base too narrow.

**Answer choices**
**A. The children studied by the second group of psychologists displayed a slightly higher level of moral reasoning when they were well rested than when they were tired.** — No Impact (2% of students)
This says rested kids reason slightly better. That's a small effect within the same kind of test — it doesn't suggest kids actually have the reasoning skill the conclusion denies. A small bump in hypothetical performance doesn't change the overall picture.

**B. Adults who respond to hypothetical moral dilemmas display a much higher level of moral reasoning than do children who responded to the same hypothetical moral dilemmas.** — No Impact (1% of students)
This just confirms adults outperform children on the hypothetical tests. Sure — but that's consistent with the second group's view either way. It doesn't tell us whether children's real-situation reasoning is different from their hypothetical performance, which is what we'd need to weaken the conclusion.

**C. The children studied by the second group of psychologists displayed a slightly higher level of moral reasoning in response to hypothetical dilemmas involving adults than in response to hypothetical dilemmas involving children.** — No Impact (3% of students)
This is comparing two different versions of the hypothetical test. Both are still hypotheticals. The argument's problem is that hypotheticals might not reflect real moral reasoning — comparing kinds of hypotheticals doesn't address that.

**D. In actual situations involving moral dilemmas, children display a much higher level of moral reasoning than did the children who, in the study by the second group of psychologists, responded only to hypothetical dilemmas.** — Correct (78% of students)
This is exactly the weakener we were looking for. Children do much better at moral reasoning in real situations than they do on the hypothetical tests the second group used.

That means the second group was measuring children at their weakest moment — verbal abstract performance — and concluding they lack the skill. But the kids actually have more reasoning skill than the test showed; the test just didn't pick it up. So the conclusion overreaches, and (D) is the answer that exposes the overreach.

**E. Some adults who respond to hypothetical moral dilemmas reason at about the same level as children who respond to the same hypothetical moral dilemmas.** — No Impact (16% of students)
This says some adults score like kids. Possibly interesting trivia, but it doesn't change anything about children's reasoning. The conclusion is about kids — whether they have the skill — and this is about a slice of the adult population.

## S2 · Q8 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus
Historian: A democracy's citizens must know some history if the democracy is to meet its challenges. However, popular historical awareness is inevitably distorted, for most people learn history through popular narratives that sustain readers' interest by implying that a few famous heroes and notorious villains have shaped all of history.

### Prompt
The historian's argument depends on assuming which one of the following?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Popular historical awareness is inevitably distorted.
- **Evidence:** Most people learn history through popular narratives, and those narratives sustain interest by implying that a few famous heroes and notorious villains have shaped all of history.
- **Evaluate:** The argument moves from "popular narratives imply X" to "popular awareness is distorted." That move only works if the implication itself is distorting — that is, if it is false (or distorting) to suggest a few heroes and villains have shaped all of history.
- **Goal:** Find the assumption that the implication about heroes and villains is itself distorting. Negate it (the implication is accurate or non-distorting), and the conclusion collapses.

**Answer choices**
**A. Historical awareness is distorted by the view that there have been only a few famous heroes or notorious villains.** — Bad Assumption (19% of students)
This says historical awareness is distorted by the view "that there have been only a few famous heroes and notorious villains." But the historian's claim is more specific: the popular narratives imply that a few heroes and villains have shaped all of history. That is not the same as denying that other heroes and villains exist; it is about who is given credit for shaping history. This answer rewords the implication in a way the historian's argument does not require.

**B. History cast in the narrative format inevitably distorts historical reality.** — Too Strong (10% of students)
The argument does not require that all narrative-format history is distorting. Its specific complaint is about narratives that imply a few heroes and villains shaped all of history. A narrative that did not push that implication could in principle be accurate. This answer overgeneralizes from a specific claim about content to a sweeping claim about format.

**C. Most historical narratives sustain interest by implying that a few famous heroes and notorious villains have shaped all of history.** — Premise Support (6% of students)
This essentially restates the historian's premise — that most popular historical narratives sustain interest by implying a few heroes and villains shaped all of history. Restating an explicit premise is not an unstated assumption the argument depends on. The gap the argument needs to bridge is whether that implication is distorting, not whether narratives use it.

**D. Only narratives written for a purpose other than sustaining readers' interest can convey an undistorted awareness of history.** — Too Strong (7% of students)
The argument concludes only that popular history is inevitably distorted; it does not commit to the much stronger claim that only non-interest-sustaining narratives can be undistorted. There could be plenty of paths to undistorted historical awareness — academic study, primary sources, classroom learning — that the argument does not address. Negating this is not necessary to support the conclusion.

**E. The implication that a few famous heroes and notorious villains have shaped all of history distorts history.** — Correct (57% of students)
This is the bridge. The historian says (a) popular narratives imply a few heroes and villains shaped all of history, and concludes (b) popular historical awareness is distorted. For (a) to imply (b), the implication itself must be distorting. Negate this answer — say the implication does not distort history — and popular narratives could spread an accurate picture. The conclusion that awareness is "inevitably distorted" no longer follows.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Popular historical awareness is inevitably distorted.
- **Evidence:** Popular narratives imply a few heroes and villains shaped all of history.
- **Evaluate:** The leap requires that the implication itself is distorting.
- **Goal:** Pick the answer asserting that the heroes-and-villains implication distorts history.

**Answer choices**
**A. Historical awareness is distorted by the view that there have been only a few famous heroes or notorious villains.** — Bad Assumption (19% of students)
The argument is about who shaped history, not about how many heroes and villains exist.

**B. History cast in the narrative format inevitably distorts historical reality.** — Too Strong (10% of students)
The argument complains about content (heroes-and-villains framing), not narrative format in general.

**C. Most historical narratives sustain interest by implying that a few famous heroes and notorious villains have shaped all of history.** — Premise Support (6% of students)
This restates the historian's explicit premise rather than identifying an unstated assumption.

**D. Only narratives written for a purpose other than sustaining readers' interest can convey an undistorted awareness of history.** — Too Strong (7% of students)
The conclusion does not require that only non-interest-driven narratives can be undistorted.

**E. The implication that a few famous heroes and notorious villains have shaped all of history distorts history.** — Correct (57% of students)
If the heroes-and-villains implication itself distorts history, then narratives spreading it produce distorted awareness — exactly the historian's leap.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The historian says popular history awareness is always going to be distorted.
- **Evidence:** Why? Because most people learn history from books that grab readers by implying a small cast of heroes and villains shaped everything.
- **Evaluate:** For the argument to work, that implication has to actually *be* distorting. If the heroes-and-villains framing were perfectly accurate, then narratives spreading it would not produce distorted awareness — they would produce accurate awareness.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that explicitly says the heroes-and-villains implication is distorting. That is the bridge the argument depends on.

**Answer choices**
**A. Historical awareness is distorted by the view that there have been only a few famous heroes or notorious villains.** — Bad Assumption (19% of students)
Read this carefully. It says the distorting view is that there are only a few heroes and villains. But the stimulus is about something different — the implication that a few heroes and villains *shaped all of history*. The argument doesn't need to assume there are only a few of them, just that crediting a few with shaping all of history is misleading.

This is just the historian's premise repeated back. The argument already says most popular narratives sustain interest by implying a few heroes and villains shaped history. ### Stimulus
Medical researcher: Studies in North America have shown that the incidence of heart disease in a population is closely related to the average fat consumption for individuals in that population. ### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, would most help to resolve the apparent conflict noted by the medical researcher?

### Standard
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that ...:** Heart disease incidence is closely related to average fat consumption. *and that* The French consume as much fat as North Americans.
- **How can it be that ...:** Heart disease occurs half as often among the French as among North Americans?
- **Goal:** We're going to need some distinction between France and North America to explain why they're not comparable in terms of heart disease. Maybe the French do something good that North Americans don't do that lowers heart disease risk, or the North Americans do something bad that the French don't do that increases heart disease risk.

**Answer choices**
**A. The average level of fat consumption by the French has been falling for several decades.** — Opposite (if anything) (2% of students)
This says French fat consumption has been falling for decades. But the stimulus tells us that currently French and North American fat consumption are equal.

So if French levels fell to now equalize North American levels, that would mean fat consumption used to be even higher among the French. If anything, that should mean the French should now have more heart disease as the high-fat past catches up with them.

This makes the puzzle worse, not better.

**B. Other factors of diet besides high consumption of fat have not been similarly linked with incidence of heart disease.** — No Impact (7% of students)
This talks about an absence of causal factors.

We need a causal factor, baby! We need a distinction between the French and North Americans that could cause two different rates of heart disease, even though fat consumption is similar.

**C. Heart disease takes years to develop and the average level of fat consumption in France increased to North American levels only a few years ago.** — Correct (86% of students)
This is a weird distinction. It's essentially saying, "Just you wait, France — you'll have proud North American levels of heart disease in just a little while".

The reason heart disease is half as common in France is because they have historically been eating less fat than North Americans (which makes perfect sense, since we know fat is linked to heart disease).

The mismatch between current fat intake and current heart disease levels is just because heart disease is a lagging indicator of fat intake; you don't see the damage that fat is doing until many years later.

**D. Certain diseases other than heart disease have also been linked to average fat consumption, and the French have a higher incidence of these than do North Americans.** — Opposite (if anything) (3% of students)
We're trying to explain why France has a "lower than expected" rate of heart disease given their fat intake, whereas this is making France seem like it has a "higher than expected" rate of other diseases given their fat intake.

Why would the French be getting MORE of these fat-related diseases and LESS of heart disease, which is also a fat-related disease?

**E. Cigarette smoking significantly increases the risk of heart disease and France has a higher percentage of cigarette smokers in its population than does North America.** — Opposite (2% of students)
We're trying to explain why France has lower-than-expected heart disease, given its fat intake.

This is an answer that would explain higher rates of heart disease, so it's the opposite of what we're looking for.

### Brief
**Argument breakdown**
- **The Paradox:** Heart disease tracks fat consumption, the French eat as much fat as North Americans, yet have half the heart disease.
- **Evaluate:** The resolution must explain why current French fat intake has not yet shown up in heart disease rates.
- **Goal:** Pick the answer that explains a delay between high fat consumption and the appearance of heart disease.

**Answer choices**
**A. The average level of fat consumption by the French has been falling for several decades.** — Cheats Paradox (2% of students)
Falling French fat levels imply higher past consumption — making low present heart disease more puzzling, not less.

**B. Other factors of diet besides high consumption of fat have not been similarly linked with incidence of heart disease.** — No Impact (7% of students)
Other factors not being linked to heart disease does not explain the French vs. North American gap.

**C. Heart disease takes years to develop and the average level of fat consumption in France increased to North American levels only a few years ago.** — Correct (86% of students)
Heart disease lags fat consumption, and French fat intake has only recently risen — so the disease has not caught up yet.

**D. Certain diseases other than heart disease have also been linked to average fat consumption, and the French have a higher incidence of these than do North Americans.** — No Impact (3% of students)
Other diseases do not explain the heart disease gap.

**E. Cigarette smoking significantly increases the risk of heart disease and France has a higher percentage of cigarette smokers in its population than does North America.** — Cheats Paradox (2% of students)
More French smokers means more heart disease risk, deepening rather than resolving the gap.

### Another Way
**Argument breakdown**
- **The Paradox:** Two facts that look like they cannot both be true at once: high fat consumption supposedly leads to heart disease, the French eat just as much fat as North Americans, but the French have half the heart disease.
- **Evaluate:** To resolve a paradox, we don't deny either fact. We find a third fact that lets both be true. The most natural way out: heart disease takes a long time to develop, and the French have only been eating that much fat for a short time. The disease is on its way; it just hasn't shown up yet.
- **Goal:** Find the answer that introduces a delay — a reason today's heart disease numbers reflect past, not current, fat consumption.

**Answer choices**
**A. The average level of fat consumption by the French has been falling for several decades.** — Cheats Paradox (2% of students)
Read this carefully. If French fat consumption is falling but is currently equal to North American levels, then it used to be even higher. So the French should be showing heart disease from those past high levels right about now. That deepens the puzzle instead of solving it.

**B. Other factors of diet besides high consumption of fat have not been similarly linked with incidence of heart disease.** — No Impact (7% of students)
This says no other diet factor has the same kind of link to heart disease. That doesn't help. Both groups still consume the same fat, and fat is supposedly the relevant factor — so the gap still has no explanation.

Here's the move. Heart disease takes a long time to develop. The French only recently caught up to North American fat consumption.

So North America has been eating high-fat for a long time, long enough for heart disease to show up in the population. The French only recently moved to that level, so their current heart disease rates still reflect the lower-fat past, not their current high-fat present.

Both facts hold at once: fat causes heart disease, the French eat as much fat now, and the French still have low heart disease — because the disease is on its way, just delayed. That is exactly how a paradox question should resolve.

**D. Certain diseases other than heart disease have also been linked to average fat consumption, and the French have a higher incidence of these than do North Americans.** — No Impact (3% of students)
This adds an interesting fact — the French get more of other fat-linked diseases — but it doesn't explain why they get fewer heart attacks. The question is specifically about heart disease, not about all fat-linked diseases combined.

**E. Cigarette smoking significantly increases the risk of heart disease and France has a higher percentage of cigarette smokers in its population than does North America.** — Cheats Paradox (2% of students)
This makes the paradox worse. France smokes more, smoking causes heart disease — so the French should have more heart disease than North Americans, not less. We need a fact that explains the lower French rate, and this gives a fact that should raise it.

The claim that journalistic investigation of the private lives of political leaders is an effort to improve society plays which one of the following roles in the argument?

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Journalistic investigation of political leaders' private lives is detrimental to society.
- **Evidence (and):** The practice makes public figures more concerned with mere appearances and makes everyone else cynical about the character of their leaders.
- **Evaluate:** We know when we read the argument on Main Conclusion or Role that we're most likely going to see an upside-down argument, where the conclusion comes earlier and the support comes later. In particular, we listen for First Sentence opinions from the author (usually those would be the Main Conclusion) or early Attributions (usually the conclusion would be a pivot/rebuttal from that idea).

  Here we start with an Attribution, so we'd anticipate a Pivot + Pushback conclusion. We get that with the second sentence. They said the tactic is an effort to improve society. The author is saying, "Well, it's not working. It's detrimental to society".

  She then supports that conclusion with a pair of premises, joined by "and".
- **Goal:** We're being asked about the opening claim, which is an Opposing idea.

  Our author isn't exactly refuting it, because it's a statement about what the tactic is attempting to do, and the author's conclusion is about what the tactic is actually doing.

  So we'll need to be more nuanced, like, "It describes a goal that the conclusion argues is not achieved".

The conclusion is that the efforts are NOT improving society, but refuting the opening claim would mean arguing that journalists aren't actually trying to improve society.

Is there an assertion that the argument concludes is false?

Not really. The conclusion isn't contradicting anything we heard asserted.

Is there any evidence that "this tactic is beneficial to society"?

Does the opening claim count as evidence?

No, not really.

It's definitely not presented as evidence. We might think that it's reasonable to say, "Action X leads to Effect Z. After all, Action X is an effort to bring about Effect Z."

There's nothing specific like that in the paragraph.

## S4 · Q12 — Weaken

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Ecologist: El Niño, a global weather phenomenon that occurs once every several years, is expected to become more frequent in coming decades due to the global warming caused by air pollution. In region T, El Niño causes heavy winter rainfall. Since rodent populations typically increase during long periods of sustained rain, it is likely that average rodent populations in region T will also increase in coming decades.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, would most weaken the ecologist's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Average rodent populations will increase in region T in the coming decades.
- **Evidence (since):** The argument suggests a chain of causation: air pollution causes global warming, which leads to more frequent El Nino's. More El Ninos cause increased heavy winter rainfall, leading to long periods of sustained rain, which typically increase rodent populations.
- **Evaluate:** This is a "Weaken" question, and the aim is to find an answer that undermines the prediction made in the conclusion. To weaken the argument, we need to disrupt the causal chain that links global warming and increased El Nino's to more rodents through heavy sustained rain.
- **Goal:** Identify the answer choice that effectively challenges any part of the causal chain, thus casting doubt on the prediction of increasing rodent populations.

**Answer choices**
**A. In region T, there is typically much less rainfall in summer than there is in winter.** — Irrelevant (1% of students)
This choice compares rain patterns between seasons in region T, but it doesn't affect the prediction about future rodent populations.

If there's more rain in winter, then we'd predict rodent populations to surge in winter, but this doesn't have anything to do with whether those surges will be bigger/smaller/same in coming decades.

**B. Rodent populations in region T often diminish during long periods in which there are no heavy rains.** — Opposite (if anything) (3% of students)
This "less rain, less rodents" type of answer seems to corroborate the argument's correlation between rodents and heavy rain. So if anything it would strengthen.

**C. In many regions that, on average, experience substantially more winter rainfall than region T does, average rodent populations are considerably lower than they are in region T.** — Unclear Impact (15% of students)
This seems somewhat promising, because it sounds like it's decoupling the argument's link between heavy rain and higher rodent populations.

But since we're comparing different regions, we don't know what other factors might be contributing to there being lower rodent populations in regions with more rain.

If rain is good for rodents, but snakes are bad for rodents, a region with lots of rain and lots of snakes could potentially have fewer rodents than a region with less rain but no snakes.

To undermine the rain/rodent link, we'd want to hear "within a given region, the rodent populations were lower when there was lots of rain than when there was much less rain".

**D. In region T, winters marked by relatively high rainfall have usually not been marked by long periods of sustained rain.** — Correct (73% of students)
We know that El Nino causes heavy winter rainfall, but the trigger the Evidence provides for a boost in rodent population is a long period of sustained rain (like it rains for 5 days straight), not just heavy winter rain.

This answer shows how coming decades could have heavier winter rainfall but not actually have any uptick in long periods of sustained rain.

This provides disconnect in the causal chain the author was selling us.

**E. The global warming caused by air pollution produces a number of effects, other than the increase in the frequency of El Niño, that could affect rodent populations.** — Unclear Impact (9% of students)
While stating that global warming could have other effects on rodent populations, it doesn't specify whether these effects would increase or decrease rodent numbers. Consequently, it creates uncertainty but doesn't directly weaken the argument since the net effect is unclear.
