# Section 2 (Logical Reasoning)

## S2 · Q1 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
CEO: While we only have the sales reports for the first 9 months of this year, I feel confident in concluding that this will be a good year for us in terms of sales. In each of the last 5 years, our monthly sales average was less than $30 million. However, our monthly sales average so far for this year is over $35 million.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the CEO's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** This will be a good year for us in terms of sales.
- **Evidence:** For the first 9 months of the year, our monthly sales average has been $5 million higher than our monthly sales average for the past 5 years.
- **Evaluate:** When we do Strengthen questions, we still think about how to object to the argument since so many correct answers are essentially ruling out objections.

  Given that the monthly sales average for the first 9 months of this year have been great, how could it still turn out to be a not-good year in sales?

  It seems like the only way the year could end up bad at this point is if the last 3 months are worse than usual (offsetting the fact that the first 9 months were better than usual).
- **Goal:** Look for an answer helping us to believe that the last 3 months will be average or better.

**Answer choices**
**A. The CEO's company typically has its highest monthly sales of the year during the last 3 months of the year.** — Correct (88% of students)
This helps us think that the remaining 3 months will be average or better. Since they are typically the juiciest months of the year, they will probably be at least as good as the last 9 months.

What this question is testing is seasonal variation of businesses and how that could potentially lead to distorted averages.

Let's say a florist was averaging $5000 a month last year, earning $60,000 for the year.

They might say, "hey, this year we made $20,000 in the first two months. We're averaging $10,000 per month, so we're gonna make $120,000 this year!"

Well it might be that last year they also made $20,000 in the first two months. After all, the second month of the year includes Valentine's Day, the holiday when florists make more money than at any other time during the year.

If a florist looked at their monthly average after Jan/Feb and then extrapolated that to the rest of the year, they might be grossly overestimating how much money they'll bring in, since the rest of the months of the year are way worse than Jan/Feb are.

Similarly, we could wonder with this argument whether the CEO's company usually has really low numbers during the last 3 months of the year. If so, she might be cherry-picking the busy 9 months to brag to her stockholders that their monthly average is way better than last year's monthly average.

But that would be because last year's monthly average is weighed down by the crappy final three months, while the 9-month average this year is only averaging together the "good" months.

This answer choice is ruling out that sort of objection, by saying, "Nah, the CEO isn't making a flattering monthly average by calculating the 9 busiest months of the year and leaving off the 3 worst performing months. To the contrary, the best is yet to come."

**B. The quality of the products sold by the CEO's company has always been considered to be relatively high.** — No Impact (0% of students)
Whether the quality was perceived to be high, medium, low, it wouldn't change our understanding of the fact that they are currently on pace to sell more than last year.

The only gap in our assessment of whether they will end up selling more than last year is what to expect out of these final 3 months.

**C. The CEO has a strong incentive to highlight any good news regarding the company and to downplay bad news.** — Weakens, if anything (0% of students)
This doesn't really weaken, but it insinuates the type of story we were talking about in choice (A), in which the CEO chooses to tout the monthly average now at the 9 month mark, rather than waiting for the year to finish.

She might be taking advantage of her knowledge that the last 3 months are underperforming months, so her monthly average will look like better news if she tabulates it from the 9 better months.

**D. The CEO's company started a new advertising campaign at the beginning of this year that has proved unusually effective so far.** — Too Weak (8% of students)
This isn't nothing, but since this ad campaign is now 9 months old, it's unlikely that it's still having much effect on consumer habits (common sense tells us that ad campaigns probably have the most effect in the first couple months of their existence).

In order for this to strengthen, we'd have to assume that the ad campaign will keep us holding steady for the last 3 months, but the answer specifies that it's been unusually effective so far, so we're not expecting its success / effectiveness to continue.

**E. Several other companies who sell products similar to those sold by the CEO's company have also reported that this year's monthly sales averages so far have been higher than previous years' averages.** — Too Weak (3% of students)
This also seems to strengthen somewhat, since it suggests that the overall market for whatever products this company sells is a product with high demand right now.

Ultimately, (D) and (E) just don't do as much to strengthen because of how important those final 3 months are to our assessment of how the year will turn out.

Both of these somewhat increase the plausibility that things will hold steady over the final 3 months, but neither of them speak to the final 3 months specifically, so they are less compelling than (A) is.

## S2 · Q2 — Point at Issue (Disagreement)

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Javier: Government workers are paid higher hourly wages than comparable private sector employees. So the government could save money by hiring private contractors to perform services now performed by government employees.

Mykayla: An analysis of government contracts showed that, on average, the government paid substantially more to hire contractors than it would have cost for government employees to perform comparable services.

### Prompt
Javier and Mykayla disagree with each other over whether

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Javier's 1st claim:** Govt workers are paid higher hourly wages than comparable private sector employees.

  *Does Mykayla say that private sector employees are paid at least as much as govt employees for comparable work?*

  No, not in a sweeping way, although she would say that private sector employees currently being contracted by the government are paid at least as much as government employees would be for that work.
- **Javier's 2nd claim:** The govt could save money by hiring private contractors to perform services now performed by government employees.

  *Does Mykayla say that the government wouldn't be saving money if they were to hire private contractors to perform services currently done by government employees?*

  Yes, she's making that objection. She's saying, "in terms of the current private contracting the government does, that's not the case". They're paying more for contractors than they would to pay government employees to perform comparable services.
- **Goal:** Let's look for "whether or not the govt would save money by hiring contractors to do the work currently done by government employees" or saying "whether or not the people the government currently contracts get paid less than government employees would to do the same work".

**Answer choices**
**A. the government could reduce spending by reducing the number of employees on its payroll** — Out of Scope: reduce employees on payroll (0% of students)
Both people would presumably agree to the truth of this common sense claim. If you reduce your payroll, you're almost invariably reducing your spending (all other things being equal).

We wouldn't want to equate "reduce number of employees on payroll" with "hire private contractors to do the work currently being done by the government".

**B. the government would save money if it hired private contractors to perform services now performed by government employees** — Correct (96% of students)
Javier would clearly say YES to this claim, since it is his second sentence.

And Mykayla would seemingly say NO to this claim, since she is saying "currently, that's not the case. The government is not saving money by hiring private contractors. In fact, the government is spending more money on these private contractors than they would to pay government employees to perform comparable work.

**C. government workers generally are paid higher hourly wages than comparable private sector workers** — Too Strong: generally / Unsupported Person 2 (2% of students)
Javier would agree with this because it is his first sentence. But Mykayla never gets into the hourly wage comparison between government employees and comparable private sector ones.

She only says that what we currently spend on private contracting (only a tiny percentage of the private sector is working for government contracts) is more than what it would cost for that work to be done by government employees.

But we have no idea if she means that because she thinks that government workers, more than 50% of the time (generally), have a lower hourly wage.

**D. every service that is currently performed by government employees could be performed by private contractors** — Too Strong: every (0% of students)
Neither author has committed to agreeing to the extreme claim that "every single thing that government employees can do can be performed by private contractors".

Given that this claim is only about what's possible, they're likely to agree on this anyway. The disagreement isn't about what's possible, it's about what's more cost-effective.

**E. the total amount of money that the government pays its employees annually is greater than the total amount that it spends on contractors annually** — Out of Scope: total amount (1% of students)
Javier hasn't made any comments that would tell us what he believes about comparing total amounts of money spent. He was only talking about comparing hourly wages.

Mykayla has only talked about comparing the total amount of money the government would spend to have its employees do the work currently done by private contractors, to the total amount of money being paid to those private contractors.

She hasn't talked about the total amount of money the government pays (all of) its employees annually.

## S2 · Q3 — Strengthen (Explain a Curious Fact)

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Biologist: DNA analysis shows that Acacia heterophylla, a tree native to the Indian Ocean's Réunion Island, is descended from the Hawaiian tree Acacia koa. Some think this occurred because A. koa seeds floated from Hawaii to Réunion, but that explanation is implausible, since the seeds will not germinate after being soaked in seawater. Moreover, both trees grow in the mountains, not near the shore. Seabirds sometimes carry seeds great distances, so they probably caused the dispersal.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the support for the biologist's hypothesis?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Curious Fact:** How did A. heterophylla, a descendant of a tree (A. koa) from the Hawaiian islands end up on an island in the Indian Ocean?
- **Hypothesis:** Seabirds carried A. koa seeds from Hawaii to the Reunion Island in the Indian Ocean.
- **Evaluate:** With this Explain Curious Fact framework, we're always asking the same two questions:
1. could there be some Alternate Explanation for the curious fact?
2. how plausible is the Author's Explanation?

  While the correct answer on Flaw, Weaken, Evaluate, and Necessary Assumption is much more likely to deal with #1, the correct answer on Strengthen is more likely to deal with #2.
- **Goal:** Primarily look for something that increases the plausibility that seabirds managed to take seeds from this Hawaiian tree all the way across the Pacific ocean to an island in the Indian Ocean.

  (Secondarily keep an eye out for an answer that rules out an Alternate Explanation for how the seeds got there.)

**Answer choices**
**A. A. koa branches carrying seeds can remain afloat for prolonged periods of time.** — Weakens, if anything (1% of students)
We would either say this does nothing to add plausibility to the seabird hypothesis, or we might even say that this strengthens the Alternate Hypothesis that the seeds could have potentially floated from Hawaii to Reunion.

The author rejected it on the basis that, "If the seeds got wet, they wouldn't be able to grow on Reunion", but this answer is making it sound like maybe the seeds could make it there without getting wet? Maybe the tree branch could stay afloat long enough that some of the seeds would stay dry?

**B. There are mountain-nesting seabird species common to Réunion and Hawaii.** — Correct (97% of students)
This adds plausibility to the seabird hypothesis by telling us that there are bird species who live in both Reunion and Hawaii (thus, there might be a migratory pathway that seabirds take from one place to the other).

When this biologist first says her hypothesis, "Maybe seabirds took the seeds from Hawaii to Reunion?", we can imagine a skeptic saying, "Bah! Are there even birds that can make that trip? Do seabirds ever fly from Hawaii to Reunion?"

This answer is providing a clue that makes it sound like maybe there are seabird species making the trek back and forth.

**C. A. koa is thought to be descended from Acacia melanoxylon, a tree native to eastern Australia.** — No Impact (1% of students)
This does nothing to increase the plausibility of the hypothesis that seabirds took seeds of A. koa from Hawaii to Reunion.

This is just telling us an origin story for A. koa, which doesn't have any bearing on our conversation.

**D. Ocean currents have sometimes carried buoyant objects from Hawaii to Réunion.** — Weakens, if anything (0% of students)
This answer seems to support the Alternate Hypothesis that seeds floated from Hawaii to Reunion, because it's establishing the possibility of something floating from Hawaii to Reunion.

This doesn't do anything to make us think that seabirds carried the seeds from Hawaii to Reunion.

**E. Many seabird species return to land very infrequently, and often do so only to breed.** — Weakens, if anything (1% of students)
This seems to somewhat hurt the hypothesis that seabirds took seeds from Hawaii to Reunion, because that would be a land-to-land trip, and this answer is saying that many seabird species very rarely return to land.

## S2 · Q4 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Although smaller class sizes are popular with parents and teachers, the evidence shows that large scale reductions in class size lead to only slight improvements in student performance. Because school finances are limited, the cost-benefit test that any educational policy must pass is not "Does this policy have any positive effect?" but rather "Is there a more productive use of education dollars?" So public funds would in fact be better spent on efforts to recruit and retain better teachers.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Public funds would be better spent on efforts to recruit and retain better teachers than on smaller class size.
- **Evidence:** Smaller class size is know to lead to only slight improvements in student performance.
- **Evaluation:** This seems to be testing a pretty straightforward assumption that "the author assumes the thing she's recommending is better than the thing she's not recommending".

  However, "better" is too fuzzy. When LSAT is testing this concept, we need to dial in on exactly what "better" means in this argument.

  For example, if I said, "Don't Kathy that watch. If you're trying to impress her parents, you should get that vase instead", then I'm assuming that "vase is better than watch". But what I'm really assuming is "vase more likely to impress Kathy's parents than watch".

  In this argument, the author is saying "better teachers is better than smaller class sizes", for what reason? What advantage does finding/retaining better teachers supposedly have?

  The advantage brought by smaller class size is "only slight improvements in student performance" -- so our author must be assuming that the advantages brought by finding/retaining better teachers is greater than "only slight improvements in student performance".
- **Goal:** Find an answer saying "recruiting and retaining better teachers would have more benefit than the slight improvement in student performance we would get from smaller class sizes".

**Answer choices**
**A. Reducing class size is an extremely expensive type of educational reform measure.** — Too Strong: extremely expensive (6% of students)
The author certainly believes that there is some expense involved in reducing class size, because she brings up the cost-benefit consideration of limited finances.

But she doesn't need to assume it's extremely expensive. Even moderately expensive would still justify her argument that there might be better ways to spend that precious money.

**B. Dollar for dollar, efforts to recruit and retain good teachers yield larger improvements in student performance than do reductions in class size.** — Correct (92% of students)
This is just affirming that "the option the author is endorsing is better than the option the author is rejecting".

It says "dollar for dollar" because the whole argument is couched in terms of how to make the best use of limited finances.

Our author is assuming, for a given amount of money, we'd get more benefit by recruiting/retaining good teachers than by reducing class size.

If we negated this, it would surely weaken (as must every correct answer on Necessary Assumption), for it would be saying that "dollar for dollar, you get larger improvements from reducing class size than from getting and keeping good teachers".

**C. Because reducing class size is a popular policy, it is much easier to get public approval for that policy than for most other educational policies.** — Unrelated to Goal / Out of Scope: getting public approval / Too Strong: much easier / most (0% of students)
This has nothing to do with the Conclusion, which is about recruiting/retaining better teachers, so it's definitely not related to our goal.

The concept of "getting approval" has never been discussed. The author certainly hasn't committed herself to a strongly worded claim like, "getting public approval to reduce class size is much easier than over 50% of other educational policies".

**D. Reducing class size is the most cost effective way to recruit and retain good teachers.** — Too Strong: most effective way (1% of students)
This conclusion is Comparative, not Superlative. It says that public funds would be better spent on Y than on X. The author doesn't need to assume that recruiting/retaining teachers is #1. She just has to assume it beats out reducing class sizes.

I can say "Harvey would be better off going to University of Texas than to University of Arizona for law school", and that doesn't mean I believe that University of Texas is the best law school.

**E. In practice, it is difficult to identify what would be the most productive use of education dollars.** — Out of Scope: difficult to identify / Too Strong: most productive (0% of students)
The author is never discussing "the most productive" use of education dollars. She is definitely encouraging us to always ask, "Is there a better way to spend our money", but she hasn't encouraged us to ask, "Is this the absolute #1 most productive way to spend our money".

She definitely hasn't offered any commentary suggesting her beliefs on how easy / difficult it would be to identify that #1 use of money.

## S2 · Q5 — Most Strongly Supported (Inference)

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Seasonal allergy symptoms are the immune system's response to pollen in the air. When large amounts of pollen are inhaled, it can trigger an inflammatory response that causes allergy symptoms. While there are medicines that minimize those symptoms, a more effective—though often impractical—strategy is simply to stay indoors on dry, windy days during allergy season.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Information:** - causal (response to): Pollen in the air → Seasonal allergy symptoms
- causal (it can trigger): Large amounts of pollen inhaled → inflammatory response → allergy symptoms
- causal (effective strategy): Staying indoors on dry/windy days → minimize allergy symptoms
- **Evaluate:** There's a lot of causal stuff going on here. When it's allergy season, there's more pollen in the air. When large amounts of pollen are inhaled, it triggers allergy symptoms.

  To avoid being triggered in this way, one can stay in indoors on dry, windy days.

  We can infer a Flip the Causal Difference-Maker type idea: "Since staying indoors prevents the allergy symptoms from occurring, it sounds like when you stay indoors you don't inhale large amounts of pollen."
- **Goal:** Let's stay flexible, guard against answers that are Too Strong / Out of Scope, and pick the most provable answer.

**Answer choices**
**A. Medicines that minimize seasonal allergy symptoms prevent people from inhaling large amounts of pollen.** — Too Strong: prevent (0% of students)
First of all, this makes zero common sense: a medicine that prevents you from inhaling something?

Medicines don't have the power to select what you do or don't breathe.

If medicine prevented people from inhaling large amounts of pollen, then it would be totally effective as a way to avoid allergy symptoms. Since we're told that medicine can minimize symptoms but a more effective strategy is staying inside, we don't have any grounds to think that medicine keeps the pollen from ever entering your body.

**B. Most types of pollen do not cause seasonal allergy symptoms.** — Too Strong: most (0% of students)
We don't know anything about any different types of pollen, so we have no means to say that anything is true of more than 50% of pollen types.

**C. People who stay indoors on dry, windy days during allergy season are unlikely to inhale large amounts of pollen.** — Correct (99% of students)
This sounds like the Flip the Causal Difference-Maker inference we predicted.

We were told that inhaling large amounts of pollen triggers allergy symptoms.

And we were told that staying indoors is a good way to avoid allergy symptoms. Thus, we can infer that staying indoors is a good way to avoid inhaling large amounts of pollen.

It would be like saying, "When Bob uses the computer in his office late at night, he tends to spend too much money via online shopping. One effective way we can cut down on wasteful online shopping is to shut the door to his office in the evening."

The implication is that "if he shuts the door to his office, he won't end up using his computer late at night".

If X causes Y, and Z is a good way to avoid Y, then Z is a good way to avoid X.

**D. People who take medicines that minimize seasonal allergy symptoms tend to stay indoors on dry, windy days during allergy season.** — Too Strong: tend to (1% of students)
We don't know anything when it comes to people who take meds for their allergies, other than that some of them have their symptoms minimized by taking such meds.

We can't say that on more than 50% of dry, windy days during allergy season, people who take allergy meds stay indoors.

In fact the passage told us that it's often 'impractical" to try to stay indoors all day, so we have a reason to think that people aren't tending to do this.

**E. People who experience seasonal allergy symptoms typically use air filters that remove pollen from the air in their homes.** — Out of Scope: air filters (0% of students)
We never talked about "air filters" at all, so we can't just totally speculate on something for which we have no information.

## S2 · Q6 — Main Conclusion

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Saturn's moon Enceladus has a rocky core and an icy surface. Between these two layers, there must be a lake of liquid water. The Cassini space probe was used to measure the density of matter composing Enceladus. These measurements revealed something denser than ice between the core and surface of Enceladus, and that could only be liquid water.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion drawn in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (must be):** There must be a lake of liquid water between these two layers of Enceladus (the rocky core and the icy surface).
- **Evidence (and):** The Cassini space probe measured the density of Enceladus: between the rocky core and the icy surface is something denser than ice, and that could only be liquid water.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we do Main Conclusion (or Role), we prime our brain before we read the paragraph with the reminder that the Conclusion is almost always earlier than the Supporting ideas. In fact, a ton of the time the Conclusion will be the very first claim. We read these listening for the first opinion we hear from the author.

  The word must is being used to convey opinion in the second sentence. It's a weird word, because in conditional logic contexts it means factual necessity. But in "Solve the Mystery / Explain the Curious Fact" contexts, must is used to convey that speculative use of must that we use.

  Pretend you were hosting a party, you left to go to the bathroom, and when you came back you see that two of your friends (a married couple) left without saying goodbye. Your brain would want to Solve the mystery / Explain the Curious Fact.

  You might scratch your temple, confused, and say, "Huh ... they must have gotten a call from their babysitter saying that something was wrong with little Avery."

  That's the speculative "must" being used in the 2nd sentence of this paragraph. We would hear more naturally as announcing the author's causal hypothesis if it occurred at the end of the paragraph:

  We probed Enceladus. We found that there's something in between the ice layer and the rocky layer. It's denser than ice. Liquid water is only plausible thing that could be there that's denser than ice. Thus, there must be a lake between the ice layer and rock layer.

  So one thing assisting our ability to find this conclusion is our knowledge that the Conclusion almost always occurs earlier than the Support (on Main Conclusion and Role questions). Another thing helping us is our awareness that must gets used to convey a speculative causal hypothesis (i.e. an opinion from the author).

  Finally, if the two claims are joined by the word "and", then they are almost always both Supporting ideas. The "and" is showing us that these ideas work together, they blend to let us derive the Conclusion.
- **Goal:** We just want to replicate the meaning of the 2nd sentence. On Main Conclusion, we make sure to import "borrowed language", if there are any pronouns or implied nouns.

  On Enceladus, there is a lake of liquid water between the rocky core layer and the icy surface layer.

**Answer choices**
**A. Saturn's moon Enceladus has a rocky core and an icy surface.** — Background Fact (1% of students)
This is the 1st sentence. We're looking for an answer that says the 2nd sentence.

The fact that this sentence is presented as fact means that it would not be our author's Main Conclusion, which will be an opinion.

**B. There must be a lake of liquid water between the rocky core and the icy surface of Enceladus.** — Correct (89% of students)
This paraphrases the 2nd sentence.

**C. The Cassini space probe was used to measure the density of Enceladus.** — Background Fact (0% of students)
This is the 3rd claim. We're looking for an answer that says the 2nd sentence.

Also, the fact that this claim is presented as fact means that it would not be our author's Main Conclusion, which will be an opinion.

**D. Density measurements reveal something denser than ice between the core and surface of Enceladus.** — Premise (1% of students)
This is the 4th claim, appearing in the 3rd sentence. We're looking for an answer that says the 2nd sentence.

The fact that this sentence is a measurement, thus a fact, means that it would not be our author's Main Conclusion, which will be an opinion.

**E. Anything denser than ice between the core and surface of Enceladus would have to be liquid water.** — Premise / Last-Claim Trap (9% of students)
This is the final claim, which will not be where we find the Conclusion on most Main Conclusion questions. The conclusion was the 2nd sentence.

This is a premise, which we can surmise because it's prefaced by the word "and", which almost always connects two supporting ideas.

We can also tell it's not the conclusion, because no support is offered for it. Why should we believe that anything denser than ice would have to be liquid water?

They didn't ever support that idea, so it can't be a conclusion.

## S2 · Q7 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Thousands of fossils from the long-extinct dire wolf have been found in a cluster of natural tar pits in which animals became trapped and were preserved for millennia. None of these fossils came from dire wolf pups under six months old. Pups under six months old, therefore, probably did not accompany adults that were scavenging or hunting.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Pups under six months old probably didn't accompany adults as they were hunting or scavenging.
- **Evidence:** We found a ton of dire wolf fossils that got stuck in some tar pits, but none of them were pups under six months old.
- **Evaluation:** Whenever the conclusion has a new term that isn't discussed in the evidence, that new term will usually be in the correct answer.

  Here, the new term was "scavenging or hunting". What is the author assuming about scavenging or hunting?

  He's explicitly concluding that there would be adults, but wouldn't be pups.

  And his evidence was about fossils found in tarpits where there were adults, but not pups.

  So he must be assuming that "the group of fossils found at the tar pits came from dire wolves who were scavenging / hunting".
- **Goal:** Look for an answer saying that "the dire wolves got trapped in the tar pits while scavenging or hunting", or an answer ruling out the possibility that these tar pits were closer to home (where the pups would be).

**Answer choices**
**A. Dire wolf pups under six months old would not have contributed to the adults' success in scavenging or hunting.** — Out of Scope: success (9% of students)
The author is only trying to prove that the pups didn't hunt or scavenge, not that they shouldn't have.

It doesn't hurt the author's argument to say, "the pups would have contributed to the adults' success". The author would be like, "cool, that may be true, but it doesn't look like the pups did hunt or scavenge."

**B. If a dire wolf pup under six months old became trapped in a tar pit, it would be better able to pull itself free than an adult dire wolf would.** — Opposite (2% of students)
The author is definitely never comparing the ability of a pup vs. an adult to get out of a tar pit.

If the author actually believed that pups had an easier time freeing themselves, then THAT could have been the reason why we found adults but not pups trapped in the tar pits.

This would actually be an alternate explanation for why there are no pups in the pits, which would weaken the author's argument (that there are no pups because this was a hunting/scavenging expedition that wouldn't have included pups).

**C. Before the dire wolf became extinct, more dire wolves became trapped in the tar pits than did any other animal species.** — Too Strong: more than any other / Out of Scope: other species (0% of students)
The author in no way cares where dire wolves rank on the "Top 100 animals who get stuck in tar pits".

**D. The entrapment of dire wolves in the tar pits most frequently occurred when those animals were scavenging or hunting.** — Correct (84% of students)
The only way that the author can go from "we didn't find pups in the pits" to "I guess the pups didn't come on hunting/scavenging" trips is for him to think that "getting trapped in the pits means that you were on a hunting / scavenging trip".

The "most frequently occurred" matches with the "probably" in the conclusion.

**E. For the dire wolves that lived nearby, the tar pits were a favorite location for scavenging and hunting.** — Too Strong: favorite location (5% of students)
It doesn't make any difference to this argument whether the tar pits are a favorite location, a mediocre location, or a despised location.

As long as it was a location they sometimes used for scavenging or hunting, the argument can work.

The author is assuming "for the dire wolves that lived nearby, the tar pits were near a location for scavenging and hunting."

## S2 · Q8 — Flaw

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Information:** Since we read Must Be True looking primarily for Conditional, Math-y, or Causal ideas, we would be most attracted to the two Most claims.

  Most NA mammal species are not rodent species, but most individual mammals in NA are rodents.
- **Evaluate:** There are a couple famous inferences we can make with two Most statements, but this example is neither of them. (Here they are, for reference, though)

  - Most A's are B / Most A's are C → infer: Some B's are C
- Most A's are B / Most A's are C → infer: Most B's are ~A / more B's than A's

  If women are responsible for a minority of tables at the restaurant but a majority of people at the restaurant, then there must be more women sitting per table.

  10 tables with 5 women each = 50 women
12 tables with 4 men each = 48 men

  Out of the 22 tables, most of them aren't female tables (10 of 22), but out of the 98 individuals, most of them are females (50 of 98).
- **Goal:** Similarly, if most species aren't rodents, but most individuals are rodents, then there must be more rodents per rodent species than there are non-rodents per non-rodent species.

  (Just like there were more women per female-table than there were men per male-table ... also, my apologies to womankind for making them the rodents in this math metaphor)

**Answer choices**

We know that rodents have chisel-like incisor teeth, but we're also told that most species of NA mammals aren't rodents.

If we knew that most species were rodents, we could infer this answer.

**B. In North America, rodent species tend to have more individual members than other species of mammals have.** — Correct (71% of students)
This is giving us the mathematical inference we predicted: if most species are not rodent, but most individuals are rodent, then there must be more rodents per rodent species than there are non-rodents per non-rodent species.

If most of the vehicles at a drive-in were trucks, but most of the people at the drive-in arrived in a car, then you could infer that there tended to be more passengers per car than there were per truck.

**C. Most species of mammals that have chisel-like incisor teeth can be found in North America.** — Unknown Group (3% of students)
We don't have any information about 51% or more of "mammals with chisel-like incisor teeth".

We know about most "NA mammal species" and most "NA individual mammals", and those are the only most groups we know about.

**D. Of the mammal species in North America, the one with the most individual members is a species of rodent.** — Too Specific: the single #1 highest (19% of students)
In each of our two math metaphors so far (male/female tables at restaurant ... or people who came to the drive-in via truck vs. car), we only know something about the overall average.

We can't get as specific as one specific table, one specific vehicle.

In other words, there can definitely be an outlier among males / trucks / non-rodents.

Our inference is only about the overall total vs. number of groups, so our inference has to be expressed as an average of individuals per group.

**E. Most nonrodent mammal species can be found in North America.** — Unknown Group (1% of students)
We don't have any information about 51% or more of "nonrodent mammals".

We know about most "NA mammal species" and most "NA individual mammals", and those are the only most groups we know about. You can't ever derive a fact about "most X" if you were never told a fact about "most X".

This is shown by the fact that after 1973 the profits of oil companies showed large increases, as did the incomes of oil-producing countries.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the commentator's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The oil crisis of 1973 wasn't due to the shortage of oil but rather the collusion between oil companies and oil-producing countries to restrict supply in order to profit from higher prices.
- **Evidence (this is shown by the fact that):** After 1973, the profits of oil companies showed large increases, as did the incomes of oil-producing countries.
- **Evaluate:** Given that ... the profits of oil companies and oil-producing countries rose greatly after 1973, How could we argue that ... the oil crisis of 1973 was caused by a real shortage of oil, and wasn't just the result of collusion?

  Well, let's start by supposing there was a shortage of oil. As supply shrinks relative to demand, the price of oil would rise. People used to pay $1 / gallon and now they're paying $3 / gallon, as a reflection of demand outpacing supply.

  Meanwhile, Profit = Revenue - Expenses

  If people are paying higher prices due to short supply, then the revenue per gallon has increased. Have the expenses of the oil producers increased?

  There's not necessarily any reason to think expenses went up, unless it's common sense that when oil is in short supply, oil producers have to spend more money to extract/refine/distribute it.

  So it's reasonable to think that if gas prices rise due to short supply (while expenses of producing gas stay pretty stable), then that would cause the profit margin on gas to go up all on its own.

  Would that explain the profits of oil companies and oil-producing countries going up after 1973? Maybe so, maybe not. Since oil was in short supply, the overall total gas being sold is lower.   Still, it's coherent to argue that depending on how much revenue and expenses did / didn't go up, a short supply on its own could explain an increase in profits.

  But we would probably guess that what LSAT really wants to test is the famous Causal reasoning flaw. The author is presenting a Curious Fact premise: "why did profits of oil companies/countries show large increases after 1973?"

  One possible explanation is that the companies/countries were colluding to artificially jack up the price in order to boost profits. But that's not necessarily the only possible explanation, so it's flawed for the author to be so sure of her causal interpretation of these events.

**Answer choices**
**A. fails to consider the possibility that a party can benefit from an event without helping to bring about that event** — Correct (86% of students)
When a Flaw answer begins with fails to consider / overlooks the possibility, we can ask ourselves whether the following idea would be an objection.

Could we say, "Hey, author -- sometimes a party can benefit from an event without helping to bring about that event"?

Sure! That maps onto this conversation pretty easily. "Hey, author, isn't it possible that oil companies/countries benefited from the higher prices of oil (brought about by a real shortage of oil) without helping to bring about the higher prices of oil (via collusion and artificial price inflation)?"

This answer reflects our objection that, "Just because someone benefited from something doesn't mean they caused it."

Just because Clorox got rich off sanitizing wipes during the covid pandemic doesn't mean that Clorox hosted a bunch of super-spreader events or actively contributed to the misinformation campaign that facilitated the spread of covid.

When answers begin with takes for granted / presumes / fails to establish, we can ask ourselves whether the author needs to assume this.

Just like in Necessary Assumption, tons of answers on Flaw are wrong because they accuse the author of assuming something way stronger than anything the author seemed to be thinking.

The author only mentions oil companies/countries benefiting from the 1973 oil crisis, but does that mean the author assumes only oil companies/countries benefited?

Of course not. That's like saying that when someone says, "Black lives matter" they are assuming "only Black lives matter".

This answer refers to the famous Equivocation flaw, in which the author uses the same term or concept twice in the argument, but each usage means something different.

While there are multiple definitions to "profit" (it could mean "revenue was greater than expenses" or it could mean "provides benefit", as in She profited from the wisdom of her grandfather), this argument is only using profit in the fiscal sense of making money.

**D. fails to establish that there was a worldwide oil surplus prior to the crisis of 1973** — Not Assumed: surplus (3% of students)
When answers begin with takes for granted / presumes / fails to establish, we can ask ourselves whether the author needs to assume this.

Did the author need to assume that prior to the oil crisis in 1973, there was an oil surplus?

No. The author is assuming there wasn't a shortage, but that doesn't mean he's assuming there was a surplus. It doesn't have to be one or the other. There can be a "balanced budget" between supply and demand. That's when prices end up being fairly constant.

**E. fails to consider the possibility that events that occur simultaneously can be causally related** — Irrelevant Objection (8% of students)
When answers begin with fails to consider / overlooks the possibility, we can ask ourselves whether the idea that follows would be a weakening objection.

Could we say, "Hey, author --- events that occur simultaneously could be causally related"?

That doesn't match up with the conversation at all. Were there simultaneous events described?

Not really. There was an oil crisis in 1973. And then after 1973, the profits of oil companies/countries went up.

Those are consecutive, not simultaneous. Furthermore, the author is thinking those two are causally related.

He just thinks the relationship isn't "real shortage caused crisis, which caused higher profits".

He thinks "collusion caused artificial shortage, which caused crisis, which caused higher profits".

- **Evidence:** Caffeinated sodas cause people to excrete more calcium. Calcium deficiency can make bones more brittle (presumably making them easier to break).
- **Evaluate:** This is the classic Curious Fact / Explanation template we expect on lots of Strengthen / Weaken questions.

  Curious Fact: Why do teenagers who drink caffeinated sodas have a higher incidence of broken bones?

  Author's Story: The caffeinated soda drains their calcium, leading to calcium deficiency, leading to brittle bones, leading to more easily broken bones.

  We always address these with the same two pressure points:
- Is there some OTHER STORY that could explain the Curious Fact?
- How plausible is the AUTHOR'S STORY?
- **Goal:** Since this is Weaken, we know to prioritize thinking about OTHER STORIES.

  How else could we explain the correlation between caffeinated soda and broken bones? Our customary go-to alternative explanations for correlations are Reverse Causality and Third Factor.

  Could it be that breaking bones came first and caffeinated sodas came next? Maybe. Unlikely, but maybe teens who break their bones become sedentary and sleepier and start drinking caffeinated sodas more to pep themselves up.

  Could there be some Third Factor that really caused the broken bones and just happens to go along with the caffeinated sodas? Sure, there's the whole Mountain Dew X-Games angle. The kids most likely to drink caffeinated sodas are the ones who most frequently engage in physically dangerous activities like skateboarding / snowboarding.

  On a first pass prioritize looking for a different Causal Story, but we could also use an answer that disrupts the plausibility of the author's storyline: first they drank caffeinated soda, then calcium deficiency, then brittle bones.

**Answer choices**
**A. Teenagers who drink large quantities of carbonated beverages containing caffeine tend to drink smaller quantities of calcium-rich beverages than other teenagers do.** — Correct (48% of students)
This gives us a Third Factor alternate explanation.

It's not the caffeinated soda that's causing these teens to have lower calcium and more brittle bones than their peers, it's the fact that they drink smaller quantities of calcium-rich beverages.

You have teens drinking Mountain Dew, and other teens drinking milk. This answer is saying that the primary reason the Mountain Dew kids have more broken bones isn't that their MTN Dew drains their calcium but rather that they didn't have a milk-boosted supply of calcium to start with.

Technically, we know the caffeinated soda drinking still contributes to lower calcium, but we now have a way to argue it's not the primary reason for their broken bones.

**B. Teenagers engage in the types of activities that carry a high risk of causing broken bones much more often than both older and younger people do.** — Wrong Comparison (29% of students)
For this to be out answer it would need to say "Teenagers who drink caffeinated sodas engage in more broken bone activities than those who don't."

**C. Some teenagers have calcium deficiencies even though they do not consume any caffeine.** — Too Weak (12% of students)
The word "some" is wrong at least 90% of the time you see it in Strengthen, Weaken, Paradox since it's so weak.

This says that there is at least one teenager who has a calcium deficiency even though they don't drink coffee. Cool. His name is Jeff, and he's sitting right here. "Sorry, Jeff, but you're irrelevant to this argument".

Arguments about correlations and averages leave plenty of room for exceptions, but LSAT always writes this type of trap answer to bait people into objecting, "But that's not true 100% of the time!" You're right, but the author agrees with you. The author never said it was 100% of the time.

**D. Some of the less popular carbonated beverages contain even more caffeine than the more popular ones.** — Irrelevant Comparison: popularity / Too Weak (0% of students)
This is again super dubious given that it says "Some". There's no impact on this argument by saying, "There is at least one less popular caffeinated soda that has even more caffeine than more popular ones".

How does that help us argue that caffeinated soda is not the primary reason that teens who drink caffeinated have a higher rate of broken bones?

Saying that "some brands have even more caffeine" doesn't allow us to think "oh, the brands that the teens are drinking don't REALLY have caffeine".

**E. The more calcium a person ingests as a regular part of his or her diet, the more calcium that person will tend to excrete.** — No Impact (10% of students)
The more water I drink, the more I'll pee. What does this have to do with assessing the putative causal connection between caffeinated soda and broken bones?

The people who make more money, spend more money, but they still end up with more money than the person who was low on money to begin with.

So people who eat more calcium excrete more calcium, but still end up being someone with higher calcium (we assume).

### Stimulus
Philosopher: Groups are not the type of entity that can be worthy of praise or blame. Blameworthiness implies conscience and agency. Nations do not have consciences. Families are not agents. Which one of the following most accurately describes the role played in the philosopher's argument by the claim that nations do not have consciences?

**Argument breakdown**
- **Main Conclusion:** If you're trying to praise or blame a group, you need to cast that statement in terms of individuals, if we're gonna properly evaluate your claim.
- **Intermediate Conclusion:** Groups are not a type of entity that you can praise or blame.
- **Evidence:** Blaming something requires that the thing has conscience and agency (an ability to decide for themselves).

  Nations don't have consciences. Families are not agents.
- **Evaluate:** On Role questions, it's unusual for the last claim of the paragraph to be the Main Conclusion (only about 10% of the time), but we know it's a conclusion from the fact that it's prefaced by Hence.

  To figure out the relationship between those two conclusions (the first and last claim) we can use the Support Test. Which one would be a supporting idea for the other?

  why should we believe that groups aren't the sort of entity you can praise/blame?

  why should we believe that if we want to praise or blame a group, we have to translate that into a statement about individuals, or else we can't evaluate it properly?

  This is how we know the first claim is subordinate to the last claim.

Why should I believe that ... nations do not have consciences?

There isn't any premise offered for that.

Main Conc: praise or blame for a group should be translated into statements about individuals.

An instance of that would be like, "for instance, if you're trying to praise New Zealand for its handling of COVID, you should really name specific individuals that deserve praise."

**E. It is the main conclusion drawn in the argument.** — Wrong Role (1% of students)
The final claim is the main conclusion.

## S2 · Q17 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Psychiatrist: Psychological stress is known both to cause negative emotions and to impair physical health. This suggests that overcoming such negative emotions when they arise could cause one's health to improve.

### Prompt
The psychiatrist's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on which one of the following grounds?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Overcoming negative emotions could cause one's health to improve.
- **Evidence:** Psychological stress is known both to cause negative emotions and to impair physical health.
- **Evaluate:** Normally, when we see a causal conclusion, there was a correlation between X and Y in the evidence. The author overconfidently concludes that X causes Y, and we consider alternate explanations such as,
- Reverse Causality (maybe Y causes X?)
- Third Factor (maybe X and Y are both caused by Z?)

  In this argument, though, the evidence also had a causal claim. It's almost as though this argument started by establishing that there is a Third Factor, Z, psychological stress, that causes X and Y (negative emotions and impaired physical health).

  The author bizarrely assumes that X causes Y, out of this love triangle.
- **Goal:** We could point out that the author's thinking, "If two things are both effects of the same cause, one of those thing is causing the other thing."

  We could raise an objection that, "even if X and Y are both symptoms of underlying condition Z, changing X might have no effect on Y."

**Answer choices**
**A. It presumes without justification that two conditions that together have a certain effect causally influence one another.** — Bad Premise Match (4% of students)
This is very close, but the two conditions (negative emotions and impaired physical health) are not conditions that together have a certain effect. Instead, they are both effects of the same cause.

This answer is saying "since negative emotions combined with bad health, often result in psychological stress, overcoming negative emotions could cause improved health."

**B. It presumes, merely on the basis that two conditions have a common cause, that one of these two conditions can causally influence the other.** — Correct (76% of students)
Any answer structured, "Presumes, merely on the basis of X, that Y", makes us check whether X matches the evidence and Y matches the conclusion.

Was the premise that two conditions have a common cause?

Yes. Negative emotions and bad physical health are both caused by psychological stress.

Does the conclusion act like one of those two conditions can causally influence the other?

Yes. The conclusion says that getting rid of negative emotions could cause one's health to improve.

**C. It confuses two causes that together are necessary to bring about an effect with causes that are sufficient for that effect.** — Wrong Flaw (4% of students)
When we see "necessary", "sufficient", or any synonyms for those ideas, they're talking about the famous Necessary vs. Sufficient flaw, which is when the author presents a conditional statement in the premise and then applies it in some illegal Backwards or Opposite fashion to derive her conclusion.

Any time we see this answer choice, we should just ask ourselves, "Was there any conditional logic?"

If not, it's wrong. If so, it's right 95% of the time. There was no conditional logic in this paragraph.

**D. It takes for granted that two conditions that together have a certain effect can, each by itself, produce the same effect.** — Bad Premise/Conclusion Match (2% of students)
This breaks for the same initial reason that (A) did. Do we have two conditions that together have a certain effect?

Nope. Negative emotions and impaired physical health are not conditions that together have a certain effect. Instead, they are both effects of the same cause.

The second half of this answer is also a mismatch, but we needn't read that far to get rid of it.

**E. It takes for granted that removing a condition that causally contributes to another condition suffices to eliminate the latter condition.** — Too Strong: suffices to eliminate (14% of students)
Saying that "the author takes for granted that X suffices to do Y" makes it seem like X should match evidence and Y should match conclusion.

The conclusion is saying that removing the condition of negative emotions could cause one's health to improve. It never says "it suffices (guarantees)" to eliminate the latter condition.

## S2 · Q18 — Most Strongly Supported (Inference)

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Some potential anticancer drugs work by depriving growing tumors of needed blood vessels. The creation of blood vessels is called angiogenesis, and the experimental drugs work by inhibiting this process. The same drugs have been found to prevent obesity in rodents.

### Prompt
The statements above, if true, lend the strongest support to which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:** As we search for Causal, Conditional, Comparative, or Pivot type wording, we see two causal claims.

  - causal (work by): Some anticancer drugs work by depriving growing tumors of needed blood vessels.
- causal (work by): These drugs inhibit the process of angiogenesis, which is the creation of new blood vessels.
- causal (prevent): The same drugs prevent obesity.
- **Evaluate:** They tell us the causal mechanism by which anticancer drugs work, inhibiting the creation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis), and then they tell us that the same drugs prevent obesity in rodents.

  So it seems like the synthesis we could get here is that the same causal mechanism somehow prevents obesity.
- **Goal:** If we were to try to use what we learned from the first two sentences with what we're told in the last sentence, we would probably be saying something like, "inhibiting the creation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis) prevents obesity in rats".

**Answer choices**
**A. The cells in tumors are more similar in structure to fat cells than to other cells in the body.** — Unknown Comparison (7% of students)
We know that both tumors and obesity are thwarted by inhibiting angiogenesis, but that doesn't mean that tumor cells and fat cells are closer to each other than to other types of cells in the body.

We have no means of comparing them to other cells in the body.

**B. Drugs that inhibit angiogenesis would probably enable obese humans to lose weight.** — Out of Scope: lose weight (16% of students)
This is a very tempting answer, since it reinforces our prediction that "inhibiting angiogenesis prevents obesity in rodents".

But preventing obesity is a very different concept from losing weight (reversing obesity).

Sunscreen prevents me from getting a sunburn, but if I'm already sunburnt, applying sunscreen doesn't help me to lose the sunburn.

**C. Fat tissue depends on angiogenesis in order to grow.** — Correct (70% of students)
This reinforces our prediction that "inhibiting angiogenesis prevents obesity in rats".

Now, are there problems with this correct answer? Oh my heavens, yes.
1. depends is very strong

However, we were told that tumors need these blood vessels to be created and that this drug prevents cancer from spreading by denying them their needed blood vessels. Since we're saying the same drug (that inhibits angiogenesis) works to prevent obesity, it's reasonable to think that fat cells also need / depend on blood vessels.

2. Fat tissue? Where did that come from?

Well, naturally it relates to obesity, but it sounds overly specific. It also might concern people that they're using outside knowledge when it comes to connecting obesity with fat tissue. You're allowed to use common sense knowledge. If you asked 10 people on the street whether obesity was connected to fat, they'd probably all say yes.

Is is common sense that obesity involves the growth of fat tissue? Hmm, pretty close. I think idiots like me wouldn't have ever defined obesity that way, because we're not smart enough about biology to think to use the term fat tissue, but if you asked me if someone who was becoming obese or more obese was gaining fat tissue, I would say, "yeah, right?"

3. Isn't this too broad? We only should be able to say "fat tissue in rodents depends on angiogenesis".

Sure, we were only told about rodents, but it's common sense that lots of testing of products and medicines happens on rodents before it's used on humans, since rodents are also mammals and our physiology is apparently similar enough that what we learn from testing rodents usually ports over pretty well to humans too. (Guinea pigs are rodents, and we probably all know the expression of "I don't want to be the guinea pig" means "I don't want to be the test subject")

4. Jeez, Patrick, if you have to itemize multiple possible qualms and then kinda make peace with each one, doesn't that add up to a pretty poorly supported answer?

Yes, kind of. But is there a more supported answer? This question stem tolerates fuzzy support, if no other answer has clearer support.

**D. Rodents with cancer are more likely to be obese than healthy rodents.** — Out of Scope: rodents with cancer / Unsupported Comparison (4% of students)
We have no way to say that rodents with cancer are more obese than healthy rodents. This answer seems to want to bait people into thinking, "If you took drug X, it would get rid of your obesity and your cancer, so I guess that means the rodents with cancer haven't taken the drug and are still obese".

But there isn't any sense in which this drug has been distributed to rodents across the world. The obesity drug has been administered to a handful of rodents in an experimental setting (we have no idea whether those rodents did / didn't have cancer).

This answer is addressing all the rodents in the world with cancer, a group we know nothing about.

**E. Drugs that inhibit angiogenesis also prevent absorption of vital nutrients.** — Out of Scope: vital nutrients (3% of students)
Nothing in this paragraph says anything about absorbing vital nutrients. This answer wants people to make an unsupported link from "If it prevents obesity, then it prevents you from absorbing vital nutrients".

### Stimulus
Although the slightest difference in shades of paint is noticeable, it is pointless to spend much time trying to match an old paint precisely when repainting only part of the interior of a house. This is because paint fades somewhat in the months after it has been applied. Thus, even if a new paint matches the old during application, the two paints will no longer match after a year or so.

The reasoning in which one of the following is most similar to that in the argument above?

- **Evidence:** The color of all paint fades and changes within about a year of being on the wall, so even if you nail the matching shade of paint initially, it will still drift from that shade and eventually not match the old paint.
- **Evaluate:** Since there was no conditional logic or quantifiers in this argument, it's probably not worth putting into algebra.

  Let's just try to remove the topic and tell the same story:

  - Premise 1: You want to find the perfect match/solution.
- Premise 2: But anything that would initially be perfect would eventually drift and no longer be perfect.
- Conclusion: Thus, it's pointless to spend too much energy trying to find the perfect match/solution.
- **Goal:** The conclusion has a signature strength/type we might be able to use for some quick eliminations.

  The conclusion on the correct answer should sound pretty dismissive of a certain strategy "it would be pointless to do X".

  The problem with the strategy needs to feel something like "even if it starts off right, it'll become wrong".

**B. It is rarely worthwhile to look for parts for an older car. Because most cars do not last more than 20 years, it is best to buy a new car instead of restoring an older car.** — Weak Conclusion Match / Bad Premise Match (2% of students)

We'd save ourselves time and brainpower by checking the conclusion first and seeing that "you should alternate between A and B" is a terrible match for the original's "it is pointless to do A".

The premises here have a little bit of "drift from perfection" in them, but there's no point in trying to even make it work when the conclusion makes it dead on arrival.

**D. Although cookies made with butter have a better texture than do cookies made with margarine, it is pointless to spend the extra money needed to buy butter, when cookies made with margarine are almost as good.** — Bad Premise Match (4% of students)
The conclusion is definitely saying that a certain strategy is pointless.

Is the evidence saying "even if you start out perfect, you'll drift away from perfection"?

No, it's saying "an inferior but easier to acquire alternative is close enough that you might as well just go with that".

**E. Unless the size and shape of a dress exactly matches the size and shape of its lining, the dress will hang unevenly when it is worn. The conclusion does say a certain strategy would be pointless.

Does the evidence say that "even if you nail it at first, you'll eventually drift from perfection"?

No, it says "almost no one will notice an imperfect attempt, so why bother".

## S2 · Q20 — Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Social observer: Advertising agencies are willfully neglecting the most profitable segment of the market: older adults. Older adults control more of this nation's personal disposable income than does the rest of the population combined. Therefore, advertising agencies can maximize their clients' profits if they gear their advertisements mainly to older adults.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if assumed, would enable the conclusion of the social observer's argument to be properly inferred?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If ad agencies gear their ads mainly to older adults, they can maximize clients' profits.
- **Evidence:** Older adults control more of this nation's personal disposable income than does the rest of the population combined.
- **Evaluate:** If this were anything other than Sufficient Assumption, I would be tempted to start marshaling counterarguments, like "yes, but ... older people's consumer habits are already stuck, so ads don't make as much different", or "most of the money they spend is on behalf of other people, so ads should target those people".

  But Sufficient Assumption just needs a math-y connector that adds on to what we're told in the premise and allows us to derive the conclusion.

  Since there is a New Idea in the Conclusion, (maximizing clients' profits), that idea must be in the correct answer.

  The one thing we know from the Evidence that we can swap out in the conclusion is the detail we're told about "older adults" (they're the overlapping idea).
- **Goal:** Maybe something like, "If you gear your ads mainly to [the most profitable segment of the market] / [the people who control more personal disposable income than the rest of the population combined], then you'll maximize your clients' profits".

  Naturally, they might disguise the correct answer in contrapositive form, and we should wary of illegal reversals / opposites.

**Answer choices**
**A. Older people generally have larger incomes and have had longer to accumulate resources than younger people.** — Unrelated to Goal (5% of students)
This doesn't have any language about "maximizing clients' profits", so it is functionally useless to us.

**B. No company can maximize its profits unless it markets its products primarily to a population segment that controls most of this nation's personal disposable income.** — Illegal Reversal / Opposite (21% of students)
This says: max out profits → market mainly to [older people]

But our conclusion is saying: market mainly to [older people] → max out profits

Unless = if it is not the case that. So one way to diagram this is: not marketing to population segment that controls most disposable income → won't maximize profits

Our conclusion is the Opposite of that: do market to population segment that controls most disposable income (i.e. older people) → will maximize profits

**C. Advertising that is directed toward the wealthiest people is the most effective means for a business to improve the reputation of its products.** — Unrelated to Goal / Out of Scope = reputation (2% of students)
This doesn't have any language about "maximizing clients' profits", so it is functionally useless to us. Also, the correct answer to Sufficient Assumption will not bring up any new ideas, such as "enhancing the reputation of products".

**D. No advertising agency that tailors its advertisements mainly to an audience that does not control much of this nation's personal disposable income will maximize its clients' profits.** — Illegal Opposite (17% of students)
This, like (B), is giving us an opposite/reversed version of the conclusion. It's saying: tailor ads mainly to segment of pop that doesn't control much disposable income (i.e. not older adults) → won't maximize clients' profits

Meanwhile, this argument is saying: tailor ads mainly to segment of pop that does control much disposable income (i.e. older adults) → will maximize clients' profits

**E. Any advertising agency that gears its advertisements mainly to a population segment that controls 50 percent or more of this nation's personal disposable income will maximize its clients' profits.** — Correct (56% of students)
We know that "older people" are a population segment that controls more than 50% of the nation's personal disposable income.

There is another modern question (a Principle question about completing the space agency's current project, since the money we'd still have to spend on it is more than the money we've already spent) that uses this same mathematical rephrasing: if one chunk is bigger than the rest combined, then that first chunk is more than 1/2.

Since older people control more than everyone else combined, they have more than half the disposable income. Pick any number for older people, make sure it's more than the combined total for non-older people, and you'll see why it's always more than half.

Older people: 10 billion
not older people: 9 billion
nation's total: 19 billion (older people > 1/2)

So since we know that this language of "pop segment that controls 50% or more" applies to "older people", this rule allows us to say that,

If an ad agency gears its ads mainly to [older people], then it will maximize its clients' profits.

Thus, we've derived the conclusion.

## S2 · Q21 — Principle (Justify) / Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Professor: It has been argued that all judges should be elected rather than appointed to their positions. But this is a bad idea. If judges ran for election, they would have to raise campaign funds. Thus, they would be likely to accept campaign contributions from special interests. It is well-known that such contributions lead to conflicts of interest for politicians, so it is to be expected that they would produce similar conflicts of interest for judges.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, would most help to justify the professor's reasoning?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** It would be a bad idea if all judges were elected rather than appointed.
- **Evidence:** If judges were elected, they'd have to raise funds, which means they'd accept campaign contributions from special interests, which means they'd be likely to run into the same conflicts of interest that politicians run into when they accept contributions.
- **Evaluate:** We want a principle that gets us from something we were told in the Evidence about "electing judges", such as
- they'd have to fundraise
- they'd take contributions from special interests
- they'd presumably run into conflicts of interest
and uses that to get us to Conclusion language like "bad idea / should have judges be elected".
- **Goal:** If they'd have to fundraise, then electing judges is a bad idea

  or

  If it would mean that they're taking contributions from special interests, then electing judges shouldn't be done

  or (most likely, since this idea was deepest in the chain of Premises)

  If it would produce conflicts of interests for judges, then we shouldn't have them be elected.

**Answer choices**
**A. If politicians should avoid conflicts of interest, then judges should avoid conflicts of interest as well.** — Bad Premise Match (6% of students)
If this answer simply said "judges should avoid conflicts of interest", then knowing what we were told about electing judges, this would strengthen the idea that judges shouldn't be elected.

But this answer is conditional, so it only has any power if you trigger it.

Can we trigger it? Were we told that politicians should avoid conflicts of interest? Nope. So this answer does nothing for us.

**B. Special interests should not make offers of campaign contributions to those running for elective office.** — Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
This is a principle that would help us say what special interests should / shouldn't do.

We're looking for a principle that would help us say whether judges should / shouldn't be elected. So this is nowhere close to the right type of wording.

**C. Judges should be appointed to their positions only if doing so ensures that they will usually be able to avoid conflicts of interest.** — Bad Conclusion Match (15% of students)
Because "only if" signifies the right side idea, this is saying: should be appointed → appointment ensures usually no conflicts of interest

If we contrapose this, then we have a rule that says, if there usually is conflict of interest, then judges should not be appointed.

That's a bad match for our conclusion, which is "judges should not be elected". The conclusion actually implies we should stick with our current system of appointing judges, so this answer would only help us to disprove the conclusion.

**D. If judges should be appointed, then it is likely that there are other public offices that should be changed from elected to appointed offices.** — Bad Conclusion Match (1% of students)
This, structurally, is useless for the same reason (C) was. It's not a rule that helps us to prove "judges should not be elected".

Bizarrely, it's another rule that allows you to prove "judges should not be appointed", which is the opposite of our conclusion.

Remember, that principles (or conditionals) only allow you to prove/conclude the idea to the right of the arrow.

This rule is written: should be appointed → xyz

So we would contrapose it to see its resemblance to the conclusion: ~xyz → should not be appointed

**E. No public office for which election campaigning would be likely to produce conflicts of interest should be changed from an appointed to an elected office.** — Correct (76% of students)
No A's are B = A → ~B

So this rule reads like this: Public office for which election campaigning would be likely to produce conflicts of interest → should not be changed from an appointed office to an elected office

Is judging a public office for which campaigning would be likely to produce conflicts of interest?

Yes. Were they to have to campaign, it is likely that they would accept campaign contributions, which is well-known to lead to conflicts of interest.

Since we can trigger this rule, we can say that "judges should not be changed from appointed to elected officers", which is a great match for our Conclusion.

## S2 · Q22 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Merle: Usually when I insert a dollar bill into the change machine at the office it makes a squeaking sound before it produces change. But the machine can make the sound only when the electric outlet it is plugged into is turned on. Therefore, it must be that the electric outlet usually is turned on.

### Prompt
Which one of the following arguments exhibits flawed reasoning most similar to the flawed reasoning exhibited by Merle's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** The machine can make sound (S) only when the outlet is turned on (OTO): S → OTO

  Usually when I use the machine, it makes the sound. (Usually, for me, S.)
- **Conclusion:** The electric outlet is usually turned on. (Usually, OTO.)
- **Evaluate:** Because this has a conditional (only when) and repeating ideas (makes sound / outlet turned on), it's a good contender for looking at the structure.

  The flaw seems to be a Sampling one. The conditional logic isn't going backwards. It's just that the author is thinking "if it usually makes the sound for me, then it probably usually makes the sound for everyone."
- **Goal:** Look for an argument with a conditional X → Y. Then a premise that's saying, "in my experience, usually X." Then a conclusion that's saying, "thus, usually Y."

**Answer choices**
**A. Everyone who has read the new horror novel found the plot disturbing. Indeed, the plot would disturb anyone with a vivid imagination. Therefore, everyone who has read the novel must have a vivid imagination.** — Bad Premise / Conclusion Match (11% of students)
This has a conditional premise: vivid imagination → disturbing

The premise should be saying "in my experience, usually vivid imagination. Thus, usually disturbing."

Instead the premise is about "everyone", when we were looking for a premise that dealt with the author's experience.

And the conclusion should be the outcome of the conditional (disturbing), not vivid imagination. This argument had a different flaw of making a backwards (Necessary vs. Sufficient) move.

**B. Some people who have read the new horror novel found the plot disturbing. But the plot would not be disturbing to people who lack vivid imaginations. Therefore, some people must have vivid imaginations.** — Bad Conclusion Match (7% of students)
This has a conditional premise: ~vivid imagination → ~disturbing, i.e. disturbing → vivid imagination

We then have a premise saying, some people found it disturbing.

The conclusion would now need to extrapolate for a broader group that there is vivid imagination.

Instead, the conclusion is safely worded, pertaining only to the "some people" in this evidence. This isn't a Sampling flaw; in fact it's valid logic.

**C. Many people who have read the new horror novel found the plot disturbing. Undoubtedly, everyone who found the plot disturbing has a vivid imagination. Therefore, many of the people who have read the novel must have vivid imaginations.** — Bad Premise / Conclusion Match (21% of students)
This has a conditional premise: found disturbing → vivid imagination

We have premise saying "many people = disturbing".

The conclusion would now need to extrapolate for a broader group that there is vivid imagination.

Instead, the conclusion is safely worded, pertaining only to the "many people" in this evidence. This isn't a Sampling flaw; in fact it's valid logic, just like (B).

**D. Most people who have read the new horror novel found the plot disturbing. But the plot cannot disturb anyone who lacks a vivid imagination. Therefore, most people must have vivid imaginations.** — Correct (51% of students)
We have a conditional: disturbing → vivid imagination

We have a premise where the trigger is true for some Sample: most [people who have read the novel] found the plot disturbing.

Then we have a conclusion that extrapolates the outcome for a broader group: most [people] have vivid imagination

This ended up straying a bit from our prediction, since it didn't use the first person, but it still went from "trigger is true for a smaller group" to a conclusion that "outcome is true for a larger group".

**E. Most people who have read the new horror novel found the plot disturbing. But the plot cannot disturb people who lack vivid imaginations. Therefore, most people with vivid imaginations must find the plot disturbing.** — Bad Conclusion Match (11% of students)
This has a conditional premise: found disturbing → vivid imagination

We have premise saying "most who read novel = disturbing"

The conclusion would now need to extrapolate for a broader group that there is vivid imagination.

Instead, the conclusion talks about "most people with vivid imaginations" and says they found the plot disturbing.

In the original argument, it said "the outcome is true for a broader group".

In this answer, it says "for most people that match the outcome, the trigger is true".

## S2 · Q23 — Method of Response (Method of Argument)

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Kira: It would be unwise for you to buy that insurance policy. It's designed to make money for the company that sells it to you. They set the prices to ensure profits.

Binh: Undeniably, the insurer is in business to make money. But the mere fact that an insurer draws a profit in no way implies that buying one of its policies is unwise.

### Prompt
Binh responds to Kira's argument by doing which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Kira's conclusion:** It's unwise for you to buy that insurance policy.
- **Kira's evidence:** It's designed to make the insurance company money. The prices are set to ensure profits.
- **Binh's response:** I agree with your evidence (it's designed to make them money), but that doesn't in any way show it's unwise to buy it.
- **Evaluate:** When Method questions involve one person responding to another, a lot of what LSAT likes to test is the area of disagreement. Did the 2nd person ..
- disagree with the Conclusion?
- disagree with the Evidence? (this almost never happens)
- disagree with an Assumption?
- just raise a further consideration?

  Binh is definitely agreeing with Kira's premise, but he just thinks it's irrelevant when it comes to her conclusion.

  He's presumably saying, "All for-profit businesses are designed the same way: the prices are set so that the business makes money. Are you saying it would be unwise for me to buy anything that a for-profit company is selling? It's unwise for me to buy milk, or eggs, because the farming company is going to profit? It's unwise for me to buy Tylenol because the pharmaceutical company is going to profit?"
- **Goal:** Look for an answer saying that Binh points out that Kira's evidence, while undeniably true, has no logical force when it comes to supporting the idea that a certain product would be an unwise purchase.

  Binh hasn't said as much, but the common sense gist of his response is, "Sure ... isn't that true of any product?" He's sort of saying, "You're acting like this is some negative property specific to this insurance policy, when it's not distinctive at all. It's a feature of every for-profit product."

**Answer choices**
**A. suggesting that Kira has overlooked a fact that, although consistent with her premises, is in direct conflict with her conclusion** — Out of Scope: new fact (20% of students)
Binh doesn't present any new facts. He just says, "Yup, your premise is true, but it's garbage in terms of lending any support to your conclusion."

**B. denying Kira's premises while suggesting that her conclusion, although possibly true, is highly unlikely** — Contradicted (3% of students)
Binh explicitly accepts Kira's premises. (in fact, I don't think I've ever seen a correct answer on Method of Response in which the 2nd person denied the truth of the 1st person's evidence)

**C. arguing that Kira's premises are not only inadequate to prove her conclusion but in fact point strongly toward its being false** — Bad 2nd Half (8% of students)
The first half of this is descriptively accurate, but Binh doesn't make any commentary on whether this specific insurance policy would / wouldn't be a wise purchase.

He's only saying that Kira's reason for rejecting it is a completely hollow reason. He doesn't think that her reason actually points in the opposite direction.

**D. conceding Kira's premises without denying her conclusion, while asserting that the latter does not follow from the former** — Correct (67% of students)
Binh does indeed agree to (concede) that Kira's evidence is true. He just says it has no power to influence her conclusion, which means "her conclusion does not follow from her premises".

His final sentence is basically saying this: The fact that [your premises] in no way implies [your conclusion].

And this answer is just saying it inverted: [Your conclusion] in no way follows from [your premises]

**E. observing that while Kira's premises each independently support her conclusion, the premises themselves are inconsistent with one another** — Contradicted (3% of students)
Binh definitely does not think the premises support her conclusion. He explicitly says that "the mere fact that [your premise] in no way implies [your conclusion]."

Also, saying that premises are inconsistent means that the premises actually contradict each other, which is an incredibly strong accusation that is (almost?) never right.

## S2 · Q24 — Weaken (EXCEPT)

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Economist: The increase in the minimum wage in Country X will quickly lead to a decrease in Country X's rate of unemployment. Raising the minimum wage will lead to more disposable income for a large segment of the working population. Much of this increased income will be spent on consumer goods. Surely this increase in demand for consumer goods will lead to an increase in the number of factory jobs necessary to meet production.

### Prompt
Each of the following, if true, would weaken the economist's argument except:

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The increase in the minimum wage will lead to a decrease in unemployment.
- **Evidence:** As people make more, they will spend more on consumer goods, which will lead to an increase in factory jobs to increase production.
- **Evaluate:** How could we argue that an increase in the minimum wage will not lead to a decrease in unemployment?

  - The first thing that probably comes to mind is that raising the minimum wage raises the overhead costs for businesses that depend on minimum wage employees. They might not be able to handle this uptick in wage expense, and they might have to lay people off, which would increase unemployment.

  We should otherwise take each step in the author's causal chain and see if we can push back.

  - Will higher min wage lead to more purchases of consumer goods? Maybe not. Maybe the people who start making more money still won't have enough to buy more stuff. Maybe they'll just pay off debts or bills.

  - Let's say there were more consumer goods purchased. Will that really lead to the hiring of more people to fill needed factory jobs? Maybe not. Maybe those factories are highly automated so if anything it would just mean more robots.
- **Goal:** We need to eliminate four answers that weaken. They will probably either take issue with the predicted implications the author mentioned concerning an increased minimum wage, or they will just offer a new reason why higher minimum wage could increase unemployment (or not reduce it).

**Answer choices**
**A. The cost of a minimum-wage increase in Country X will be passed on to consumers in the form of significantly higher prices for consumer goods.** — Weakens (11% of students)
This hurts the connection between higher minimum wage and more purchases of consumer goods. Perhaps the people with higher wages will be eager to spend their extra money, but if the prices of consumer goods go up in general, that will have the effect of decreasing spending on consumer goods.

**B. Most of the consumer goods sold in Country X are produced outside the country.** — Weakens (8% of students)
This weakens the connection between higher spending on consumer goods and more domestic jobs being created.

Yes, spending on goods will line the pockets of the manufacturers of these goods, but if they're in other countries mostly, then the new jobs created will be in other countries and won't help to reduce the unemployment in Country X.

**C. In many factories in Country X, most workers are paid much more than the current minimum wage.** — Correct (60% of students)
This has no impact. Someone might think we can weaken the argument by saying, "since most workers make much more than minimum wage, they won't be affected by an increase in the minimum wage, so that weakens the idea that it will quickly lead to a decrease in unemployment."

But this answer is very limited in scope, since it applies to many factories. It's saying that in at least five factories a majority of the workers make well above the current minimum wage.

Okay, does that hurt any part of the story the author is trying to tell? Was she thinking that factory workers were paid at or near the current minimum wage?

No, she's was saying "anyone who DOES make the current minimum wage will have more disposable income when the min wage increase happens, and that extra spending and consumer demand will lead to the creation of factory jobs (that might get paid at any rate, doesn't matter)."

This answer is just kind of a word salad, because in the story the author is telling, the people who make minimum wage are responsible for driving up consumer demand, and the decrease in unemployment will come from the labor demand that creates for factory jobs. But whether factory jobs involve minimum wage is irrelevant.

**D. The cost to employers of an increase in the minimum wage in Country X will be made up by reductions in the workforce.** — Weakens (9% of students)
This gets at our common sense objection that if we raise minimum wage, some employers won't be able to afford all their employees anymore, so they will lay some off, thereby increasing unemployment.

**E. Most factories that produce consumer goods in Country X have large surpluses of goods as a result of years of overproduction.** — Weakens (12% of students)
This attacks the connection between "more consumer goods purchased" and "factories will need to hire more people to make more goods".

It's saying, "Nah, they're good with goods. They've got a large surplus just sitting in a warehouse from years of overproduction."

## S2 · Q25 — Principle (Apply / Justify Reasoning)

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Art critic: An arrangement of objects tends to be aesthetically pleasing to the extent that it gives the impression that the person who arranged the objects succeeded at what he or she was attempting to do.

### Prompt
The generalization expressed by the art critic, if correct, most helps to justify the reasoning in which one of the following arguments?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Principle:** The more someone gets the impression that the person who arranged a group of objects succeeded at what he or she was attempting to do, the more aesthetically pleasing that arrangement is.
- **Evaluate / Goal:** This isn't a conditional principle, so we can expect to see some judgments that are more comparative on continuum in nature.

  Because of that, it seems like you can take it in either direction.

  - If you have the impression the arranger succeeded in their mission → more aesthetically pleasing
- If you have the impression the arranger failed in their mission → less aesthetically pleasing

**Answer choices**
**A. The new art installation is very pleasing aesthetically. However, even though it is not apparent to viewers, the artist did not intend the wooden panels to be arranged the way they in fact are. So the panels should be rearranged to reflect the artist's intention.** — Weak Match (6% of students)
The viewers don't realize that the arranger didn't accomplish their goal, but this principle needs the viewers to know that the arranger did accomplish their goal. Otherwise we shouldn't be labeling it very aesthetically pleasing.

**B. The wooden panels in the art installation probably are not arranged in the way that the artist wanted them arranged, for the installation is less aesthetically pleasing than other installations.** — Weak Match (16% of students)
I don't hate this one, but the Conclusion / Premise should probably be swapped. We would use the criterion of "this arrangement gives the clear impression that the arranger succeeded at their intent, thus aesthetically pleasing".

But maybe more importantly is that this talks about whether or not the artist succeeded, whereas what we need to hear about is whether the arrangement "gives the impression" that the artist succeeded.

**C. The arrangement of wooden panels in the art installation is likely to give the impression that the artist who arranged them did not quite succeed at what she was attempting to do, for the arrangement is almost, but not quite, symmetrical.** — Out of Scope: symmetrical (5% of students)
This clarifies whether or not the impression of success was given off, but this doesn't have anything about how aesthetically pleasing the art is / isn't.

**D. The art installation would be more aesthetically pleasing if the wooden panels in it were arranged more symmetrically, for then it would seem more like the artist had gotten the panels arranged the way she wanted.** — Correct (69% of students)
This clearly connects the idea of "giving off the impression of successful attempt" with "more aesthetically pleasing".

**E. The artist could make the arrangement of wooden panels in the art installation more aesthetically pleasing by making it less symmetrical, for then it would give less of an impression that every aspect of the arrangement was fully planned.** — Opposite (4% of students)
This is saying "by making this look less fully planned (less intentional / less like a successful execution of the arranger's vision)", it will be "more aesthetically pleasing".

That has the principle backwards.

## S2 · Q26 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
The three-spine stickleback is a small fish that lives both in oceans and in freshwater lakes. While ocean stickleback are covered with armor to protect them from their predators, lake stickleback have virtually no armor. Since armor limits the speed of a stickleback's growth, this indicates that having a larger size is a better defense against the lake stickleback's predators than having armor.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, weakens the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Having a larger size is a better defense against the lake's predators than is armor.
- **Evidence:** The lake stickleback has virtually no armor, while the ocean stickleback is covered with armor to protect them from their predators.

  The armor limits the speed of a stickleback's growth.
- **Evaluate:** Yikes. This is a tough one to organize. I would use the typical Curious Fact / Explanation template that is so common on Strengthen and Weaken.

  Curious Fact: how come the lake stickleback has no armor, while the ocean stickleback does have armor?

  Author's Story: for the sake of protection against the lake's predators, the stickleback would be better off being large than having armor, and having armor slows a stickleback's growth. So natural selection resulted in larger, armorless stickleback in lakes.

  We should then think our two questions for this template:
1. is there some OTHER WAY we could explain this curious fact?
2. how plausible is the AUTHOR'S WAY of explaining it?

  1. Maybe the armor is made from salt and other minerals that would be found in an ocean but not in a freshwater lake. Perhaps the lake just doesn't have the same raw materials the ocean has that would allow a stickleback to biologically construct the typical armor. Maybe the armor would have been useful for predators, but large size offers some other advantage (better success mating?)

  2. To believe the author's story, I definitely need to assume a difference between the ocean predators and the lake predators. I'm not sure what that difference would be, but it should be where armor is a better defense against the ocean attackers and large size is better against the lake attackers. To weaken the argument, we could hear an answer saying that "the lake stickleback and the ocean stickleback have similar predators".
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that suggests a different way to explain why the lake stickleback lack armor, or for an answer that undermines the plausibility that stickleback are better off with largeness than with armor, when it comes to fending off the lake's predators.

**Answer choices**
**A. Sticklebacks with armor are unable to swim as fast, making them most vulnerable to fast-moving predators.** — Unclear Impact (10% of students)
We don't know whether the ocean or the lake has faster predators, so there's no way to apply this new information to the distinction we're trying to explain.

**B. Having a larger size is an important factor in whether lake stickleback, but not ocean stickleback, survive cold winters.** — Correct (46% of students)
Here is the Alternate Explanation answer we expect, when we're Weakening a Curious Fact / Explanation type argument.

The lake stickleback aren't larger because that helps them more against the lake's predators, they're larger because that helps them survive cold winters.

Importantly, this alternate cause has to be something that only applies to the lake stickleback, so we're happy to see that "but not ocean stickleback" distinguisher in this answer.

**C. Unlike ocean stickleback, the lake stickleback are more often preyed upon by predatory insects than by larger fish.** — Unclear Impact (41% of students)
The author's explanation assumes a difference between the lake stickleback's predators and the ocean stickleback's predators. This answer establishes that there is a difference. In that sense, it strengthens the plausibility of the author's hypothesis.

But the author wasn't just assuming any ol' difference. She's assuming that armor works better against ocean predators and size works better against lake predators.

This answer suggests that ocean predators tend to be larger fish, whereas lake predators tend to be predatory insects. Does our common sense say that armor works better when you're being attacked by larger fish, but size works better if you're being attacked by predatory insects?

People's answers to that question are pretty divergent. I've heard students (and other LSAT teachers) make the case that you'd rather have armor against predatory insects, and I've heard students/teachers make the case that you'd rather have size against predatory insects.

The fact that we test takers don't agree about what impact it has to be bigger vs. possess armor, in regards to predatory insects, suggests that there isn't a strong common sense take on the impact of this answer.

Meanwhile, the correct answer unequivocally weakens by showing that there's an alternate explanation for why the lake stickleback would "want to evolve" to be larger.

**D. Both ocean stickleback and lake stickleback feed primarily on the same types of foods.** — Strengthens, if Anything (2% of students)
This would rule out any alternate story that relates to different eating needs. It's possible that lake stickleback evolved to be larger because that helped them eat lake-food.

But this rules out that possibility. Since the author thinks that "different predators" are the causal difference-maker, when it comes to why the lake stickleback lacks armor and the ocean stickleback has armor, her argument is strengthened by controlling for any other potential causal difference-maker.

If you want to argue that "different predators" is why these two species are different, you want to believe that the difference is not due to different food supply, different mating habits, different habitat demands, etc.

**E. Sticklebacks originated in the ocean but began populating freshwater lakes and streams following the last ice age.** — No Impact (2% of students)
Since the author is positing an evolutionary adaptation to the lake, the author needs to assume that the stickleback has been in the lake long enough for natural selection to take its slow effect.

So, if anything, establishing that the stickleback diverged into ocean and lake varieties a long, long time ago might strengthen the argument by making it seem like the two stickleback populations have been apart for long enough that they plausibly could have evolved different traits.
