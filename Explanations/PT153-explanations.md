# Section 2 (Logical Reasoning)

## S2 · Q1 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus
In situations where it is difficult to make informed decisions about products, consumers should be provided with the relevant information. The difficulty of determining whether a food product contained nutritious ingredients was resolved by requiring food manufacturers to print nutritional information on their products' packaging. Similarly, many consumers are interested in conserving energy, and since there is no easy way for consumers to determine how much energy was required to manufacture a product, ______.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most logically completes the argument?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Setup:**
- **Principle:** If it's difficult to make an informed decision about a product, consumers should be provided with the relevant information.
- **Example:** It was difficult to decide whether a given food product had nutritious ingredients, until we required food manufacturers to print nutritional info on the package.
- **Analogy:** Similarly, consumers want to make an informed decision about conserving energy, and it's difficult for consumers to determine how much energy was required to make a product.
- **The Blank:** We know we're looking for a Conclusion that can be drawn, because the setup here was: Since there's no easy way, ______ . Any time since/because starts off a sentence, then the second clause in the sentence is a conclusion supported by the first clause.
- **Evaluate:** Like so many Fill-in-the-Blank questions, this one wants us to Complete the Analogy. We want to "carry over" what he heard about in the example of nutritional information on food products to what we're now talking about in relation to energy. We're operating by this principle that says, "if it's not easy to make an informed decision, consumers should provided with the relevant info". It wasn't easy to decide if food products had nutritious ingredients, so manufacturers were required to print nutritional info on the packaging. It's not easy to decide how much energy went into manufacturing a product, so manufacturers should be required to put information about how much energy it took to manufacture a product on the packaging.
- **Goal:** We can be flexible about "required" and "packaging". The core component is that manufacturers should provide to consumers the relevant information about how much energy was used to make a product.

**Answer choices**
**A. consumers who are informed of the amount of energy used to produce a product should choose energy efficient products** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
We're looking for a statement about what manufacturers should provide, or about what consumers should be provided with.

manufacturers should provide to consumers the relevant information about how much energy was used to make a product.

This answer is a statement about what consumers should choose.

**B. manufacturers should use less energy while producing products** — Unrelated to Goal (0% of students)
We're looking for a statement about what information manufacturers should provide, or about what information consumers should be provided with.

manufacturers should provide to consumers the relevant information about how much energy was used to make a product.

This answer is a statement about how much energy manufacturers should use.

**C. providing consumers with information about the energy used to produce a product would reduce the impact of fossil fuels on our lives and economy** — Out of Scope: reduce impact of fossil fuels (1% of students)
This starts off on the right foot, but we should just be saying,

"Providing consumers with information about the energy used to produce a product should happen".

We don't have any grounds for taking this conversation into the zone of what impact such information would have on fossil fuels.

This would only work if in the food-half of the analogy we had heard that, "Once consumers had information about nutrition, they started eating more nutritious foods".

**D. consumers should demand products that require less energy to produce** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
We're looking for a statement about what information manufacturers should provide, or about what information consumers should be provided with.

manufacturers should provide to consumers the relevant information about how much energy was used to make a product.

This answer is a statement about what sort of products consumers should demand.

**E. manufacturers should be required to label their products with information about the amount of energy used to produce those products** — Correct (98% of students)
This completes the analogy, without going overboard into anything new or undiscussed.

manufacturers should provide to consumers the relevant information about how much energy was used to make a product.

They actually stuck very close to the food-nutrition example, in which the relevant information that consumers wanted was "required to be put on the packaging".

## S2 · Q2 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** B

### Stimulus
Biologist: Some small animals will instinctively go limp, "playing dead" when caught by a predator. But it is hard to see how playing dead can have survival value in this situation. The predator means to eat the animal just the same, whether or not it plays dead.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, would most help to resolve the apparent paradox described by the biologist?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that ...:** A predator will eat an animal, whether it's pretending to be dead or still looking alive.
- **How is it that ...:** Instinctively going limp and "playing dead" when caught by a predator can have survival value?
- **Goal:** Let's look for any answer that helps us understand how "playing dead" helps the small animal being chased by the predator.

**Answer choices**
**A. Many small animal species will play dead when surprised by a loud noise or unexpected movement.** — No Impact (4% of students)
This doesn't help us understand how "playing dead" helps a small animal being chased by a predator.

This is talking about a small animal playing dead in response to a loud noise or sudden movement (not in response to a predator).

**B. Predators often leave their food in a hiding place rather than eating it immediately.** — Correct (92% of students)
This helps us understand how "playing dead" helps a small animal being chased by a predator.

A wolf catches up with a rabbit, and the rabbit plays dead. The wolf now carries the rabbit's limp, seemingly-lifeless body over to the wolf's hidey-hole, plops the rabbit in there for later, and then goes back out to continue the night's hunt.

When the wolf comes back later to have it's delicious rabbit dessert, it realizes that the rabbit has run away!

Wolf: "Hoodwinked! It wasn't really dead. It was just playing dead, and then it ran away as soon as I left the area. You rascally rabbit."

**C. A small animal is more likely to play dead when caught by a predator if the predator species is common in the area.** — No Impact (1% of students)
This doesn't help us understand how "playing dead" helps a small animal being chased by a predator.

This just tells us about conditions in which the animal is more likely to be play dead, but we still don't know why playing dead would be helpful in those conditions.

**D. Most predators prey upon a variety of species, not all of which play dead when caught.** — No Impact (1% of students)
This doesn't help us understand how "playing dead" helps a small animal being chased by a predator.

It just tells us that some of a predator's prey don't play dead. But that doesn't tell us how the prey that do play dead get any survival value out of it.

**E. Many small animal species that do not play dead are capable of fighting off predators.** — No Impact (2% of students)
This doesn't help us understand how "playing dead" helps a small animal being chased by a predator.

It's not talking about small animals who play dead.

## S2 · Q3 — Evaluate

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus
Food columnist: Only 2 percent of imported seafood is subjected to health safety inspections. So if you want to increase the likelihood that the seafood you buy will be safe to eat, you should buy only domestic seafood.

### Prompt
The answer to which one of the following questions would most help in evaluating the food columnist's argument?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (So):** If you want to increase the likelihood that the seafood you buy will be safe to eat, you should buy only domestic seafood.
- **Evidence:** Only 2% of imported seafood is subjected to health safety inspections.
- **Evaluate:** Let's just try to argue the Anti-Conclusion. Given that .... only 2% of imported seafood is subjected to health safety inspections. How could we ague that .... buying only domestic seafood is not increasing the likelihood your seafood is safe to eat? - Maybe only 1% of domestic seafood is subjected to inspection? The author seems to be assuming that a higher proportion of domestic is inspected than is imported. - Maybe 100% of imported seafood is inspected in the host country that exports it? We just spot check 2% when it arrives at our docks to verify it looks good.
- **Goal:** Find an answer that seems like a crucial component in comparing the safety of imported seafood to domestic seafood. (The correct answer should be a question that can be answered in a way that would weaken the argument)

**Answer choices**
**A. Do the health safety inspections detect all health risks present in the seafood that is inspected?** — Too Extreme: all / Unrelated to Goal: domestic vs. imported (2% of students)
The fact that this isn't a question that helps us assess the comparative risk between domestic vs. imported means it's useless to us.

There isn't any answer here that would weaken.

We could say, "no, health safety inspections don't detect every single health risk present", but that wouldn't give us a way to argue that imported seafood is at least as safe as domestic seafood.

It would be a fact that applies evenly to both of them.

**B. What kinds of health risks can seafood pose?** — Unrelated to Goal: domestic vs. imported (0% of students)
The fact that this isn't a question that helps us assess the comparative risk between domestic vs. imported means it's useless to us.

There isn't any answer here that would weaken.

We could say, "one health risk that seafood can pose is salmonella poisoning", but that wouldn't give us a way to argue that imported seafood is at least as safe as domestic seafood.

It would be a fact that applies evenly to both of them.

**C. What percentage of imported food other than seafood is subjected to health safety inspections?** — Out of Scope: other than seafood (1% of students)
This argument is only about comparing the risk of domestic seafood to imported seafood.

Other types of food are irrelevant.

**D. What percentage of domestic food other than seafood is subjected to health safety inspections?** — Out of Scope: other than seafood (1% of students)
This argument is only about comparing the risk of domestic seafood to imported seafood.

Other types of food are irrelevant.

**E. What percentage of domestic seafood is subjected to health safety inspections?** — Correct (97% of students)
This would help us compare domestic seafood to imported seafood.

We already know what % of imported seafood gets inspected (only 2%). The author is acting like a higher percentage of domestic seafood gets inspected, but she never established that this is the case.

Here, we have a question that can be answered in a way that would weaken.

If we said, 'Only 1% of domestic seafood is inspected", then the fact that 2% of imported seafood is inspected would allow us to argue that imported seafood is at least as safe to eat as domestic.

If we had an argument that said, "Sharon is much stronger than Tom. After all, Tom can only bench press 100 lbs.", we would be like, "Okay, can Sharon bench press more than 100lbs?"

And an analogous answer choice would say:
(E) How much can Sharon bench press?

## S2 · Q4 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** B

### Stimulus
Dog owner: In general, large dogs need less intensive exercise than smaller dogs to stay fit. A dog that is not exercised at the level of intensity it needs is more apt to be troublesome than one that is. So for any apartment dweller who has limited time to give a dog exercise but who wants to have a dog, a large dog is less likely to be troublesome than a small one.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption the dog owner's argument requires?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If you live in an apartment, want a dog, but have limited time to give a dog exercise, a large dog is less likely to be troublesome than a small one.
- **Evidence:** Large dogs need less intensive exercise than smaller dogs. A dog that is not exercised at its needed level of intensity is more troublesome than one that is.
- **Evaluate:** Since we're trying to prove that "small dog would be more troublesome than large dog in an apartment", and we have a rule that says "if you're not exercised at your needed level of intensity, you'll be more troublesome", we know that our logical job is to establish this: Small dogs, in an apartment owned by someone with limited time to give a dog exercise, are less likely than big dogs to be exercised at their needed level of intensity. We do have one other fact that could potentially be used. Smaller dogs need more intensive exercise than large dogs. So as an extension of assuming that "small dogs are less likely to be exercised at their needed level of intensity", we could say "apartment dwellings do not allow small dogs to hit their needed level of intensity".
- **Goal:** Because "more/less likely to be troublesome" shows up in the Conclusion and the Evidence, this seems bound to test connect-the-dots thinking. We just need to establish that small dogs are less likely to get their needed intensity of exercise than are large dogs, given the context of "living in apartment / owner has limited time to give the dog exercise". But if I were using my objection brain, I would think, "Can't small dogs just run around the apartment and get their exercise that way?" (the author is assuming, "no, they can not just get their exercise that way).

**Answer choices**
**A. An apartment dweller who has limited time to exercise a dog is unlikely to want to own a dog.** — Out of Scope: not wanting a dog (1% of students)
This argument is only about the people in the conclusion, who are apartment dwellers who have limited time to give a dog exercise but who want to have a dog.

The author doesn't have to assume anything about other people / other situations.

**B. Providing a dog with more intensive exercise requires more time than providing a dog with less intensive exercise.** — Correct (92% of students)
The author was definitely Assuming a Difference, when it came to large dogs and small dogs. He thought that an apartment dweller with limited time would have an easier time exercising a large dog to its needed intensity than they would exercising a small dog to its needed intensity (which is higher than that of a large dog).

So if the author thinks that we'll have a harder time exercising the dog that requires more intensive exercise, he must be thinking that dogs who require more intensive exercise require more time to exercise than do dogs with lower exercise intensity needs.

If we negate this, and it says, "Providing the more-intense dog with adequate exercise does not require any more time", then it would be a huge objection.

It would destroy the assumed difference between how things would go with "apartment + limited time + large dog" vs. "apartment + limited time + small dog".

**C. At least some apartment dwellers who have limited time to exercise a dog should not own a dog.** — Out of Scope: should (2% of students)
The author isn't saying anything normative that would allow us to derive her assumptions on what should be the case. She's just describing what is.

Also, this sentiment does not sound like her. She is offering advice for what people w/ limited time, in an apartment, should do if they want a dog (get a large one!)

**D. Of dogs owned by apartment dwellers, those that live in large apartments are less likely to be troublesome than those that live in small apartments.** — Unknown Comparison: large vs. small apt. (2% of students)
We only talked about apartments in one categorical way. We can't divide up that category into large apartments and small apartments and pretend like the author had a clear position on that split.

**E. In general, the more often a dog gets exercise, the more likely it is that the dog will stay fit.** — Frequency vs. Intensity (3% of students)
This answer shouldn't be appealing to us, in the sense that it is lost in the language of the first sentence, which was a premise. We're being tested on what is assumed as the author moves from Premise to Conclusion. We're not being tested on the internal logic of something said in the Premise.

The paragraph talks about exercising your dog at the right intensity in order to stay fit. It doesn't talk about the frequency with which you give your dog exercise, so that concept is also out of scope.

## S2 · Q5 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus
One theory concerning the importance of vitamin C in the human diet holds that vitamin C plays a crucial role in the production and maintenance of the body's supply of collagen, an important protein occurring almost exclusively in connective tissue and bones. For this reason, some doctors believe that vitamin C can be useful in treating the symptoms of some common illnesses.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Information:** One theory: vitamin C plays a crucial role in producing and maintaining a supply of collagen, a protein found almost only in connective tissue and bones.
- **causal (for this reason):** Doctors think vitamin C can be useful in treating the symptoms of some common illnesses.
- **Evaluate:** Our job on these questions is to combine claims in order to derive some synthesis, and we primarily look for clues based on Causal, Comparative, Pivot or Conditional wording. The Causal Connector "for this reason" is giving us a big hint that this question wants us to spell out the logic of this causal connection. If I said, "Kim is a huge fan of Lebron James. For this reason, she is going to hate what I just tweeted", what can we infer from the connection between the first thing and the second thing? The most reasonable suspicion is that, "I just tweeted something that portrays Lebron James in a negative light". At a minimum, we can supportably infer, "I just tweeted something that relates to Lebron James." In this example, "Vitamin C plays a crucial role in making / maintaining the body's supply of collagen. For this reason, doctors think Vitamin C can be useful in treating symptoms of some common illnesses." "Vitamin C" is the Overlapping Ingredient in both claims. The way that a synthesis usually works is that we remove the overlap and combine the one-offs. Combining A is B with B is C gives us A is C. The synthesis removes the overlapping B and ties together the one-offs of A and C. Combining Kim likes Lebron with so, Kim won't like my tweet gives us my tweet relates to Lebron. The synthesis removes the overlapping Kim and ties together the other two ideas.
- **Goal:** Combining Vitamin C helps us with collagen with so, Vitamin C could help with symptoms gives us symptoms relate to collagen. Let's look for something like "the symptoms of some common illnesses relate to our supply of collagen / our supply of an important protein occurring mainly in connective tissue and bones".

**Answer choices**
**A. Some doctors believe that there are illnesses that affect the state of connective tissue or bones.** — Correct (84% of students)
If some doctors think that Vitamin C can useful in treating symptoms of some illnesses because of the fact that Vitamin C plays a crucial role in producing and maintaining the body's supply of an important protein that occurs mainly in connective tissue and bones,

then the doctors must think that the symptoms of some illnesses relate to the supply of an important protein occurring mainly in connective tissue and bones.

The symptoms of an illness relate to what parts of the body are attacked by the illness (or to what parts of the immune system attack that illness). If giving a patient more of a protein that occurs within connective tissue and bones would help them with symptoms of illness X, it sounds like illness X affects connective tissue and bones, causing certain related symptoms.

**B. Some doctors believe that vitamin C is the only substance that produces and maintains collagen.** — Too Strong: the only (1% of students)
This would only be supportable if we were told that "For this reason, some doctors believe that vitamin C is essential in treating the symptoms of certain illnesses".

If we needed vitamin C, then that would support that it's the only substance that help us with collagen. But saying that vitamin C "can be useful" is not strong enough to support that it's "the only" source of collagen assistance.

**C. Some doctors believe that strengthening connective tissue and bones increases the body's ability to use certain vitamins.** — Unsupported Causal Relationship (5% of students)
The passage is suggesting that taking vitamin C would have an effect on our connective tissue and bones.

This answer is saying that our connective tissue and bones would have an effect on our ability to take vitamin C.

**D. Some doctors believe that use of vitamin C is the most effective treatment for certain common illnesses.** — Too Strong: most effective (5% of students)
All we heard is "can be useful in treating the symptoms of some common illnesses". We have no support for the way more extreme claim that vitamin C is "the #1 most effective treatment for certain common illnesses".

**E. Some doctors believe that any illness that can be ameliorated with vitamin C causes deterioration of connective tissue and bones.** — Too Strong: any illness (6% of students)
All we heard is "can be useful in treating the symptoms of some common illnesses".

This is saying that "100% of illnesses that can be helped by vitamin C are related to deterioration of connective tissue and bones".

Vitamin C might do a lot of cool things besides help us with collagen, so it's possible that there are lots of illnesses that have nothing to do with connective tissue and bones, but vitamin C still ameliorates them (makes them better) based on one of its other effects.

## S2 · Q6 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** D

### Stimulus
Principle: If someone makes an error, it is unethical for a coworker to use that error to his or her own advantage.

Application: Because Mark used his coworker Rashmi's clients' e-mail addresses to advance his own career, his action was unethical.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps to justify the above application of the principle?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Principle:**
```
X makes an error → Unethical for coworker Y to
use X's error to Y's advantage
```
- **Application Conclusion:** Mark's action was unethical.
- **Application Evidence:** Mark used his coworker Rashmi's clients' e-mail addresses to advance his own career.
- **Evaluate:** On every Strengthen + Apply the Principle question, we're given a conditional principle. The Application will conclude the right side of the arrow. Our job is just to see where the application fell short of triggering the left side of the arrow. To prove Mark was "unethical", using this principle, we need to know 1. Mark's coworker made a mistake 2. Mark used that coworker's mistake to his own advantage. The application establishes that Mark used something from his coworker (the clients' email list) to Mark's own advantage. Mark used it "to advance his own career". But we haven't established that Rashmi made any mistake.
- **Goal:** Let's look for an answer that gives us the missing part of the trigger. We need to know that Rashmi made a mistake, and that mistake led to Mark using Rashmi's client email list.

**Answer choices**
**A. Mark had the e-mail addresses of Rashmi's clients only because he had copied them from Rashmi's directory while she was at lunch.** — Missing #1 (2% of students)
We can't use this principle to prove that Mark did something unethical until we know,
1. Mark's coworker made a mistake
2. Mark used that coworker's mistake to his own advantage.

We were never told that Rashmi made any sort of mistake, and this answer still is not telling us that (unless we were to infer that the 'mistake' that Rashmi made was not locking up her directory while she was at lunch).

**B. A coworker of Rashmi and Mark had access to Rashmi's clients' e-mail addresses and shared them with Mark.** — Missing #1 (1% of students)
We can't use this principle to prove that Mark did something unethical until we know,
1. Mark's coworker made a mistake
2. Mark used that coworker's mistake to his own advantage.

We were never told that Rashmi made any sort of mistake, and this answer still is not telling us that (unless we were to infer that the 'mistake' that Rashmi made was trusting that her coworker who had access to her list wouldn't just go and share it with Mark).

**C. Rashmi offered to help Mark develop a client base by sharing her own clients' e-mail addresses with him.** — Missing #1 (2% of students)
We can't use this principle to prove that Mark did something unethical until we know,
1. Mark's coworker made a mistake
2. Mark used that coworker's mistake to his own advantage.

We were never told that Rashmi made any sort of mistake, and this answer still is not telling us that (unless were infer that the 'mistake' that Rashmi made was offering to help Mark develop a client base).

It doesn't seem unethical of Mark to accept her help, though.

**D. Mark had access to Rashmi's clients' e-mail addresses only because she unintentionally left them visible in an e-mail that she sent to both Mark and her clients.** — Correct (94% of students)
We can't use this principle to prove that Mark did something unethical until we know,
1. Mark's coworker made a mistake
2. Mark used that coworker's mistake to his own advantage.

We were never told that Rashmi made any sort of mistake, but this answer supplies that fact.

Rashmi unintentionally (mistakenly) put the clients' emails into the visible part of an email (you've got to use that Bcc, Rashmi!)

This answer, more than any other, clarifies that Rashmi made a mistake, and thus Mark used a coworker's mistake to his own advantage.

**E. Mark happened upon a list of many of the e-mail addresses of Rashmi's clients while conducting market research.** — Missing #1 (1% of students)
We can't use this principle to prove that Mark did something unethical until we know,
1. Mark's coworker made a mistake
2. Mark used that coworker's mistake to his own advantage.

We were never told that Rashmi made any sort of mistake, and this answer still is not telling us that. Mark just found these email addresses on his own, apparently.

## S2 · Q7 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus
Kevin: My barber shop sells an herbal supplement that, according to my barber, helps prevent baldness because it contains an enzyme that blocks the formation of a chemical compound that causes people to lose hair.

Sabine: That's simply not true. The fact is, your barber makes money by convincing people to buy that product.

### Prompt
Sabine's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The barber's herbal supplement does not help prevent baldness by inhibiting an enzyme that leads to hair loss.
- **Evidence:** The barber is just saying that to make money.
- **Evaluate:** Well, this doesn't seem like a very sophisticated counterargument. Sabine is just deeply cynical, assumes the barber is making up the purported efficacy of the herbal supplement, and is not even entertaining the possibility that the barber is saying something legitimate.
- **Goal:** This feels like a version of the famous Ad Hominem flaw, since Sabine is dismissing someone's claims based on their ulterior motive (trying to sell bottles of herbal supplement). We also could see correct answer wording getting at the fact that Sabine failed to address the supposed causal mechanism of the herbal supplement. LSAC would want Sabine to respond by addressing whether there's any legitimacy to the idea that inhibiting an enzyme could slow down hair loss.

**Answer choices**
**A. discounts scientifically plausible evidence merely because the person offering it is not a scientist** — Bad Premise Match (1% of students)
We don't know enough about enzymes and hair loss to know if the barber's claim is scientifically plausible, but it at least sounded scientific. We should examine the biochemistry, if we're trying to shoot down the barber's claim. So Sabine did unfairly discount the scientific explanation.

But this answer says the premise was, "It simply does not do that. This barber is not a scientist", when the argument was "It simply does not do that. This barber just wants to make money".

**B. takes for granted that a product will be harmful if it is sold on the basis of an unsubstantiated claim** — Out of Scope: harmful (0% of students)
Does the author assume/conclude that the herbal supplement will be harmful? Nope. So we can eliminate this.

**C. rejects an explanation without proposing an alternative explanation** — Not a Flaw (1% of students)
It's not a flaw to reject an explanation without proposing an alternative explanation. If you're saying that my client killed Mr. X, my job as the defense attorney is to undermine that explanation.

It's not my job to tell you who did kill Mr. X.

Our problem is that Sabine "rejects an explanation without addressing the reasoning offered for that explanation".

**D. draws a conclusion about someone's motives for making a particular claim without providing evidence that any such claim was actually made** — Bad Conclusion Match (6% of students)
Does the author conclude something about someone's motives?

Nope. The conclusion is about the efficacy of the herbal supplement. The premise is about the motives of the barber.

Also, the second half of this answer is trying to make an objection like, "But we don't even know if a claim was made!" We definitely know a claim was made.

**E. rejects a claim merely because the person making the claim stands to benefit by doing so** — Correct (92% of students)
Does the author reject a claim in his conclusion?
Yes, "That is simply not the case."

Is the author's sole premise that the person making the claim stands to benefit?
Yes, "your barber makes money convincing people of that"

This is the famous Ad Hominem flaw.

## S2 · Q8 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus
Analyst: When Johnson attacked his opponent by quoting her out of context, his campaign defended this attack by claiming that the quote was even more politically damaging to her in context. But those who run his campaign clearly do not believe this. They have since had plenty of chances to refer to the quote in its proper context but continue to quote it out of context.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, most strongly supports the analyst's reasoning above?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The people who run Johnson's campaign clearly don't believe that "quoting his opponent in context is even more politically damaging then quoting her out of context". (i.e. the people running Johnson's campaign think that quoting his opponent out of context is equally or more damaging)
- **Evidence:** They continue to quote her out of context, even though they've had plenty of chances to say the quote in its proper context.
- **Evaluate / Goal:** The argument is essentially,
```
if they keep quoting        they don't think
the opponent out       →    "in context" is more
of context                  politically damaging
```
We need some answer that helps take us from "they keep using out of context" to "they think quoting out of context is equally or more damaging than in context".

**Answer choices**
**A. In criticizing an opponent, political campaigns will pursue the line of attack they believe to be most politically damaging.** — Correct (81% of students)
If political campaigns will always pursue the most politically damaging attack, and Johnson's political campaign keeps pursuing a policy of quoting his opponent out of context, then apparently Johnson's political campaign believes that quoting his opponent out of context is the most politically damaging line of attack.

Thus, this gives us strong support for the conclusion: Johnson's campaign clearly does not believe that quoting her in context is the more politically damaging version.

**B. In criticizing an opponent, political campaigns do not use techniques that they would find objectionable if used against their candidate.** — Bad Conclusion Match (3% of students)
Since Johnson's campaign keep using the technique of "quoting opponent out of context", then according to this rule, Johnson's campaign would not find it objectionable for the opponent's campaign to quote Johnson out of context.

But who cares? We're not trying to decide whether Johnson's campaign would or wouldn't find it objectionable for their candidate to be quoted out of context.

We're trying to decide whether Johnson's campaign believes or doesn't believe that quoting his opponent "in context" is more politically damaging.

## S2 · Q18 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** C

### Stimulus
Some killer whales eat fish exclusively, but others also eat seals. Different groups of killer whales "chatter" in distinct dialects, and the dialects of seal-eating killer whales are recognizably different from those of killer whales that do not eat seals. Harbor seals use their ability to distinguish between different killer-whale dialects to avoid seal-eating killer whales. Marine biologists hypothesize that young harbor seals start with an aversion to all killer whales but then learn to ignore those that do not eat seals.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, provides the strongest support for the biologists' hypothesis?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Hypothesis:** Young harbor seals start off with an aversion to all killer whales but then learn to ignore those that do not eat seals.
- **Evaluate:** When we see the word hypothesis in the question stem, we know that we can use a Curious Fact / Explanation mindset to think about the paragraph (hypothesis = causal explanation). We don't know whether we'll see a typical argument format in which the hypothesis is supported by some evidence or whether it'll just be a hypothesis thrown out there without any particular support. This one is the latter. We can use the hypothesis to figure out what would be the Curious Question that this hypothesis would be answering. Curious Question: How is it that seals distinguish between the harmful killer-whales and the harmless killer-whales? Hypothesis: They start off afraid of all killer whales but then learn to ignore the harmless ones. We can evaluate hypotheses through two lenses: - is there some other way to explain this? - how plausible is the author's way? What could be a different storyline for how the seals distinguish between harmful / harmless killer whales? - maybe they're just born with a genetic ability to differentiate it - maybe they start out being unafraid of all killer-whales and then learn to avoid the ones that are harmful
- **Goal:** Since we're doing Strengthen, a correct answer could rule out one of these alternative hypotheses, but it's more likely that the correct answer gives us a way to help the plausibility of the author's hypothesis. We would want to see baby seals afraid of all killer whales and then once they learn the dialect of the harmless whales, no longer being afraid of them. A common Strengthen answer form is No Cause / No Effect, which could sound something like "harbor seals who are deaf (and thus can never learn to decipher the dialect of the harmless whales) remain afraid of whales their whole lives".

**Answer choices**
**A. Killer whales that eat seals also eat other marine mammals that are similar in size to seals.** — Out of Scope: other marine mammals (1% of students)
The fact that the harmful whales also eat other big mammals doesn't tell us anything about how seals acquire their ability to distinguish between harmful / harmless whales.

**B. Unlike harbor seals, which can hear killer-whale chatter even at great distances, most fish cannot hear that chatter, even close at hand.** — Out of Scope: most fish (2% of students)
We really don't care whether other aquatic animals do / don't have good hearing. The seal has good enough hearing to distinguish between the harmful / harmless whales. We already know that from the evidence. We don't need to be reassured that they have good enough hearing. We want new information that helps us judge the means by which they figure out the difference between harmful / harmless dialects.

**C. When mature harbor seals first listen to the recorded chatter of killer whales that eat only fish but whose dialect is unfamiliar, the seals rapidly swim away from the sound.** — Correct (59% of students)
This is a No Cause, No Effect answer in the sense that the story we're being sold is this:
```
1st              2nd              3rd
seals are        seals learn      seals start
born scared of   dialects of      ignoring the
all k-whales     harmful vs       harmless ones
                 harmless
```
This answer is emphasizing that "until they learn to distinguish dialects, they are scared by the sound of k-whales".

The fact that this is a mature seal makes the answer harder to compute, but it's establishing that a seal's default position to an unknown whale is "I'm afraid". Over time, the seal can be convinced that a given dialect is associated with harmless behavior, but it ain't takin' any chances at first.

So "if a seal hasn't learned a harmless k-whale's dialect" (No Cause)
"it has not yet learned to ignore it (it is still in the initial state of aversion)" (No Effect)

**D. Young harbor seals show no natural aversion to any seal predators other than killer whales.** — Weakens, slightly (10% of students)
This makes the author's hypothesis less plausible. If seals are born with innate aversions to this predator (killer whales), then why wouldn't they have innate aversions to other predators?

**E. If a fish-eating killer whale mistakenly attacks a harbor seal, that seal, if it survives, will subsequently avoid all killer whales that chatter in the attacker's dialect, but other harbor seals will not.** — Unclear Impact (28% of students)
According to this story, "being attacked" is the causal difference-maker that explains the difference between the attacked seal and all the other seals.

Does it help the author's hypothesis to say that "being attacked > having a supposedly harmless dialect"?

No, not at all. If anything, the fact that an attacked seal starts avoiding all whales with that dialect makes it seem like actions (attacks) speak louder than words (dialect).

But more importantly, this answer says nothing regarding whether seals are being born with fear and acquire partial-trust, or whether they're being born with trust and acquire partial-fear.

- **Evidence:** An athlete injured her lower back after a strenuous competition. She tried stretching for a few days but the pain persisted. She then used a heating pad, and within a few days the pain was gone.
- **Evaluate:** Since part of this argument involves a Causal Interpretation of a before/after, we can use our Curious Fact / Explanation mindset. The conclusion is only about whether or not heating pads are better at relieving pain (not about whether they're better at healing the underlying injury).

So in the first half of this answer choice, we are awarding this author the victory, rather than resisting the conclusion he is trying to draw.

**B. It fails to consider the fact that lower back pain resulting from athletic competitions often disappears after several days regardless of any attempts to relieve it.** — Alternate Explanation (9% of students)
This is saying, "Maybe the heating pad wasn't why the pain went away; lower back pain from a sports injury often just goes away on its own after a handful of days".

**C. It fails to consider that the athlete's experience regarding the effectiveness of different methods of relieving lower back pain may not have been representative of that of the general population.** — Unrepresentative Sample (9% of students)
This answer calls out the Sampling move in the conclusion. Even if we grant that a heating pad was more effective for this athlete, that wouldn't show that heating pads are generally more effective, because her experience may have been atypical.

**D. It overlooks the possibility that the effectiveness of different methods of relieving lower back pain may vary substantially depending on the underlying cause of the lower back pain.** — Unrepresentative Sample (5% of students)
This answer calls out the Sampling move in the conclusion. Even if we grant that a heating pad was more effective for this athlete, that wouldn't show that heating pads are generally more effective, because her injury may have been one that was more conducive to heating pads than other injuries would have been.

## S2 · Q25 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** B

### Stimulus
Katelin says that we will be hit by a major snowstorm tomorrow. So she probably believes that tomorrow's antique car show will be canceled, for it will certainly not be held if we are hit by a major snowstorm.

### Prompt
The questionable pattern of reasoning in the argument above is most closely parallel to that in which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:**
```
Katelin says we'll be       Thing A predicts X.
hit by a major
snowstorm tomorrow.

If we are hit be a major    If X happens,
snowstorm, then the         Y happens.
antique car show will
be canceled.
```
- **Conclusion:**
```
Katelin must believe        Thing A believes
the antique car show        Y will happen.
will be canceled
```
- **Evaluate:** This is a flaw that has been used a handful of times throughout LSAT's history. It's assuming that someone believes an Implication of their Belief, which is flawed because we don't know whether the person has enough information to connect one belief to its implication (or whether they have the will/skill to derive inferential beliefs, if they do have that information). For example: Katelin says that Clark Kent will be late to this morning's meeting. Clark Kent is Superman. So, Katelin probably believes that Superman will be late to this morning's meeting. Maybe you and I know that Clark Kent is Superman, but we don't know if Katelin does. If she doesn't know "Clark Kent is Superman", then there'd be no reason for her to have any belief about Superman being late to the meeting. Similarly, if she has no idea that "a major snowstorm would effectively cancel the antique car show" (she might not even be aware there is an antique car show planned), then she might not have any beliefs about tomorrow's antique car show.
- **Goal:** Look for an example where one Premise says "Person A believes X". A second premise should convey, "if X, then Y". And then the Conclusion should be saying, "so Person A probably believes Y."

**Answer choices**
**A. Jorge says that given today's pollution levels, many species of migratory birds will become extinct. Thus, he probably believes that the extinctions will occur, since it is widely known that pollution might not be reduced at all.** — Weak Premise Match (12% of students)
We do have someone with a belief:
Jorge believes many migratory birds will become extinct.

Is there an implication stated of what happens if migratory birds become extinct?

No, the other premise doesn't even talk about birds. It just says "pollution might not be reduced at all".

**B. Bo says that the soil in his backyard is poorly drained, and since raspberry bushes will not grow well in soil that is poorly drained, Bo probably believes that raspberry bushes will not grow well in his backyard.** — Correct (65% of students)
We do have someone with a belief:
Bo believes his backyard soil is poorly drained.

Is there an implication stated of what happens if soil is poorly drained?

Yes! If soil is poorly drained, then raspberry bushes won't grow well.

So does the conclusion accuse Bo of believing that "raspberry bushes won't grow well"?

Yes, and it correctly matches the strength of the original conclusion (probably).

**C. Wanda says that no form of coercive force is ever justified. Thus, since most people who say this believe that government should be abolished, Wanda probably believes that government should be abolished.** — Weak Premise Match / Valid Logic (7% of students)
We do have someone with a belief:
W believes no coercive force is ever justified.

Is there an implication stated of what happens if it's never justified to use coercive force?

Not quite. The other premise doesn't say that something else is implied by this belief; it just says that most people who have this first belief also have this 2nd belief.

So concluding that W probably also has this 2nd belief is actually a valid probabilistic conclusion, since "most" people who believe what she believes hold that 2nd belief too.

**D. My chemistry professor says that most chemists are good at math. So, my chemistry professor might believe that most chemists are good at chess, since most people who are good at math are also good at chess.** — Bad Conclusion/Premise Match (10% of students)
The quickest way to get rid of this answer is to peek at the conclusion and see that it's saying "my chemistry professor might believe". That's different from concluding "probably believes".

Concluding that someone might believe something is incredibly safe, usually valid. It would only be invalid if we had established that it would be impossible for someone to believe something.

So we're not going to replicate the flaw with "might".

If we happened to dig in deeper, we do have someone with a belief:
chemistry professor believes that most chemists are good at math.

Is there an implication stated of what happens if most chemists are good at math?

Not really. There's no conditional implication. There's some chance that the chemist who's good at math is also good at chess, but the two things don't need to overlap.

**E. Dr. Bowder says that eating garlic increases one's alertness. So, since she knows that whatever improves one's circulation increases one's alertness, Dr. Bowder probably believes that eating garlic increases one's circulation.** — Bad Premise Match (6% of students)
We do have someone with a belief:
Dr. Bowder believes eating garlic increases one's alertness.

Is there an implication stated of what happens if garlic increases alertness?

No, there's just a premise saying that "something else also increases alertness".

2 problems:
- we wouldn't get any implication by combining "eat garlic → more alert" and "improve circulation → more alert", because those two don't chain together.

- this second premise is identified as something Dr. Bowder knows. In order to mimic the original argument, the second premise should be a conditional relationship, but we aren't supposed to know whether or not the person we're talking about is aware of that relationship.

This answer choice is acting more like, "Katelin thinks Clark Kent will be late. And, since Katelin knows that Clark Kent is Superman, .... "

**Correct answer:** B

People should patronize businesses that meet high ethical standards, and the news media should help them to patronize those businesses. Therefore, when a business performs a notably ethical action, the news media should publicize that fact, for hearing of a business's ethical conduct is often enough to motivate people to patronize that business.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the argument?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If a business does something notably ethical positive, the news media should publicize that fact.
- **Evidence:** People should patronize businesses that meet high ethical standards, and when people hear about a business's ethical conduct (i.e. through news media publicity) they are often motivated to support that business.
- **Evaluate:** GIVEN that we should patronize businesses with high ethical standards, and GIVEN that people often patronize a business when they hear about it acting ethically, HOW COULD WE ARGUE that when a business performs a notably ethical action, the news media should not necessarily publicize that fact? The one way that came to me was if it's a transparent publicity stunt, we don't want to promote it and give that fossil fuel enabling company the good press it deserves. Like if Exxon is accelerating climate change, the media shouldn't cover a "tree-planting ceremony" Exxon is doing near city hall and then motivate people to patronize Exxon as though they're some highly ethical company.
- **Goal:** Find way to say that sometimes the media might drive people to patronize a business that isn't meeting high ethical standards, they're just doing a notably ethical action to get good publicity.

**Answer choices**
**A. Some businesses that have high ethical standards do not actually meet those standards.** — Too Weak (11% of students)
We need an objection like "many businesses that perform a notably ethical action are not meeting high ethical standards in general".

**B. Meeting high ethical standards is primarily a matter of refraining from unethical behavior.** — Correct (46% of students)
This is a tough correct answer, but it's helping us to argue that a company that performed a notably ethical action isn't necessarily meeting high ethical standards.

It essentially makes the objection that "meeting high ethical standards is about not doing harm, it isn't about doing something nice, like a tree-planting".

Our author is thinking that when a company does a notably ethical action, they reveal themselves to be meeting high ethical standards (and so the media should publicize them).

This answer is saying "a notably ethical action does not reveal that a company is meeting high ethical standards. We need to judge that by seeing whether they refrain from unethical behavior."

If the media's goal is to publicize highly ethical companies, they should go based off whether a business refrains from doing something unethical.

**C. It is relatively easy for a business to meet its ethical standards if it does not set them very high.** — Out of Scope: meeting its standards (15% of students)
The argument doesn't talk about ethical standards as a company by company thing. It's acting like "high ethical standards" is an objective measurement that we can say businesses do or don't meet.

This answer would have worked if the argument had said, "People should patronize businesses that meet high ethical standards. And since Company X for 10 years has met the ethical standards they set for themselves, the media should publicize them." We might object that they set really easy standards to meet, and thus aren't necessarily highly ethical.

**D. The news media is more likely to publicize a business's unethical conduct than it is to publicize a business's ethical conduct.** — Out of Scope: more publicized (16% of students)
This is a normative argument about what media should do. What they do do in practice is not relevant. It wouldn't make any difference to this argument if the media is currently more likely to publicize bad behavior than good behavior.

**E. Some businesses that meet high ethical standards would not do so if they could not remain profitable while meeting those standards.** — Too Weak / No Impact (12% of students)
Answers with "some" are almost always wrong on Strengthen / Weaken / Paradox, because some is "at least one", so you really only get one data point with that strength.

Either way, it's not relevant to the argument whether companies would abandon high ethical standards were those standards keeping them from being profitable. As long as some companies are meeting high ethical standards, the author's argument can work. He's saying we want to patronize those, so the media should publicize them.

The only sticking point is whether "performing a notably ethical action" is a reliable indicator of high ethical standards.

### Stimulus
The coat patterns of large cat species correspond to the habitats in which those species live and hunt. Species with spotted coats are at home in trees and dappled forests, while species living in the open plains, such as lions, have plain coats. Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the anomaly described above?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that ...:** Coats of cats match habitats: - spotted coats go with trees / forests - plain coats go with open plains
- **How can we explain that ...:** The cheetah is a spotted cat that lives in the open savannah?
- **Evaluate:** We can use a little real world common sense here to help us with this one (normally that's a dangerous move on Paradox, but on earlier questions they tend to align with our real world common sense). Why do coat patterns normally correspond to a habitat? Because cats are hunters who camouflage themselves and lie in wait. So in a big golden plain, a plain coat is camouflage. In a densely contoured forest environment, spotted coats help blend in with the dappled mix of sunlight and shadows. So how come the cheetah gets away with living in the open savannah, with some loud coat of spots? Doesn't that keep it from being able to camouflage itself?
- **Goal:** The correct answer will probably explain to us why the cheetah doesn't need camouflage (it doesn't have to hide from any predators / it doesn't need to hide from its prey because it's so fast it'll catch ya no matter what), or it will explain some other means cheetah have of obtaining the camouflage effect of having your coat match your habitat. In general terms, we want an answer to show us why a cheetah doesn't need to have a matching coat for the sake of camouflage.

## S3 · Q11 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus
Studies have shown that those who take daily doses of vitamin C are less likely to contract colds than are those who do not. Thus, if a person contracts a cold, he or she probably does not take daily doses of vitamin C.

### Prompt
The questionable reasoning in the argument above most closely parallels that in which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (thus):** If a person contracts a cold, he or she probably doesn't take daily doses of vitamin C.
- **Evidence:** Studies show those who take daily doses of vitamin C are less likely to contract colds than are those who do not.
- **Evaluate:** At first this might seem like pretty sound reasoning. Since taking vitamin C daily seems to make one less likely to get a cold, we might think that someone with a cold probably doesn't take daily vitamin C. If we don't understand the flaw, we can at least focus on the structure, and the structure may be enough to get us to the correct answer. Since the ideas of "vitmain C" and "contracting a cold" appear in both premise and conclusion, this argument lends itself to an algebraic representation.
```
Prem:
People who do X are less likely to have Y happen

Conc:
If Y happens to someone, then they probably don't do X
```
Why is this actually a flawed argument? It's because there might be way more people who take daily vitamin C than those who don't. So even though taking vitamin C makes you less likely to get a cold, there will still be a ton of people who take vitamin C with colds. Here's an analogy. Say there are 300 million or so natural born citizens in the U.S. and maybe 20 million naturalized citizens (i.e. they were born elsewhere but at some point moved to the U.S., passed the citizenship test, and got U.S. citizenship). Foreign-born citizens of the U.S. are usually more likely to know American history (because they had to learn it to pass the citizenship test) and usually more likely to know formal rules of English grammar (because they had to learn English mainly through classes, not through informal conversation). So we could probably say that natural born U.S. citizens are less likely to know American history or less likely to know formal rules of grammar. But does that mean if we find an American who happens to know history or grammar, that they are probably not a natural born citizen? Or to put it another way, does that mean that "most of the Americans who know U.S. history or formal rules of grammar are foreign-born?" No, it doesn't. Even though foreign-born Americans are more likely to know that stuff, they are vastly outnumbered in raw totals by the quantity of natural born citizens, so it's still the case that "most of the Americans who know U.S. history and rules of grammar are natural born".
- **Goal:** Anywayyyy, since that is a tricky statistical flaw to understand, we are probably best served by taking our algebraic recipe into the answers.
```
Prem:
People who do X are less likely to have Y happen

Conc:
If Y happens to someone, then they probably don't do X
```

**Answer choices**
**A. To be classified as a hurricane, a tropical storm in the Atlantic Ocean must have sustained wind speeds of over 74 miles (119 kilometers) per hour. So tropical storms with sustained wind speeds at about that level are not hurricanes.** — Bad Conclusion Match (2% of students)
Since the original conclusion is a conditional claim with a "probably" strength, this conclusion here doesn't look at all tempting.

It could be written as a conditional, but it would have a certain outcome "are not hurricanes".

We can bail for that reason on a first pass. If we needed to dig deeper, we would see that the premise is a rigid "to be X, you must be Y" rule.

Our premise was "people who are X are less likely to be Y".

**B. Everyone who ate the tuna salad prepared in the cafeteria has contracted botulism. So those who ate other dishes prepared in the cafeteria will probably also get this disease.** — Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
The conclusion is making a "probably" prediction, so this is worth reading.

However, the evidence isn't giving us a claim like, "People who are X are more/less likely to be Y".

It's giving us a Universal:
All who ate tuna got botulism.

**C. Laboratory rats given an extremely low-calorie diet live up to 20 percent longer than do those given a more ordinary diet. So most people could extend their life spans by eating a great deal less than they usually do.** — Bad Conclusion Match (1% of students)
This conclusion is not a "probably"-strength prediction, so we can bail on a first pass.

If we looked deeper, we'd also notice a switch from evidence about "rats" to a conclusion about "people".

The original argument definitely didn't contain any category switch like that.

**D. People who train diligently to play poker can achieve a certain level of proficiency, so those players owe their success to hard work alone.** — Bad Conclusion Match (1% of students)
The conclusion is a very strong, categorial claim:
People owe their success to X alone

We're looking for a softer conclusion:
People who are X are probably Y

That's reason enough to bail on a first pass.

If we looked deeper, the premise is not at all of the form "people who are X are more likely to be Y".

**E. Automobile engines that undergo regular oil changes are more likely to be problem free than are those that do not. So automobiles with engine problems probably have not had regular oil changes.** — Correct (96% of students)
Good news — the structure was enough!

Our original algebra was like this:

P: ppl who are X are less likely to be Y
C: something that is Y is probably not X

P: People who take daily C are less likely to have cold.
C: So someone that has cold is probably not taking daily C.

This argument is saying,
P: Engines that get regular oil changes are more likely to be problem free.
C: So engines with problems probably don't have regular oil changes.

We would just have to rearrange how that first sentence gets expressed to line up the same "less likely":

P: Cars that get regular oil changes are less likely to have engine problems.
C: So if a car has an engine problem, it probably doesn't get regular oil changes.

And just like the original argument (and our analogous one about U.S. citizens), this logic doesn't have to hold. There might be way more cars that get regular oil changes than don't. So even though those oil changes make you less likely to have an engine problem, there are still going to be a ton of cars that have regular oil changes that still end up with engine problems.

Guam has 40 times more spiders than nearby islands have. Biologists argue that this is a consequence of the accidental 1940s introduction into Guam of the brown tree snake, which by the 1980s had eliminated ten of twelve native bird species. Curious Fact ... why does Guam have so many more spiders than nearby islands? Author's Explanation ... because Guam has lost 10 of its 12 bird species (in 1940 they introduced a tree snake that decimated 10 of 12 native species by the 1980s, and these birds used to prey on spiders and use their webs in constructing nests). Of course spiderwebs are more prevalent. There are 40 times as many spiders. I bet there's more spider poop too. This is telling us something we would have already known from common sense.

So the more birds we think there are, the more we'd be weakening the argument.

We don't care whether the brown tree snakes still exist, are extinct, are easy to kill, are hard to kill.

## S3 · Q15 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus
If the glee club pays cash today to rent the equipment it needs for next Saturday's party, its usual rental agency will accept a much lower price than the club would pay for renting the equipment next Saturday. The club's party committee knows the costs involved but nonetheless will wait until next Saturday to pay. The committee clearly cares little about saving money.

### Prompt
Which one of the following assumptions is required by the argument above?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The glee club's party committee clearly cares little about saving money.
- **Evidence:** They could pay in cash today to rent the equipment they need for next Saturday's party, or they could wait until the day of the party and would have to then pay a much higher price. They know this, but plan to wait to rent the equipment on the day of the party.
- **Evaluate:** If we wanted to anticipate a LINKING answer, it might sound like, "If you're knowingly forsaking a chance to pay a much lower price for something you need, then you care little about saving money". But I would suspect this is headed into DEFENDER territory, so let's think about what type of Objections we could make to this argument. Given that ... they're electing to pay more in order to pay the day of the party, How can we argue ... they care a lot about saving money! We would basically just have to make an excuse for their behavior. Why are they waiting? - maybe it's just not feasible for them to rent the equipment now: they have no place to temporarily store it / they don't have the money for it yet in their budget, but will by next Saturday / they can only buy stuff via credit card, not cash. - maybe they're not 100% sure the event will happen. Perhaps it might get rained out, and they don't want to waste money on prematurely renting equipment for an event that doesn't end up happening. - maybe they're still shopping around trying to find a better price. It says that this is their usual rental agency, but maybe they think it's worth waiting until next Saturday and paying more in case they can scope out a cheaper way to rent this equipment before then.
- **Goal:** Since the author is answering a Curious Fact (why aren't they renting the equipment now, for much less?) with an Explanation (they care very little about saving money), and since this is Necessary Assumption, the correct answer will probably either: - rule out some Alternate Explanation for they're waiting until next Saturday to rent it, or - establish something that would need to be true for the Author's Explanation to be plausible (i.e. they do have enough money and the storage capacity to rent it now)

**Answer choices**
**A. At least some influential members of the club's party committee are interested in the savings available by renting the equipment today rather than renting it next Saturday.** — Too Specific / Weakens (1% of students)
There's no way we could derive from this paragraph that our author is clearly envisioning this specific scenario about internal disagreements between some influential members and the rest of the committee.

Furthermore, if we negated this, it would be saying, "There are not any influential members pushing the committee to rent the equipment today", and that would just strengthen the author's case.

**B. Most members of the club's party committee are aware of the glee club's financial situation.** — Too Strong: most (5% of students)
The word "most" is wrong on Necessary Assumption 99% of time we see it, because arguments don't usually hinge on whether something is at least 51% or at most 49%.

If we negate this, we're just saying, "at most 49% of the party committee is aware of the glee club's financial situation". Does that badly weaken the argument?

No. Maybe people would think, "it shows that the decision to wait until next Saturday is a minority decision, so maybe this minority doesn't care about saving money, but the remaining majority does."

The problem is that "being aware of the club's financial situation" is not necessary in order to weigh in on whether to rent the equipment today vs. next Saturday.

There are plenty of rich people who have a very secure financial situation but still care a lot about saving money. In other words, you wouldn't base your decision about whether to rent today or next Saturday on your financial situation.

In any financial situation, cheaper = better, if you care about saving money.

**C. At least one rental agency will not accept a lower price for equipment rental today than it would accept next Saturday.** — Out of Scope: other agencies policies (5% of students)
This is saying that the author has to assume that there is at least one rental agency out there that doesn't offer this "early-bird discount".

Why would the author need to assume there's a rental agency that doesn't offer this pre-pay discount? If we negate this answer, it's saying "all rental agencies will accept a lower price today than they will next Saturday".

If anything, that negation would strengthen the author's argument. He could say, "Everybody's prices will be higher next Saturday, so the fact that they're waiting shows they don't care about saving money."

**D. The club's party committee has a number of responsibilities that take priority over saving money.** — Weakens (8% of students)
This actually seems to offer an alternative explanation for why the club is waiting. They would like to save money by renting the equipment today, sure, but they have a number of other responsibilities that take precedence.

If we negated this, it would say, "the club has no higher responsibility than saving money", which would only strengthen his position that waiting until next Saturday shows they don't really care about saving money.

**E. There is enough cash available to the glee club today to pay for the equipment rental at today's lower price.** — Correct (81% of students)
We could say this establishes a minimum plausibility for the author's explanation that the glee club knows about this discount opportunity, has the means to take advantage of this discount opportunity, but is eschewing it anyway.

We could also say this rules out an alternate explanation for why the glee club isn't renting the equipment today. If we negated this, it would say that the glee club doesn't have enough cash to rent the equipment today.

Would that weaken? Yes, a ton. It would allow us to say, "Yo, author — the committee cares a lot about saving money. The real reason they're not renting it today, at a cheaper rate, is that they just don't have cash to do so."

The paragraph never specified that this early-bird discount needed to be paid in cash, but the sufficient trigger that allows them to get the discount does specify "paying cash today".

In order for the author to conclude that the glee club is carelessly passing up the sweet discount offer that she has presented for us, she has to assume that the glee club has the ability to take advantage of that offer in the first place.

## S3 · Q17 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus
After monitoring blood levels of lycopene (a nutrient found in some fruits and vegetables) in 1,000 middle-aged study participants over a 12-year period, researchers found that participants with low levels of lycopene were more than twice as likely as those with high levels to have a stroke during that period. Clearly, lycopene reduces the risk of stroke.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (clearly):** Lycopene reduces the risk of stroke.
- **Evidence:** In a study of 1,000 middle-aged people over a 12-year period, the people with low levels of lycopene were more than twice as likely as those with high levels to have a stroke during that period.
- **Evaluate:** We've got the classic pattern of "Evidence = Correlation" and "Conclusion = Causality". The most common formulation for "X and Y are correlated" is to say "People who are X are more likely than those who aren't to be Y". And then the conclusion is using the causal verb reduces. Whenever the author's conclusion assumes to states a causal explanation for the curious comparison presented in the evidence, then we go into our Explain Curious Fact mindset. Curious Fact ... why is low lycopene correlated with more strokes / why is high lycopene correlated with fewer strokes? Author's Explanation ... Lycopene reduces the risk of stroke. We always evaluate this template with the same two questions: 1. Can we think of an Alternate Explanation for the Curious Fact? 2. How plausible is the Author's Explanation? Any time we're given a correlation between x and y, and then the author assumes or concludes that x causes y, we automatically react with these two alternate explanation possibilities: - could it be y causes x? (Reverse Causality) - could it be z causes x and y? (3rd factor) Could having a stroke cause low levels of lycopene? Sounds weird, but maybe it's possible. Could there be some 3rd factor that both explains why someone would have low lycopene and explain why they have more strokes than average? Sure, maybe there's a genetic condition that leads to both. Maybe the people who consume the least lycopene have some other behavior that increases their risk of stroke (perhaps bodybuilders avoid fruits and veggies in favor of protein, so they have low lycopene, and the strain that weightlifting puts on their bodies increases their risk of strokes. That would create a correlation between low lycopene and more strokes, even though the lycopene wasn't causing the strokes.)
- **Goal:** Since this is Weaken, we primarily expect an answer to - suggest an Alternate Explanation for why low lycopene is correlated with strokes but we'd also consider an answer that - hurts the plausibility of the Author's notion that lycopene reduces the risk of stroke. That parenthetical detail that lycopene is "a nutrient found in some fruits and veggies" feels like a Breadcrumb, like a detail that didn't need to be in the paragraph, so it's probably in there because the correct answer is going to use it in some way.

**Answer choices**
**A. Most fruits and vegetables that are rich in lycopene also contain high levels of several other nutrients that are thought likely to reduce the risk of stroke.** — Correct (79% of students)
Here we get a 3rd Factor Alternate Explanation.

The real causal factors are nutrients XYZ. They reduce the risk of stroke. They happen to be found in fruits and veggies that are rich in lycopene. So people who eat these fruits and veggies are getting their risk of stroke reduced by nutrients XYZ. And they're simultaneously ingesting a lot of lycopene.

This explains why there ends up being a correlation between lycopene and fewer strokes, but lycopene isn't the causal factor reducing the risk of stroke; nutrients XYZ are.

**B. Countries in which people consume substantial quantities of lycopene-rich fruits and vegetables generally have lower rates of stroke than other countries.** — Strengthens (2% of students)
This provides more data points that fit the author's hypothesis (More Cause, More Effect). She thinks that consuming lycopene causes lower risk of stroke, and this is talking about countries that eat a lot of lycopene and have a lower risk of stroke.

**C. Middle-aged people typically have lower lycopene levels than young adults.** — Unclear Impact (5% of students)
Because the answer fails to tell us whether middle-aged people have more / fewer strokes than young adults, we can't really do anything with it.

If we made a common sense guess that "the older you are, the higher your risk of stroke", then this would strengthen, because the older middle-aged people have lower lycopene and more strokes, just like the correlation in the argument.

Just like (B), this would represent more data points that support the plausibility of the author's conclusion.

**D. Study participants with high levels of lycopene consumed, on average, twice the quantity of fruits and vegetables as those with low levels of lycopene.** — No Impact (8% of students)

## S3 · Q25 — Principle-Conform

**Correct answer:** D

### Stimulus
Ecological terms like "invasive species" invoke human cultural standards like belonging, citizenship, fair play, and morality and apply them to the natural world. These terms can influence ecologists' opinions of certain organisms before any data is gathered about their ecological impact. To prevent this, they should avoid using such terms.

### Prompt
Which one of the following conforms most closely to the principle illustrated above?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion / Recommendation:** Ecologists should avoid using terms like "invasive species".
- **Evidence:** Terms like that invoke human cultural standards about fairness and can influence ecologists' opinions of certain organisms before any data is gathered about their impact.
- **Evaluate:** If an author is saying, "You should avoid doing X, because doing X leads to Y", then the author is assuming the principle that "you should avoid doing things that lead to Y". Here the author is saying, "Ecologists should avoid using terms like invasive species, because those terms are value-laden and bias ecologists to be hatin' on certain organisms before any data's been gathered on their impact."
- **Goal:** So the author is assuming the principle that, "ecologists should avoid having opinion of organisms before any data is gathered about their impact." But we need our principle to be more generic / abstract, so that it could apply to a different topic. We should look for stuff like: - avoid doing X because doing X would cause you to have a baked-in prejudice before you've even seen the data

**Answer choices**
**A. Police should not use terms like "thief" when talking to suspects no matter how strong the evidence of their guilt, since suspects are officially innocent until they have been convicted by a court.** — Weak Match (18% of students)
This has a similar conclusion about, "should not use a certain term", but is the evidence saying, "If you use that term, you'll have a negative association with something before any evidence has been examined"?

No, it's saying, "don't use that term even if you have really strong evidence".

This answer could be fine if it said, "Police should not use terms like thief when talking about suspects before any evidence has been gathered concerning their potential guilt."

**B. Environmental regulators should not argue publicly that particular substances are harmful, since the government's decisions about which substances are harmful should be made by legislators.** — Bad Evidence Match (2% of students)
This also recommends that a certain occupation avoid saying certain things, but the evidence here is that these people are the right people to be deciding such matters.

In the original, we were saying that ecologists shouldn't use certain terms (not because it was someone else's job to decide on those terms, but) because they would form a negative opinion of certain organisms before any data is gathered about whether they're actually bad for an ecosystem.

**C. Opinion writers should avoid using overly unflattering language to describe their opponents' views on controversial issues, since such language often leads readers simply to discount the views of the writer that uses it.** — Bad Evidence Match (10% of students)
This also recommends that a certain occupation avoid saying certain things, but the evidence here is that saying such things will turn the audience against you.

In the original, we were saying that ecologists shouldn't use certain terms (not because it would make other people discount the views of the ecologist, but) because the ecologists would have a prejudiced feeling about an organism before data is gathered about whether it's actually bad for an ecosystem.

**D. Software developers should refrain from using psychological terms to describe the performance of their programs, since this could influence their expectations about how those programs will handle unanticipated inputs.** — Correct (69% of students)
This recommends that a certain occupation avoid saying certain things, because using terms would influence how software developers think about how their programs will react to surprise situations.

It's a bit of a strained match, but there are a couple key similarities:
- people in occupation X shouldn't use certain terms because it will change how people in that occupation think about certain things

This is the only answer that says, "Person A shouldn't use term X, because using term X to refer to thing Y will change how Person A thinks about thing Y."

That would be enough to make this the best available answer, but we also have a second similarity:

- Ecologists were judging that a new organism was "bad" for an ecosystem because of the loaded term "invasive", before they actually had data on it.

- Software developers were describing their software as "sensitive, nimble responsive" and judging thusly that their software would react adeptly to unanticipated inputs, before they actually have data on how the software will respond to those inputs.

**E. People should be careful when attributing motives to another person in order to explain that person's behavior, since psychological studies suggest that such attributions are frequently incorrect.** — Bad Evidence/Conclusion Match (1% of students)
This isn't about recommending that an occupation avoid certain terms, so it's not as much like the original.

And the evidence isn't saying that "using a term like this will bias the professional's thinking before they actually have data on the subject".

Instead, the evidence is saying, "making this sort of prediction usually means that your prediction is wrong".

## S3 · Q26 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus
Employee: Vernon's behavior in last month's incident was certainly unprofessional enough that our company was justified in firing him. But several higher-ranking employees whose behavior in the incident was just as unprofessional haven't been fired and are treated as employees in good standing. So for the sake of consistency, the company must give Vernon his job back.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the employee's argument?

### Written (single style)
> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If the company is going to be consistent, then they must give Vernon his job back.
- **Evidence:** Vernon got (rightfully) fired for unprofessional behavior, but several higher-ranking employees who acted similarly didn't get fired and are even in good standing.
- **Evaluate:** We might intuitively be offended by this argument and hear ourselves saying, "Wait a sec, the answer is to also fire the offending higher-ranking employees, not to give all of you a pass." If that didn't occur to us, we would try to get there by attacking the conclusion. We would need to argue that "for the sake of consistency, we shouldn't give Vernon his job back or we need't do so, there's some other way to be consistent ..." At that point we might think, "the other way to be consistent would be to fire the other people who should have gotten the same treatment as Vernon.
- **Goal:** The answer will almost certainly be expressing our sentiment that you don't need to re-hire Vernon to be consistent, you could (and probably should) choose instead to just fire the higher-ranking employees that so far have not been punished.

**Answer choices**
**A. illicitly using a key term in different senses during the course of the argument** — Wrong Flaw (1% of students)
This refers to the famous Equivocation flaw, but in this argument, all the repeated terms were used consistently.

**B. confusing behavior that is sufficient to justify an action with behavior that is required to justify that action** — Wrong Flaw (16% of students)
This is the famous Necessary vs. Sufficient flaw, which is when the author presents a conditional rule and then uses it in a backwards or opposite fashion in order to get to his conclusion.

There was no conditional logic here, so as soon as we see Nec vs. Suff, we out.

**C. offering as its primary evidence a premise that is equivalent to the argument's conclusion** — Wrong Flaw (2% of students)
This refers to the famous Circular Reasoning flaw, in which
- the conclusion restates a premise
- conclusion presumed to be true
- presupposes what it seeks to establish
- assumes what it sets out to prove

This answer is wrong 99% of the time, so learning the telltale taglines makes this easier to dispense with. Circular arguments have no actual supporting ideas, just the author's presumption of being correct. This argument definitely has supporting ideas, such as descriptions of the inconsistent treatment of Vernon vs. some higher-ranking employees.

**D. treating behavior that can sometimes result in a certain consequence as behavior that always results in that consequence** — Wrong Flaw (35% of students)
This is a very seldom occurring distinction we might call Possible vs. Certain.

The flaw here had nothing to do with that sort of distinction. Because the answer has the structure, "treats X as Y", we'd want to know whether X matches the evidence and Y matches the conclusion or an idea the author had en route to the conclusion.

Was there a premise about "behavior that sometimes results in a certain consequence"?

We could potentially say Vernon's unprofessional behavior resulted in the consequence of getting fired.

Does the author now start thinking/concluding that "unprofessional behavior always results in getting fired"?

Definitely not. In fact the opposite. The next part of the argument is presenting counterexamples where unprofessional behavior did not result in that consequence.

This answer might be closer to working if it said, "treats behavior that can sometimes results in a certain consequence as behavior that should always result in that consequence". With that answer, we'd have to interpret it as "since unprofessional behavior SOMETIMES results in not-getting fired, it should always result in not-getting fired".

**E. inferring that one specific response to a problem is necessary without considering another equally supported response** — Correct (45% of students)
This is just testing whether we heard the alternative option: you know, the other way you could be consistent would be to just fire the offending higher-ranking employees.
