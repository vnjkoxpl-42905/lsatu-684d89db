# Section 2 (Logical Reasoning)

## S2 · Q2 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Anthropologist: One of the distinctive traits of humans is the ability to support a large brain with a small gut, which requires getting more calories from less food. It was likely the development of cooking that made this possible. After all, our ancestors developed large brains around the time that they began to control fire. And even today, people who choose to eat only raw food have difficulty getting enough calories.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the anthropologist's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** It was likely the development of cooking that made it possible for humans to support a large brain with a small gut.
- **Evidence:** Supporting a large brain with a small gut requires getting more calories from less food. Our ancestors developed large brains around the time that they began to control fire. Today, people who choose to eat only raw food have difficulty getting enough calories.
- **Evaluate:** The conclusion is a causal explanation: cooking made it possible for us to have big brains and small guts. The first piece of evidence is a conditional relationship: Big brain + small gut requires more calories from less food. The second piece of evidence is a temporal correlation: Big brains and controlling fire (which we can pretty reasonably infer is a precondition for cooking) happened around the same time. The last piece of evidence is a control group: those who don't cook food don't get enough calories. But....enough calories for what exactly? Do the raw foodists really not get enough calories to support their big ol' brains? It doesn't specify, which makes this pretty poor evidence.
- **Goal:** As is the case with all arguments, we can strengthen in a variety of ways. The goal of prediction is not to come up with what the right answer must say, but instead to think through what a right answer might do. We're given a conditional premise: Big brain + small gut requires more calories from less food. If we establish that the only way to satisfy that requirement is to cook your food, this argument looks a whole lot better.

**Answer choices**
**A. Cooked foods contain the same number of calories as raw foods.** — Weakener (1% of students)
If this is true, it weakens the argument that we need to cook to get sufficient calories for our big fat brains.

**B. Raw meat contains more calories than a similar quantity of raw vegetables.** — Irrelevant Comparison (1% of students)
We need to compare the caloric content of cooked food and raw food in a way that shows that cooked food gives us more calories. This answer compares the caloric content of two different raw foods.

**C. The human body is able to extract a similar number of calories from cooked food and raw food.** — Weakener (1% of students)
Like A, if this is true, it weakens the argument that we need to cook to get sufficient calories for those big juicy brains of ours.

**D. The human body uses more calories to process raw food than it uses to process cooked food.** — Correct (95% of students)
But boy is this one subtle! Our prediction was something that establishes we need to cook to get enough calories for our brains to be so big. That could take many forms, including a comparison of the caloric content of cooked food vs. raw food. But caloric content isn't the only relevant factor. How many of those calories we can actually extract is relevant, which answer choice C alludes to. How many calories we spend on that extraction is also relevant, and that's what makes D correct. If we have to spend more calories to process raw food than we do to process cooked food, that strengthens the argument that you'll end up with more calories overall when you cook. Think of it like profit in sales. The profit is the amount of money you collect on top of what you spent to make the thing you're selling. If you spend less to make the thing, you profit more. Similarly, if you spend fewer calories digesting cooked food, you net more calories when you eat cooked food.

**E. Domesticated plants and animals are richer in calories than their wild counterparts are.** — Irrelevant Comparison (1% of students)
We need to compare the caloric content of cooked food and raw food in a way that shows that cooked food gives us more calories. This answer compares the caloric content of domesticated vs. wild food.

## S2 · Q4 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
The northern cardinal, a nonmigratory songbird, was rare in Nova Scotia in 1980; the province was considered to be beyond that bird's usual northern range. By 2000, however, field observations indicated that northern cardinals were quite common there. The average winter temperature rose slightly over that period, so warmer winters are probably responsible for the northern cardinal's proliferation in Nova Scotia.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (so):** The increase in Northern Cardinals in Nova Scotia by 2000 is probably due to warmer winters.
- **Evidence:** The argument is built on the observed change that Northern Cardinals were rare in 1980 but became common by 2000, and we know that winters have gotten warmer from 1980 to 2000.
- **Evaluate:** This argument follows the "Explain Curious Fact" pattern, where the author provides a causal explanation for a change. *Curious Fact:* How come there are more northern cardinals in NS? *Explanation:* It's cuz the winters there are warmer now.
- **Goal:** We usually weaken Explain Curious Fact by suggesting an alternate explanation for the curious fact (what could be a *different* reason why there are more northern cardinals in NS?), and sometimes undermining the plausibility of the author's explanation (debunking that warmer winters brought them to NS).

**Answer choices**
**A. Bird feeders, an important source of nutrition to wintering birds, became far more common in Nova Scotia after 1980.** — Correct (91% of students)
This choice suggests that bird feeders became more common after 1980, providing an alternate explanation for the increase in cardinals by indicating that more abundant food sources (bird feeders) rather than warmer winters could be the reason for their population growth in Nova Scotia.

**B. Because of their red plumage, northern cardinals are easier to spot than most other songbird species are.** — No Impact (1% of students)
This states that Northern Cardinals are easier to spot than other birds, which doesn't provide a reason for their population increase or challenge the link to warmer winters. It remains neutral and doesn't affect the argument's causal claim.

**C. Some songbird species other than the northern cardinal also became more common between 1980 and 2000.** — Unclear Impact (1% of students)
This offers weak support by stating that some species became more common elsewhere, which doesn't address why Northern Cardinals specifically increased in Nova Scotia. The imprecision fails to provide a concrete alternate explanation or directly challenge the argument.

**D. According to field observations, the populations of migratory birds fluctuated less during the period from 1980 to 2000 than the populations of nonmigratory birds.** — Irrelevant Comparison (3% of students)
This discusses population fluctuations between migratory and non-migratory birds, which doesn't concern non-migratory Northern Cardinals directly nor address their increase in Nova Scotia.

**E. Birds that prey on songbirds became more common in Nova Scotia between 1980 and 2000.** — Opposite (if anything) (4% of students)
This mentions that prey on songbirds became more common, suggesting an increased threat rather than a reason for more cardinals, thereby not providing an alternate explanation for their rise.

## S2 · Q5 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A person's personality is linked to that person's genes. And since a person's genes do not ordinarily change over time, it follows that a person's personality remains unchanged with the passing of time.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is most closely parallel in its reasoning to the flawed reasoning in the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** Personality is linked to genes. Genes do not usually change over time.
- **Conclusion:** So personality does not change over time.
- **Evaluate:** Whether or not we feel good about articulating an actual objection to this logic, we can feel good about our ability to keep track of repeated concepts (personality, genes, don't change -- all said twice). Premise 1 - X is linked to Y. Premise 2 - Y doesn't change over time. Conclusion - X doesn't change over time.
- **Goal:** Let's look for something that matches the structure listed above (or something that is similarly vulnerable to an objection like, "Just because two things are linked doesn't mean that if one is the same, the other is the same. Two things could be linked even if one thing is changing and the other thing isn't."

**Answer choices**
**A. The way historians understand the First World War is related to what happened in that war. But what actually happened in that war cannot change. Therefore, historians' understanding of the war cannot change.** — Correct (91% of students)
This looks dreamy. We can match it, ingredient for ingredient. Premise 1 - X is linked to Y. Historians' understanding of WWI is linked to actual events of WWI. Premise 2 - Y doesn't change over time. Actual events of WWI cannot change. Conclusion - X doesn't change over time. Historians' understanding of WWI cannot change.

**B. Market forces are to some degree influenced by governmental actions. Hence, a change in the government's policies could result in a change in the economy.** — Bad Evidence / Conclusion Match (2% of students)
First of all, this only has one premise. That's enough to disqualify. The original argument had two premises. Secondly, this conclusion's strength of language is totally different: X could result in a change (choice A) vs. X remains unchanged over time (original)

**C. It is well known that some diseases have genetic causes. Therefore, it should be possible to prevent such diseases by manipulating the genes that cause them.** — Bad Evidence / Conclusion Match (1% of students)
First of all, this only has one premise. That's enough to disqualify. The original argument had two premises. Secondly, this conclusion's strength of language is totally different: X should be possible by doing Y vs. X remains unchanged over time (original)

**D. Getting regular exercise over a long period contributes to the prevention of heart disease. Therefore, getting regular exercise over a short period contributes slightly to the prevention of heart disease.** — Bad Evidence / Conclusion Match (2% of students)
First of all, this only has one premise. That's enough to disqualify. The original argument had two premises. Secondly, this conclusion's type of claim is totally different: X contributes slightly to Y vs. X remains unchanged over time (original)

**E. The levels of certain hormones control body temperature. Therefore, if one has a high fever, the levels of one's hormones must be elevated as well.** — Bad Evidence / Conclusion Match (5% of students)
First of all, this only has one premise. That's enough to disqualify. The original argument had two premises. Secondly, this conclusion's type of claim is totally different: If X is high, then Y is high as well vs. X remains unchanged over time (original)

## S2 · Q6 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Political analyst: Several years ago, McFarlane, the military dictator, had Brooks, the former prime minister, arrested on charges of corruption. After years of negotiation, McFarlane has pardoned Brooks, and she has agreed to join his government. Almost all of McFarlane's supporters believe that Brooks is guilty of corruption. Moreover, almost all of McFarlane's opponents will oppose anyone who agrees to join his government. So Brooks will have few supporters in this country.

### Prompt
The political analyst's argument depends on the assumption that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (So):** Brooks will have few supporters in this country.
- **Evidence:** Brooks was arrested on charges of corruption several years ago, by McFarlane (the military dictator). Brooks is now pardoned and has agreed to join McFarlane's government. Almost all of McFarlane's supporters believe Brooks is guilty of corruption. And almost all of McFarlane's opponents will oppose anyone who agrees to join his government.
- **Evaluate:** The goal is to prove that Brooks will have few supporters, and the author is trying to do so by arguing via a "Damned if you do, damned if you don't" style of binary. She's trying to make the case that, "If you're A, you don't support Brooks. And if you're not A, you don't support Brooks." M's supporter → think B is guilty of corruption. M's opponent → don't support anyone agreeing to join M's govt (as B has). We want to ask ourselves whether both of those roads end in the idea of "Brooks does not have their support", and then we also want to ask whether those are the only two starting points. The author has established that if you're an opponent of M, then you will oppose Brooks, since she has agreed to join his government. But the author hasn't established that if you're a supporter of M, that you'll oppose B. She's only established that if you're a supporter of M, you think Brooks is guilty of corruption. So there is a Missing Link assumption happening here that, "If you believe Brooks is guilty of corruption, then you won't support her." If we were trying to think of Objections (for the sake of Defender assumptions that would rule them out), we'd be trying to think of ways to argue that even given this evidence, it's possibly that Brooks has many supporters. One possible objection could be, "Maybe most of the country doesn't identify as a supporter or an opponent of M. They're just kind of neutral or ambivalent. We don't know how people who aren't opponents/supporters of M feel about Brooks. The author is sort of assuming a binary, that everyone either supports or opposes M."
- **Goal:** Look for a link from "If believe guilty of corruption, then don't support" or look for an answer ruling out the possibility that we're failing to consider people in this country who are neither supporters nor opponents.

**Answer choices**
**A. Brooks's joining McFarlane's government inappropriately gives that government a semblance of legitimacy** — Out of Scope: inappropriately / legitimacy (2% of students)
This argument has nothing to do with what effect, if any, Brooks will have by joining McFarlane's government. And it has nothing to do with the author's opinion, if any, on whether Brooks should join the government. The argument is strictly about tallying up the current number of supporters for Brooks in this country.

**B. there is less corruption in the country's government now than when Brooks was prime minister** — Out of Scope Comparison: less corruption (1% of students)
This argument has nothing to do with comparing timeframes or administrations. The author is never judging current or past levels of corruption. The argument is strictly about tallying up the current number of supporters for Brooks in this country.

**C. Brooks's political positions do not overlap with those of McFarlane** — Too Strong: do not overlap (8% of students)
This actually seems to go against the facts that are presented. It would be weird for Brooks to agree to join McFarlane's government if absolutely none of her political positions overlapped with McFarlane's. If we negated this and said, "At least one political position of Brooks overlaps with that of McFarlane", that wouldn't weaken the argument in the slightest. That wouldn't help us argue that Brooks actually has many supporters in this country.

**D. most people in the country are either supporters or opponents of McFarlane** — Correct (88% of students)
This gets at the False Choice, the imagined binary, that the author was assuming. She was thinking, "Almost all the supporters oppose Brooks. Almost all the opponents oppose Brooks. So I guess that almost everyone opposes Brooks". This is assuming that "Almost everyone is a supporter or an opponent". If we negate this answer and say, "Yo, author -- most people in this country are neither one of McFarlane's opponents nor his supporters", that would badly weaken the argument. That would mean the argument hasn't told us anything about how most citizens feel about Brooks. So she might have many supporters, maybe even a majority of citizens! Suppose we said this argument, "Almost all the baseball fans at Jackson High like Toronto Blue Jays the most. Almost all the basketballs fans at Jackson High like Toronto Raptors the most. Thus, most students at Jackson High have a favorite team that is based in Toronto." That would be assuming that "most students at Jackson High are either baseball fans or basketball fans". If most students aren't, then the evidence hasn't told us anything about the majority of students. It might be that more than 50% of students only watch football and their favorite team is based in the U.S.

**E. the charges on which Brooks was arrested were unfounded** — Out of Scope: unfounded charges (1% of students)
This argument has nothing to do with judging whether it was legit or not for Brooks to have been arrested on corruption. Whether the author believes those charges were unfounded or substantive makes no difference. The argument is strictly about tallying up the current number of supporters for Brooks in this country.

## S2 · Q7 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Amber -- fossilized tree resin sold as a gemstone -- is particularly valuable when it contains fossilized life forms. Forgers can create fake amber and, in an attempt to improve its value, often embed small, normal-appearing insects in it. Therefore, pieces that are sold as amber are far more likely to be fake if they contain normal-appearing insects than if they do not.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (Therefore):** If a piece of so-called amber contains a normal-appearing insect, it's more likely to be fake (than would be a piece of so-called amber containing no insect or a not-normal appearing insect).
- **Evidence:** Forgers create fake amber and often embed small, normal-appearing insects in it.
- **Evaluate:** The conclusion is saying that a normal-appearing insect inside a supposed piece of amber is a sign that it's probably fake amber. After all, forgers often put normal-appearing insects in their fake amber. If we were trying to object to this argument, we would have to argue that there are way more legitimate pieces of amber with normal-appearing insects in them out there on the market than there are fake pieces with normal-appearing insects in them. Say that in any given year, forgers produce about 1,000 pieces of fake amber with a normal-appearing insect in them. Meanwhile, in any given year, 10,000 pieces of real amber with a normal-appearing insect in them are found. In that marketplace, finding a piece of supposed amber that has a normal-appearing insect in it would not be a sign that it's likely a fake. It could be a fake, but it's 10 times more likely that it's real.
- **Goal:** We need to rule out this objection. We need the marketplace to contain more examples of fake amber with normal-appearing insects than real amber with normal-appearing insects in it. Or we just need to be more convinced that seeing a normal-appearing insect is a sign that the amber is probably fake.

**Answer choices**
**A. Amber is often sold by small shops and street vendors that take few precautions to ensure authenticity.** — Weak Impact (9% of students)
The fact that shops that sell amber aren't good at ensuring authenticity definitely increases the plausibility that we might buy fake amber. But this answer has nothing to do with convincing us that seeing a "normal-appearing insect" is a sign that the amber is probably fake. This answer applies to any type of supposed-amber. So while it has some strengthening effect, it's mild.

**B. Pieces of amber that contain fossilized life forms are generally larger than plain pieces of amber.** — No Impact (3% of students)
The size of the pieces of supposed-amber is not useful or relevant to us. This is just saying that if there's an insect in the amber (whether normal-appearing or abnormal-appearing), the piece will be bigger than if there isn't an insect. But it doesn't help us argue that seeing a "normal-appearing insect" makes it more likely that it's fake.

**C. Amber that contains insects usually demands a higher price than does amber that contains small plants.** — No Impact (5% of students)
This reinforces something that was already implied. We know that forgers often add an insect to improve the value of their piece of fake amber, so the fact that amber w/ insects sells better than amber w/ plants matches that same gist. But we didn't need to be convinced that adding an insect improves the value. We need to be convinced that seeing a piece of supposed-amber with a normal-appearing insect in it is a sign that the amber is fake.

**D. It is very difficult to distinguish between genuine and fake amber without destroying some of it.** — No Impact (2% of students)
We're not concerned with what it takes to figure out whether the amber is real or fake, whether that process is destructive or not. We're only concerned with why seeing a normal-appearing insect suggests that the amber is fake.

**E. Insects struggling to free themselves from tree resin are usually fossilized in awkward or grotesque positions.** — Correct (81% of students)
This helps us argue that seeing a normal-appearing insect means that the amber is probably fake. After all, when insects get trapped in amber in nature, they aren't normal-appearing. They appear in awkward and grotesque positions. Most of the real amber that contains insects has awkward-appearing insects. Thus, seeing a normal-appearing insect suggests that the amber isn't real.

## S2 · Q8 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Widespread use of the Internet has led to an increase in certain crimes such as information theft and to new crimes like hacking. This seems due, at least in part, to the impersonal nature of the Internet. People seem to feel more free to harm others through the Internet than in person, suggesting that people feel less morally constrained when they use the Internet. For this reason, steps should be taken to educate people about the ethical use of the Internet.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (for this reason):** Steps should be taken to educate people about the ethical use of the Internet.
- **Evidence:** People seem to feel more free to harm others through the Internet (such as info theft and hacking) and feel less morally constrained.
- **Evaluate:** This argument involves a Problem and a Recommended solution. To strengthen this argument, we would want to hear that this recommended solution will actually help to address the problem. Would "educating people about ethical use of the Internet" have any affect on this problem? Would ethical education lead to feeling more morally constrained?
- **Goal:** Let's look for something like, "If people don't feel morally constrained enough, then they should receive ethical education" or anything else that would strengthen the recommendation the author is offering for this problem.

**Answer choices**
**A. Education about the ethical use of a tool increases one's sense of moral responsibility regarding its use.** — Correct (92% of students)
This suggests that the author's recommendation (education about ethical use of Internet) would increase people's sense of moral responsibility regarding use of the internet. Currently they feel less morally constrained when they use the Internet. They feel more free to harm others. An increased sense of moral responsibility would make someone feel more constrained / less free to harm others. Also, this answer and (C) are the only two answers that deal with the conclusion / with the Recommendation: education about ethical use.

**B. When new technologies emerge, society needs to formulate new ethical guidelines to cover the use of those technologies.** — Weak Conclusion Match (5% of students)
The argument called for educating people about ethical use of the Internet, implying that standards of ethical use already exist but people need to be educated about it. This answer is saying that society needs to formulate new ethical guidelines..

**C. The more educated that people are about the ethical usage of the Internet, the greater the amount of harm that they can do to others.** — Weakens (0% of students)
This makes it sound like the recommended solution would backfire and make the problem even worse. The more we educate them about ethical use, the more ham people can do.

**D. People feel morally constrained from doing an action only if that action causes harm to others.** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
This says nothing about "educating people about ethical use", so it does nothing to strengthen the author's recommendation that "ethical education" is how we should tackle this problem of people behaving badly on the Internet.

**E. People who harm others through impersonal means are no less culpable for their actions than are people who harm others in person.** — Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
This says nothing about "educating people about ethical use", so it does nothing to strengthen the author's recommendation that "ethical education" is how we should tackle this problem of people behaving badly on the Internet.

## S2 · Q10 — Evaluate

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
One year ago, a municipality banned dishwasher detergents containing phosphates. Anecdotal evidence indicates that many residents continued to use detergents containing phosphates; they just purchased them from out-of-town stores. However, it is clear that some residents did switch to phosphate-free detergents, since phosphate pollution from the municipal wastewater treatment plant decreased significantly in the past year.

### Prompt
The answer to which one of the following questions would most help in evaluating the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (it is clear that):** Some residents did switch to phosphate-free detergents.
- **Evidence (since):** Phosphate pollution from the municipal wastewater treatment plant decreased significantly in the past year.
- **Evaluate:** Since the author's conclusion is assuming a causal Explanation for a Curious Fact, we can use our Curious Fact / Explanation mindset. Curious Fact ... why did the phosphate pollution from the treatment plant decrease significantly over the past year? Author's Explanation ... some residents switched to phosphate-free detergents. We, of course, jump into action thinking - could there be some Alternate Explanation for the curious fact? - how plausible is the Author's Explanation?
- **Goal:** On Evaluate, we're looking for a question that could be answered in some way that would be an Objection. An Objection to this argument would either, suggest an Alternate Explanation for why municipal treatment plants have lower phosphate pollution this year, or undermine the Author's Explanation that some residents have switched to phosphate-free detergents.

**Answer choices**
**A. Why did many residents continue to use detergents containing phosphates?** — No Impact (2% of students)
We don't care about people who are still using phosphate detergents. We're trying to ascertain whether any residents stopped using phosphate detergents. So interviewing the people who still use detergents won't help us at all.

**B. What pollutants, if any, are present in phosphate-free dishwashing detergents?** — No Impact: phosphate-free (2% of students)
We're trying to ascertain what caused the treatment plant's phosphate pollution to go down. Phosphate-free detergents don't contribute to "phosphate pollution", so who cares what other sort of pollutants they may have? We are investigating whether anyone switched to phosphate-free detergents. Telling us the ingredients of those detergents doesn't help us figure out whether anyone switched to using them.

**C. Were any changes made in the past year to the way the municipality's wastewater treatment plant treats phosphates?** — Correct (74% of students)
This does what Weaken answers most typically do on Curious Fact / Explanation arguments -- it suggests an alternate explanation for the curious fact. Why did phosphate pollution from the treatment plant decrease this past year? The author is assuming it must be because some residents switched to phosphate-free detergent (so there was less phosphate going into the treatment plant). But she's failing to consider Alternate Explanations: what if the phosphate pollution decreased instead because they just installed better equipment or processes at the treatment plant?

**D. Does most of the phosphate pollution in the municipality's waterways come from treated wastewater from the municipal treatment plant?** — Irrelevant: where the water goes next (21% of students)
We're trying to figure out why less phosphate pollution came out of the plant last year. This answer is about where the water goes after it's come out of the plant, and whether it ends up being a significant source of something somewhere else. All that matters to this question is solving the causal mystery of why less phosphate pollution came out of the treatment plant. Is it because less phosphate entered the treatment plant? If so, why did less enter? Or is it because the treatment plant removed more phosphate while the wastewater was inside the plant? Once this water exits the plant and we measure the plant's output of phosphate as lower than before, we're done with caring about it.

**E. Did municipal officials try to stop people from bringing detergents containing phosphates into the municipality?** — Weak Impact (2% of students)
If officials did try to stop people from bringing detergents w/ phosphates into the municipality, then that would mildly strengthen the plausibility that some residents probably switched to phosphate-free detergents. But if we say, "No, officials didn't try to stop people from bringing illegal detergents into the city", it doesn't weaken. It's perfectly plausible that some residents might switch to phosphate-free detergents, given that one year ago the phosphate-variety was banned, with or without official trying to stop rulebreakers from crossing the border with their contraband detergent. The objection we get from (C) is that, "the treatment plant is emitting less phosphate because they improved their filtering processes this year, not because residents switched to phosphate-free." The objection we would get from (E) is that, "it's not plausible that some residents switched to phosphate-free detergents, because the city officials didn't try to stop people from bringing phosphate detergents into the city."

We can imagine these ideas chaining together to give us something like this sort of chain: if two nations don't interact much, then won't understand other's needs / problems, and so sympathy / justice will be difficult. If we wanted to pretend this was a true conditional, it would look something like this: little interaction → little understanding of needs / problems → no sympathy or justice. Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** There is harm in promoting a folk remedy that has no effect.
- **Evidence:** People who are convinced to use an ineffective remedy continue with it for years rather than pursuing conventional treatments that would almost certainly help them.
- **Evaluate:** We know that the rule we select needs to help us prove that "doing X is harmful" or some equivalent synonym. Why is promoting folk remedies harmful? It's not literally harmful, because the remedy has no effect, but it steers people away from treatments that would probably help. So we're defining "harm" as "directed you away from something that would help".

**Answer choices**
**A. One should not promote a remedy if one believes that using that remedy will cause harm.** — Bad Conclusion/Premise Match (6% of students)

Additionally, the trigger of this rule doesn't match the premise. We're never told that the people promoting folk remedies believe that using those remedies will cause harm.

**B. It is harmful to interfere with someone doing something that is likely to benefit that person.** — Correct (61% of students)
This is structurally appealing because it says, "If you're doing ____ , it's harmful". Is promoting folk remedies "interfering with someone doing something that is likely to benefit them"? Yeah, we could make that language work. **C. To convince people of something for which one knows there is no evidence is to be dishonest.** — Bad Conclusion Match (4% of students)
This rule is set up to prove "Thus, doing this thing is dishonest", but we're looking to support a conclusion that says "Thus, doing this thing is harmful".

This principle helps one to conclude that "Person X is responsible for the harm, even if the harm was done unintentionally". Can we apply this to promoting folk remedies? This principle is trying to support an argument that sounds like, "Ted hurt Reggie's feelings, although Ted didn't mean to. Nonetheless, Ted is still responsible for hurting Reggie's feelings."

We want to try to support / prove the idea that promoting a folk remedy causes harm. This rule doesn't even mention harm, so it is very unappealing at first glance. Applying this rule to promoters of folk remedies, we would get that the promoter is in part responsible for the fact that the person they convinced would probably avoid conventional treatments for years. ## S2 · Q19 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A recent study examined the daytime and nighttime activity patterns of two populations of tree-dwelling lemurs -- the first living in a rain forest, where tree canopy cover is consistent year-round, and the second living in a deciduous forest, where many trees lose their leaves during the winter months. Both groups of lemurs were found to be more nocturnal during winter months than they were the rest of the year. However, the winter increase in nocturnal activity was significantly more pronounced for the population living in the deciduous forest than it was for the population living in the rain forest.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the difference between the two lemur populations with respect to winter activity patterns?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Comparing the Two Populations:**
```
              population 1                    population 2
        both tree-dwelling lemurs
        both more nocturnal activity during winter

        live in rain forest             live in deciduous forest
        tree canopy                     many trees
        consistent                      lose leaves
        year round                      during winter

        small winter increase           big winter increase
        in nocturnal activity           in nocturnal activity
```
- **Evaluate / Goal:** We need to explain how the rainforest lemurs are different from deciduous lemurs, in regards to why the deciduous lemurs have a bigger uptick in nocturnal activity during the winter. Since we were told one difference between the two (year-round canopy vs. lose leaves), we anticipate that the correct answer will "Use that difference to explain this difference". How come the lemurs who live in trees that "lose leaves during winter" are doing "more nocturnal activity" than are the lemurs who live in trees that "still have a full canopy of leaves throughout winter"?

**Answer choices**
**A. For both lemur populations, the primary competitors for food resources are species active during daylight.** — No Impact / No Distinction (3% of students)
This helps explain why both lemur populations have an incentive to hunt for food nocturnally (fewer competitors), but this does nothing to explain why one group's nocturnal activity was significantly more than another's. It doesn't point to a factor that would be different between the two groups of lemurs.

**B. The primary predators for both lemur populations are high-flying birds that rely on their eyesight to hunt prey during daylight.** — Correct (70% of students)
This points to a similarity, when we're looking for a difference, but wait — when it comes to a tree-dwelling lemur population that lives in trees with a full canopy leaves vs. a population that lives in trees where the leaves have fallen off (in winter), will they have different experiences of a "high-flying birds, their primary predator, which relies on eyesight to hunt prey during the day"? Sure! Since the bird is looking below, at the tops of trees, the lemurs who live underneath a canopy of leaves are much better hidden than are lemurs who live in a tree with no leaves. If you're a lemur who lives in a tree with no leaves in it, and you're primarily hunted by birds that use their eyesight to find you during daylight, then you're not going to want to be making yourself seen during the day. You'll keep your activity real low; try to stay hidden and motionless, so that the predator birds don't see you. You'll have to do your hunting for food at night, when the darkness will prevent the high-flying birds from being able to see you as well. Meanwhile, if you're a lemur living in a rain forest with a constant canopy of leaves, then it doesn't really matter whether you're out in the day or at night. The high-flying birds can't see past the canopy of leaves to see you moving about.

**C. In both habitats, species of predatory snakes active during daylight are most active during winter months.** — No Impact / No Distinction (5% of students)
This answer might help explain why both populations have more nocturnal activity in the winter. Both of them are thinking, "Shoot — I wanna go out during the day, but I know in the winter there's an uptick of predatory snakes moving around during daylight. I'll just wait until it gets dark." This doesn't do anything to explain the difference though, because we don't have any common sense reason to think that rainforest lemurs would have a different experience of these snakes than deciduous lemurs would, so we don't have way to explain why deciduous lemurs are so much more active at night than rainforest lemurs are.

**D. The lemur population in the rain forest is twice the size of the population in the deciduous forest.** — No Impact - Population Size (4% of students)
The raw numbers of population size doesn't explain why the smaller population engages in way more nocturnal activity than the bigger population does. There's no common sense link between being a smaller population and thus having a bigger uptick in nocturnal activity during the winter.

**E. The lemur population in the rain forest eats both plants and insects whereas the population in the deciduous forest eats only plants.** — Unclear Impact (18% of students)
This does provide a difference between the two populations, but there isn't a common sense link between this difference and the one we know. Rainforest lemurs (year-round canopy of leaves, eat plants and insects, small uptick in nocturnal behavior during winter) vs. Deciduous lemurs (lose leaves during winter, eat only plants, big uptick in nocturnal behavior during winter). There's no obvious sense of why "if you eat only plants (rather than plants and insects), then you'll have a bigger uptick in nocturnal behavior during the winter".

## S2 · Q21 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Principle: If one does not criticize a form of behavior in oneself or vow to stop it, then one should not criticize that form of behavior in another.

Application: If Shimada does not vow to stop being tardy himself, he should not criticize McFeney for tardiness.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, justifies the above application of the principle?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Principle:**
```
not criticizing yourself                          shouldn't criticize
for behavior X                    and    →         someone else
not vowing to stop X                               for doing X
```
- **Application:**
```
Shimada doesn't vow to        →        shouldn't criticize
stop being tardy himself               M for tardiness
```
- **Evaluate:** Principle-Application questions are dreamy, because they are very self-similar. 1. We're given a rule 2. The application is trying to conclude the right side of the rule 3. Something about the left side of the rule still hasn't been clearly established, and that's our whole job -- figuring out what that is. This application is indeed concluding the right side of the rule, shouldn't criticize someone else for [tardiness]. Have we established that "Shimada doesn't criticize himself for being tardy" and that "Shimada does not vow to stop being tardy himself?" Not quite. We only established that Shimada hasn't vowed to stop being tardy himself. Why do we need both? Isn't the rule saying or? It does seem that way at first, but if it were really an or trigger, then saying that Shimada doesn't vow to stop being tardy himself would be sufficient to trigger the rule. If the application were already a correct usage of the rule, then there would be no room for them to ask us a Strengthen question. You can't strengthen something that's already valid. So part of how we'd figure out that the trigger is and, not or, is just by understanding that something has to be missing currently. When you say that something is not X or Y, you're saying ~ (X or Y). This is almost like distributing a -1 in algebra. ~ (X or Y) = ~X and ~Y. Think of a more conversational example: If you are not vaccinated or recovered from covid, then you should wear your mask. When we say "X or Y → Z", we could re-write that as X → Z and Y → Z. When we see, If you are not vaccinated or recovered from covid, then you should wear your mask, if we treated it the same way as the above, we'd get not vaccinated → should wear mask, recovered from covid → should wear mask. That second one doesn't make any sense. If you've had covid and recovered, then you should wear a mask? That's not what we meant. We were saying if you haven't already had and recovered from covid, then you should wear a mask. This trigger, If you are not vaccinated or recovered from covid, is really meaning "If you are not vaccinated or (not) recovered". But the "not" isn't written again there because it's modifying the "vaccinated or recovered". The trigger is saying, "If you're not belonging to the crew of people who are (X or Y), then you should wear your mask." To not belong to that crew means that you have not been vaccinated and have not already recovered from covid. The tl;dr version of that is, when we say "if you're not X or Y", we're saying, "If it is not the case that at least one of these two things is true about you". So we're saying, "If it is the case that neither of these things is true about you".
- **Goal:** We have half the trigger (hasn't vowed to stop being tardy), but we need the other half of the trigger (doesn't criticize himself for his tardiness).

**Answer choices**
**A. Both McFeney and Shimada are regularly tardy, but Shimada criticizes McFeney's tardiness without criticizing his own.** — Correct (87% of students)
This establishes that tardiness "is a form of behavior in Shimada" as well as "a form of behavior in McFeney", and it establishes that Shimada doesn't criticize his own tardiness. That's what we were missing. Now that we've established that Shimada is tardy, doesn't criticize himself for it , and doesn't vow to stop being tardy, we can apply this principle and justifiably conclude, "Shimada shouldn't criticize tardiness in others".

**B. McFeney is regularly tardy, but Shimada is almost never tardy.** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
We need to know that Shimada doesn't criticize himself for his tardiness, or else we haven't established the trigger condition of this principle.

**C. McFeney often criticizes Shimada for being tardy, but neither Shimada nor McFeney ever vows to cease being tardy.** — Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
We need to know that Shimada doesn't criticize himself for his tardiness, or else we haven't established the trigger condition of this principle. This only says that Shimada gets criticized by others for being tardy. We need to know whether Shimada ever criticizes himself for his tardiness.

**D. Shimada criticizes McFeney for regularly being tardy, but also criticizes himself for occasional tardiness.** — Opposite of Goal (9% of students)
We need to know that Shimada doesn't criticize himself for his tardiness, but this says that he does.

**E. Neither McFeney nor Shimada is regularly tardy, but Shimada criticizes McFeney for tardiness nonetheless.** — Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
We need to know that Shimada doesn't criticize himself for his tardiness, or else we haven't established the trigger condition of this principle.

## S2 · Q22 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Everyone should have access to more than one newspaper, for there are at least two sides to every story. Since all sides of an important story should be covered, and no newspaper adequately covers all sides of every one of its stories, some important stories would not be adequately covered if there were only one newspaper.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the reasoning of the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Everyone should have access to more than one newspaper.
- **Intermediate Conclusion:** Some important stories would not be adequately covered if there were only one newspaper.
- **Evidence:** There are at least two sides to every story. All sides of an important story should be covered, and no newspaper adequately covers all sides of every one of its stories.
- **Evaluate:** The opinion indicator "should" in the first sentence alerts us to the possibility that we have a First Sentence Conclusion. The "for" in the second half of that first sentence shows that we're supporting that initial opinion (F.A.B.S. are the four main support indicators: for, after all, because, since). The "since" at the beginning of the the second sentence paired with the "and" that ropes in the 2nd claim of that sentence, means that the first two ideas of the final sentence are supporting the third idea in the final sentence, which makes that an Intermediate Conclusion.
```
All sides of                                          some imp
important story          No paper can        →        stories would   →   everyone should
should be covered   AND  cover all sides of             not be                have access to
                         all of its stories             adequately             2+ papers
                                                         covered with
                                                         only one
                                                         newspaper
```
We can object to the move from the Premises to the Intermediate Conc, or to the move from the Intermediate to the Main Conc. Let's start with that simpler move, from Intermediate to Main: GIVEN THAT if you only had one paper, some important stories would not be adequately covered. HOW COULD WE SAY it's not the case that everyone should have access to more than one newspaper? We could just say "what's the big deal if some people don't get adequate coverage of important stories? You, author, need to establish the premise that everyone should have access to adequate coverage of important stories." How about the move from Premises to Intermediate Conclusion. GIVEN THAT all sides of an important story should be covered, and that no newspaper can adequately cover all sides of all stories, HOW COULD WE SAY it's possible that if there were only one newspaper, all important stories would be adequately covered? Well, this one is tricky. We might not see the wiggle room until we read the answer choices and it gives us the hint, but the 2nd premise there isn't what it needs to be. It needs to be saying: no newspaper can adequately cover all sides of all important stories. Instead, it says that no paper can adequately cover all sides of all stories. Okay, that's fine. As long as the paper adequately covers all sides of the important stories, we don't care if they only cover one side of some of their unimportant stories. So we've found a way to accept the premises but still argue that one newspaper could adequately cover all important stories.
- **Goal:** Stay flexible. The answer might speak to this last objection (what if a newspaper were able to cover all sides of all the important stories?) or it might speak to the move to the Main Conclusion (who said that everyone needs to have access to adequate coverage of all important stories?)

**Answer choices**
**A. The argument confuses the inability to cover all sides of every story with the inability to cover all sides of any important story.** — Correct (48% of students)
This is addressing that last objection we discussed. If a newspaper is able to cover all sides of its important stories, then the author's Intermediate Conclusion in the last sentence is wrong — if there were only one newspaper, it's possible that the newspaper could adequately cover all the important stories. The author only established that one newspaper couldn't cover all sides of all stories, but she didn't establish that one newspaper would be unable to cover all sides of all the important stories (important stories might be a tiny subset of all stories, so it might be realistic to run multiple articles on the same big, important story in order to cover all sides).

**B. The argument overlooks the possibility that two newspapers could provide the same incomplete coverage of the same important stories.** — Not an Objection (48% of students)
Would it hurt the argument if two papers were essentially duplicating the same blind spots? (i.e. they're both covering sides 1 and 2 of an important story and failing to cover sides 3 and 4) No, it doesn't hurt the author's argument, because the author was never promising (in sufficient terms) that "IF you do have access to more than one newspaper, then you will have all sides". She was just arguing that "if you don't have access to more than one newspaper, then you won't have all sides".

**C. A conclusion about what newspapers should do is inferred solely from statements about what newspapers in fact do.** — Bad Conclusion Match (1% of students)
Whenever we see an answer structured "a conclusion about X is inferred solely from statements about Y", we should check to see whether X matches the conclusion and whether Y matches the evidence. Do we have a conclusion about what newspapers should do? Nope. There's a conclusion about what people should have access to. That's not the same as "what newspapers should do". Let's stop reading and eliminate.

**D. The argument takes for granted that everyone has access to all newspapers.** — Too Strong: everyone / all (1% of students)
Has our author committed herself to the crazy extreme notion that every person in the world has access to every newspaper in the world? Of course not. Flaw answers love to pair up assumption wording (takes for granted / presumes / fails to establish) with overly strong ideas. Our author would like everyone to have access to at least two papers. That's as far as she went in terms of talking about people's access to papers.

**E. The argument is concerned only with important stories and not with all stories.** — Not a Flaw (2% of students)
This is descriptively true, seemingly. The argument is definitely focused more on important stories. But there's nothing inherently wrong or flawed with focusing on one subset of a group.

## S2 · Q23 — Must be True

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Most of the mines that Moradco operates in the province of Velyena have never violated environmental regulations. Every one of the gold mines that Moradco operates throughout the world has at some time or another violated environmental regulations.

### Prompt
Which one of the following statements follows logically from the statements above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:**
```
quantifier (most)
Most Moradco mines           never violated
in Velyena                   enviro regulations

conditional / quantifier (every)
Moradco               →       violated some
gold mine                     enviro regulation
```
- **Evaluate:** We're given a Most statement and a conditional All statement. In general, Most statements are only interesting as a starting point if you have two most claims about the same group, because then you can prove some overlap. given Most A's are B, these, Most A's are C, we can infer: B and C overlap at least once. But since there aren't two Most claims, the conditional should automatically command our attention. What does a Must Be True question do with a conditional? It either chains it together with other conditionals (there aren't any other conditionals in this two claim paragraph), or we apply this conditional to a fact that was provided, usually via the contrapositive. Does the conditional in the 2nd sentence apply to what's talked about in the 1st sentence? Is there any Overlapping Concept? Yes, both sentences are talking about whether or not something has violated environmental regulations. We know that most of M's mines in Velyena have never violated. And we know that all of M's gold mines have violated. So none of those mines we're talking about in the first sentence are gold mines.
- **Goal:** Since the mines being talked about in the first sentence clearly aren't gold mines, since they have never violated an environmental regulation, we can infer that "most of the mines that Moradco operates in the province of Velyena [are not gold mines]". Formally,
```
Most Moradco's           never
mines in Velyena         violated

+

never                →   not one of
violated                  Moradco's gold mines
-------------------------------------------------
Most Moradco's            not one of
mines in Velyena          Moradco's gold mines
```
Like most Must Be True questions that test our ability to apply a conditional rule, you have to contrapose the rule to see how the available fact(s) triggers it.

**Answer choices**
**A. Moradco operates more mines in Velyena than any other company operates there.** — Too Strong: #1 mine operator (0% of students)
We have no basis for thinking Moradco operates more mines than any other company, let alone derive that they are they #1 most numerous mine operator. They could just as easily be the mining company with the fewest mines in Velyena.

**B. The total number of gold mines that Moradco operates is larger than the total number of mines it operates in Velyena.** — Unknown Comparison / Relative vs. Absolute (3% of students)
There was no information at all about actual numbers of gold mines. Most and Every aren't numbers, they're percentages (over 50% and 100%, respectively). Just because "every" means 100% and "most" only means "over 50%" doesn't mean that there are more gold mines than Velyenan mines. There might be 100 mines in Velyena (at least 51 of which have never violated regulations). And there might be 6 gold mines that Moradco operates throughout the world (all 6 of which have violated regulations).

**C. Most of the gold mines that Moradco operates are not located in Velyena.** — Unknown Most Group (10% of students)
Most is a really dangerous word on Must Be True and Must Be Assumed (i.e. Necessary Assumption). We can never derive a statement about "Most X's" if the stimulus didn't provide a statement about "Most X's". There was no statement in the stimulus that said "Most gold mines from Moradco". We only had "Most Moradco mines in Velyena". This answer can be disproven with some numbers. There might be 100 mines in Velyena (at least 51 of which have never violated regulations, up to 49 of which have violated regulations). And there might be 11 gold mines that Moradco operates throughout the world (all 11 of which have violated regulations). Do we know that most of those gold mines are outside Velyena? Do at least 6 of them have to be outside Velyena? No, all 11 of them could be in Velyena and it wouldn't go against anything we were told. If there are 100 Moradco mines in Velyena, up to 49 of them could be gold mines.

**D. Most of the mines that Moradco operates in Velyena are not gold mines.** — Correct (86% of students)
This is what we predicted. The idea behind Inference questions is testing our ability to synthesize. Given these two claims: A is B, B is C, we derive A is C. We filter out the thing the two claims have in common and join the other two things. Given these claims: Most of Moradco's Velyena mines have violated. If have violated, then not Moradco's gold mines. We filter out the Overlapping Concept to derive: Most of Moradco's Velyena mines not Moradco's gold mines.

**E. Most of the mines that Moradco operates throughout the world are not gold mines.** — Unknown Most Group (1% of students)
We can never derive a statement about "Most X's" if the stimulus didn't provide a statement about "Most X's". There was no statement in the stimulus that said "Most of M's mines in the world". We only had "Most Moradco mines in Velyena". This answer can be disproven with some numbers. There might be 10 mines in Velyena (at least 6 of which have never violated regulations and thus are not gold). And there might be 100 gold mines throughout the world, 100 of which have violated regulations. In that legal scenario, Moradco has 110 total mines throughout the world, and most of them (at least 100 of them) are gold mines.

## S2 · Q24 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Tariffs on particular products tend to protect the small percentage of the population that works in industries that make those products while hurting everyone else through higher costs. Polls show that in fact most people oppose such tariffs. So politicians would be more likely to be reelected if they voted against these tariffs.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption on which the argument relies?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Politicians would be more likely to be reelected if they voted against these tariffs on particular products.
- **Evidence:** Most people oppose tariffs on particular products. These tariffs tend to protect small % of people who work in that industry while hurting everyone else through higher costs.
- **Evaluate:** This argument is mainly making a move from "Most people oppose position X" to "politicians would be more likely to be reelected if they opposed position X". Intuitively it sounds like opposing an unpopular position would help your reelection chances, but maybe one's reelection chances hinge more on something else. Most people support universal background checks on gun purchases and raising the minimum wage, but that doesn't mean a Republican would be more likely to be reelected by talking about gun control and minimum wage boosts. Most people support tight restrictions on late term abortions, but that doesn't mean a Democrat would be more likely to be reelected by running on tight restrictions for late term abortions. A Republican wouldn't run on gun control, because the vast majority of people who like gun control are Democrats and aren't going to vote for that Republican just because she agrees with them on this issue. A Democrat wouldn't run on restricting abortions because the vast majority of people who oppose abortions are Republicans and aren't going to vote for that Democrat. A stronger argument would say, "If most of a politician's previous voters oppose such tariffs, then they would be more likely to be reelected if they voted against these tariffs." Since reelection basically hinges on getting more of "your voters" to turnout, it's less important whether "most voters oppose X" and more important whether "most of the people who previously voted for you / would consider voting for you oppose X".
- **Goal:** Look for an answer offering the Missing Link of, "If you vote against something that most people oppose, it improves your chances of reelection", or look for an answer that, when negated, allows us to say that "even though most people oppose tariffs, voting for tariffs could still be the right move for a politician".

**Answer choices**
**A. Supporters of tariffs on particular products are not significantly more likely than opponents to base their vote for a politician on the politician's stand on this issue.** — Correct (66% of students)
Whenever we see a Necessary Assumption answer is ruling out an idea with the word "not", it's worth negating the answer by removing the 'not' to see if it turns into an objection. If it were the case that "supporters of tariffs are significantly more likely than opponents of tariffs to base their vote on a politician's stance on tariffs", does that weaken? Definitely. That sounds like a way to argue that "even though most voters oppose tariffs, a politician could still be wise to vote for tariffs." For a lot of people, voting is complex calculation of many different factors, and their opposition to tariffs might be really far down the list. (Sort of like choosing a law school is a complex calculation of many different factors. It could be true that "most law school applicants don't want to live in a state that charges high taxes", but see if that keeps them from accepting admission to Stanford Law) Some voters are single-issue voters: they care so much about one issue, that they vote based on that. (Pro-life / anti-abortion is one such issue that is famous for being the reason a voter chooses his/her preferred candidate) So this answer, stated positively, is saying "our author is assuming that tariffs aren't an issue where it's way more likely to HELP you if you support it than HURT you if you oppose it."

**B. Politicians always vote according to what is most likely to get them reelected.** — Too Strong: always (10% of students)
The author has not committed herself to this extreme maxim. If we negate this, it will be saying "there is at least one instance when a politician did not vote according to the most likely reelection path". That one instance wouldn't hurt this argument in the slightest. Furthermore, the argument isn't about what did / does happen. It's a hypothetical about what would happen, if someone did something. When I say, "If Marty complimented his mom more, he'd see her smile more often", I'm not assuming that "Marty always does whatever maximizes his mom's smiling".

**C. Politicians should support only general tariffs, since such tariffs would be more widely popular with voters than tariffs on particular products.** — Too Strong: only / Out of Scope: general tariffs / Opposite Logic (2% of students)
We don't know anything about general tariffs. We're never allowed to assume that the "opposite trait" is true of the "opposite category". You can't go from hearing "Black lives matter" to thinking we said "non-black lives don't matter". We only heard that you should not support particular tariffs because they are unpopular. That doesn't mean we should support general tariffs because they are popular.

**D. Politicians should never support measures that favor only a small percentage of the population.** — Too Strong: never / Out of Scope: should (8% of students)
The author isn't generalizing about any case in which a measure favors a small percentage. The author is focused less on how big a percentage is helped and more on the idea that "most people oppose a measure". Most people may support food assistance for the poor, even if that's only a small percentage of the population that benefits. Furthermore, nothing in this argument is about what should be done. The author's argument is purely descriptive. "If they did X, Y would more likely occur." That's not saying they should do X.

**E. People who would be hurt by tariffs generally know that they would be hurt by them.** — Out of Scope: know tariffs will hurt / Too Specific: generally (14% of students)
"Generally" means more than 50% of the time. Does it make any different to this author whether 51% vs. 49% of people who would be hurt by tariffs know they would be hurt by tariffs? Of course not. The author doesn't rely on people's accurate perception of how tariffs will affect them. It's just relying on polls that show that most people oppose such tariffs (you might oppose tariffs, even if tariffs benefit you or are neutral to you).

## S4 · Q4 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Film director: It's inaccurate to say that filmgoers stayed away from my film because it received one or two negative reviews. My film had such a small audience during its opening weekend simply because it was competing with several other films that appeal to the same type of filmgoer that mine does, and the number of such viewers is relatively small.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps to support the film director's explanation?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Curious Fact:** Why did this film have such a small audience during its opening weekend?
- **Director's Explanation:** It wasn't because it had a couple negative reviews. It's because it was competing with several other films that share the same audience, and it's a pretty small audience.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we're evaluating an author's causal hypothesis for a curious fact, we have two different pressure points to consider: 1. Alternate Explanations for that curious fact 2. the plausibility of the author's explanation
- **Goal:** Since this is Strengthen, we want an answer to either rule out an Alternate Explanation for why this film had such a small audience during its opening weekend (e.g. "There wasn't a terrible storm that kept people from going to the movies that weekend) or increase the plausibility of the author's explanation (e.g. corroborate the story that the intended audience for this film was split between seeing this film and seeing several other films meant to appeal to the same niche).

**Answer choices**
**A. The film director's film received no positive reviews.** — Weakens (0% of students)
This adds plausibility to an Alternate Explanation (the film had a crappy opening weekend because of the bad reviews it received), so this would weaken.

**B. Filmgoers seldom see more than one film in a weekend.** — Correct (92% of students)
This adds some plausibility (although not much!) to the author's causal storyline. The director thinks that her audience (let's pretend it's young people from 14-22 years old) was probably torn between seeing her film and the several other ones that were targeted at that age group. We might object to this storyline by saying, "Why should that mean a bad weekend for your film? Couldn't they just see those other movies and your movie, all in the same weekend?" And this answer rules out that objection. It basically makes it seem like if there are four movies out that target the same audience, then, all other things being equal, each of those four movies will only get 25% of that audience to its film in a given weekend.

**C. The total number of filmgoers was larger than average on the weekend the film director's film opened.** — Weak Impact (2% of students)
This seems to somewhat rule out alternate explanations for why her film had a small audience that would fall in the category of, "There was a storm / there was a pandemic / it was the weekend of the Superbowl". In other words, this answer would be saying "the film didn't have a bad opening weekend because that weekend just happened to be bad for all movies". So it does strengthen somewhat, by ruling out a broad category of alternate explanations. But LSAC usually thinks that increasing the plausibility of the author's explanation is more powerful than ruling out some alternate explanation. (It's hard for us to register that (B) has much strengthening power, though, because it's such a common sense notion that you rarely see more than one movie in a weekend.)

**D. Each of the other films that the film director alludes to received one or two positive reviews.** — Weak Impact (3% of students)
This does a little to increase the plausibility of the author's explanation as well, but if we negated (B) vs. negated (D), the negated version of (B) would weaken more than the negated version of (D). If these competing films didn't receive one or two positive reviews, they would still be on equal footing with what we know of the director's film (all we know is that the director's film got one or two negative reviews). The director's hypothesis that her film had a weak opening weekend because of these competing films doesn't depend on the idea that these competing films were better reviewed by critics. It's really just a hypothesis that a small audience got divided up into smaller chunks, because some saw her movie, some saw competing movie A, or B, or C. Meanwhile, if you negate (B) and think that filmgoers often see more than one movie in a given weekend, then it's much harder to argue that "because some of this film's audience saw competing movies A, B, or C, that means they wouldn't see this film during its opening weekend."

**E. Most filmgoers are drawn to a variety of kinds of film.** — No Impact (2% of students)
We're not concerned with what's true of most filmgoers, because this film has a relatively small niche audience.

## S4 · Q6 — Flaw

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Letter to the editor: Your newspaper's advertisement claims that you provide coverage of the high school's most popular sports. Clearly this is false advertising. Of the school's students, 15 percent compete on the track team, while only 5 percent of the students play basketball. Hence, track is far more popular than basketball, yet track gets no coverage and basketball gets full-page coverage.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the letter to the editor is most vulnerable to the criticism that it

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (clearly):** Your ad's claim that you provide coverage of the high school's most popular sports is false advertising. (i.e. Your paper does not provide coverage of the high school's most popular sports)
- **Evidence:** Track gets no coverage, while basketball gets full-page coverage.
- **Intermediate Conclusion (hence):** Track is more popular than basketball.
- **Evidence:** 15% of the students are on the track team, while 5% play basketball.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we're doing Flaw questions, we read the paragraph with a special ear for the 10 famous flaws (since the flaw ends up being one of those about 1/3 of the time). Sure enough, we have a famous flaw here. This is the rare bird known as Equivocation, the flaw of using the same term (or concept) in two very different ways. Previous examples have featured talking about maturity, as in how responsible you are, vs. maturity, as in whether you've gone through physical puberty. Or there was one about happiness, as in a prolonged satisfaction with your life, vs. happiness, as in a fleeting moment of pleasure. In this case, the argument is trading on an ambiguity in the term "popular". The newspaper was saying, "We provide coverage of the high school's most popular sports", meaning, "we provide coverage of the sports that are most popular with our readers". The author is interpreting "popular" to mean the sports that are most participated in by students. A lot of questions earlier in an LR section play off our real world common sense, and this is definitely trying to tap into people's awareness that the only high school sports random people in the community are likely to care about are the big spectator sports like football, basketball, baseball.
- **Goal:** We want to look for an answer calling out the Equivocation flaw (saying that the argument used the term "popular" in two different ways) or an answer that is otherwise spelling out the idea that the newspaper was probably referring to most popular in terms of what its readers want to see, not in terms of what percent of the student body participates in it.

**Answer choices**
**A. infers a cause from a mere correlation** — Wrong Flaw (3% of students)
This refers to the famous Causal flaw, specifically a subtype in which the author presents a correlation between X and Y (e.g. people who are X are more likely than those who are not X to be Y), and then overconfidently assumes that this means that X causes Y. There was no correlation in the evidence and the conclusion is definitely not causal. (It's just a rebuttal -- a rejection of an idea)

**B. bases its conclusion on a sample that is too small** — Wrong Flaw (9% of students)
This refers to the famous Sampling flaw, in which an author relies on a sample that seems untrustworthy, because it's either too small, biased, self-selecting, or likely to have skewed data for some other reason. This flaw is saying, "Because X was true in these cases, X will be true in this wider set of similar cases". That doesn't resemble this argument.

**C. misinterprets a key word in the newspaper's advertisement** — Correct (85% of students)
The key word is "popular", which the advertisement meant in terms of "which sports are most popular with our readers", and which the author interpreted as "which sports are most popular in terms of student participation".

**D. employs as a premise the contention it purports to show** — Wrong Flaw (2% of students)
This refers to the famous Circular reasoning flaw, although this is the most creatively overwrought wording I've ever seen for it. Circular reasoning sounds like: the premise restates the conclusion; the premise assumes the truth of the conclusion. More variations: establishes what it seeks to prove; presupposes what it sets out to establish; employs as a premise the contention it purports to show. The conclusion is that "the newspaper does not cover the most popular sports (guilty of false advertising". Was there also a premise that said "the newspaper does not cover the most popular sports"? Nope.

**E. criticizes the source of a claim rather than the claim itself** — Wrong Flaw (1% of students)
This is the famous Ad Hominem flaw, in which an author dismisses an idea or an argument, because of the source of the claim/argument is someone with a biased/vested interest in the matter or someone with conflicting past behavior. This argument doesn't resemble that at all. The conclusion is literally criticizing a claim "your advertisement's claim is false advertising".

### Stimulus
Several movie critics have claimed that this movie will inspire people to act in socially irresponsible ways, yet this claim relies entirely on survey data that have turned out to be deeply flawed. ### Prompt

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The claim that this movie will inspire people to act in socially irresponsible ways is untrue and potentially harmful to the moviemaker's reputations.
- **Evidence:** The claim relies entirely on survey data that has turned out to be deeply flawed.
- **Evaluate:** This one would be tough to get if we don't know our famous flaws. If an author is saying, "they failed to prove X. there's no proof for X. the argument for X rests on a dubious assumption. the experiment that claimed to prove X turned out to be flawed", or in this case "the claim of X relied on what turned out to be bad survey data", then what is the valid conclusion and what is the flawed conclusion? The valid conclusion to reach is: okay, so we still don't know whether X is true. The flawed conclusion that LSAT authors go to is this: okay, so now we know that X is false. We call this flaw Unproven vs. Proven False (other companies sometimes call it stuff like Absence of Evidence). The movie critics have not successfully proven claim X (that the movie will inspire dangerous behavior). But that doesn't mean that claim X is untrue.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer saying "just because the data they relied on is bad doesn't mean we get to assume that their claim was false".

**Answer choices**
**A. infers that a claim is false merely on the grounds that no satisfactory evidence for it has been offered** — Correct (82% of students)
Even if we weren't aware of the Unproven vs. Proven False famous flaw, we can test whether this answer is valid by trying to match up its parts. The thing being inferred is the conclusion (or in rare cases, an assumption). The grounds on which we infer something is the evidence. Is the author's conclusion saying that a claim is false? Yes, it says "these critics have made a claim that is untrue". Is the premise saying that no good evidence has been offered? Yes, it says "this claim relies entirely on data that turned out to be badly flawed". If we can match the abstract description to the argument, then it's a correct answer.

**B. fails to consider that a pejorative claim that is true can be more harmful to a person's reputation than a false claim** — Not an Objection / Relative vs. Absolute: more harmful (4% of students)
First, some vocab: "pejorative" means insulting or derogatory. When I say "my friend Paul is gay", that's not a pejorative sense of 'gay'. When people (twenty years ago) would say "oh, I don't like chess. I just think it's kinda gay." That's using 'gay' as a pejorative, which we thankfully don't or shouldn't do anymore. Was their a pejorative claim in this argument? Not really. Would it be an objection to say that an insulting claim can be more harmful to reputation than a false claim? Not at all. "Reputational harm" is tacked onto the conclusion for no reason other than creating trap answers. The author's evidence doesn't try to support that this claim is potentially harmful to reputations. Even if it were a meaningful part of the conclusion, the author is only claiming that the claim is potentially harmful, not more harmful than other things. If I conclude that "eating cheeseburgers every day is bad for your health", you can't object to my conclusion by saying, "you fail to consider that smoking two packs of cigarettes every day is even worse for your health".

**C. relies on a sample that is likely to be unrepresentative** — Wrong Flaw (2% of students)
This refers to the famous flaw Sampling, and since it says "flawed survey data" within the paragraph, students might pick this just thinking maybe we were doing a sampling flaw. But the author is pointing out that her opponents relied on a bad sample; she's not relying on a sample.

**D. attacks the persons making an argument rather than attacking the substance of the argument** — Wrong Flaw (2% of students)
This refers to the famous Ad Hominem flaw, in which you invalidate someone's argument by saying that they had an ulterior motive biasing them or that they have conflicting past behavior that makes it hard to believe their present claims. In doing so, Ad Hominem authors are failing to actually confront their opponents' arguments -- they don't engage with the evidence their opponents are putting forward. That's definitely not a match for what's going on here, because our author is engaging with her opponent's evidence. She's saying, "since their evidence was flawed, their conclusion is false".

**E. fails to consider that, even if an argument's conclusion is false, some of the evidence used to justify that conclusion may nonetheless be true** — Reversed Parts (10% of students)
The correct version of this would say fails to consider that, even if the evidence used to justify an argument's conclusion is flawed, the argument's conclusion may nonetheless be true.

## S4 · Q13 — Must be True

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Most people who are skilled banjo players are also skilled guitar players. But most people who are skilled guitar players are not skilled banjo players.

### Prompt
If the statements above are true, which one of the following must also be true?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:** Most skilled banjo are skilled guitar, but most skilled guitar are not skilled banjo.
- **Evaluate:** We know that correct answers on Inference questions want us to synthesize claims, and we only get two claims here. They have a math-y, quantified feel, so we want to think mathematically. The famous Most + Most inference that has been tested throughout the years is this: Most A's are B, Most A's are C, infer: Some B's are C / Some C's are B. But that's not what this is, since the two Most facts are about different groups. This is a new type of Most + Most inference that has now appeared 2 or 3 times on the last twenty tests. Most A's are B, Most B's are ~A, infer: There are more B's than A's. Why is that a valid inference? A little math helps. Let's say there are 9 skilled banjo players in the world. If we say that most of them are skilled at guitar, then at a minimum we have 5 people who are skilled at banjo and guitar. So now we get to the fun part: Could we also have 9 skilled guitar players in the world? If so, 5 of them are skilled at banjo and 4 aren't. But that would contradict our statement that "most skilled guitar players aren't good at banjo." What we have to remember is that the people in this world that are both skilled banjo and skilled guitar are appearing in both of these groups. When we talk about all the skilled banjo players, we'll be counting these 5 people who are good at banjo and guitar. And when we talk about all the skilled guitar players, we'll be counting these 5 people good at both in that tally as well. In order for most skilled guitar players to be bad at banjo, we need at least 11 skilled guitar players: 5 are also skilled at banjo, but the majority, the other 6, are not skilled at banjo. Here it is more graphically:
```
Just Banjo        Banjo and Guitar        Just Guitar
    4                    5                     6
```
9 people are good at banjo. 11 people are good at guitar. 5 people are good at both. When we say that Most A's are B but Most B's are ~A, we're saying that people who are both A and B are a majority of Group A but a minority of Group B. In our example before, there were 5 people good at both banjo and guitar. The statements are saying that these 5 people are a majority of the skilled banjo players, so the max total for banjo would be 9. The statements are saying that these 5 people are a minority of the skilled guitar players, so the min total for guitar would be 11. if you want to see the other example of this, check out Test 80, Section 1, Q22.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer saying there are more skilled guitarists than skilled banjo players.

**Answer choices**
**A. There are more people who are skilled at playing both the guitar and the banjo than there are people who are skilled at playing only one of the two instruments.** — Contradicted (6% of students)
The people who do guitar and banjo are a minority (under 50%) of the total guitar playing group. So the number of people skilled only at guitar has to be bigger than the number of people skilled at both.

**B. A person trying to learn how to play the guitar is more likely to succeed in doing so than is a person trying to learn how to play the banjo.** — Out of Scope: more likely to succeed (4% of students)
This is a Must Be True question and there's no way we can invent ideas about which instrument one is more likely to succeed on.

**C. Playing the guitar takes more skill than playing the banjo does.** — Unsupported Comparison: more skill (1% of students)
Just like (B), the idea of takes more skill is completely out of scope, and we could never derive that idea from facts that are purely mathematical in nature.

**D. There are more people who are skilled at playing the guitar than there are people who are skilled at playing the banjo.** — Correct (83% of students)
Let's say that there are x people who are good at banjo and guitar. We are told that x > 50% of skilled banjoists. We are told that x < 50% of skilled guitarists. That means that skilled guitarists has to be a bigger number. If I say, "Whoa, I can't pay $3000 a month for that apartment. That's more than half my monthly income" and my friend Regina says, "That's less than half of my monthly income", we can infer two things: Regina's a jerk; Regina makes more money per month than I do

**E. There are more people who are skilled at playing the banjo than there are people who are skilled at playing the guitar.** — Contradicted (7% of students)
The correct answer is the opposite of this idea, so check out the breakdown of the stimulus and the explanation for (C) again to remind yourself why there have to be more guitarists than banjo players.

The proposition that certain entrepreneurs fail in managing company growth plays which one of the following roles in the argument above?

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (Hence):** The lack of managerial skills and the lack of entrepreneurial ability can each inhibit the development of successful companies.

For instance = support indicator. consequently = This can indicate a conclusion or the result of a cause/effect relationship. **Answer choices**
**A. It is the main conclusion drawn in the argument.** — Wrong Role (2% of students)
The main conclusion is the final claim of the paragraph. A claim could never be the main conclusion if it's followed by the word "Hence", because the "hence" indicates that a conclusion is being drawn on top of that.

**B. It is presented as an example of the phenomenon the argument seeks to explain.** — Not an Example of Main Conclusion (7% of students)
Is this argument seeking to explain a phenomenon? Not really. That language means "to offer a causal explanation for something that happened / is happening". Our claim is giving an example of how someone's managerial skills could be lacking. But the argument doesn't seek to explain "how someone's managerial skills could be lacking".

**C. It is meant as an aside and is not supposed to provide evidence in support of the argument's conclusion.** — Wrong Role (1% of students)
This portrays our claim as neutral Background info, but we know this claim plays a supporting role. After all, it's followed by the word Hence. Conclusion indicators such as So, Thus, Hence, Therefore always indicate that the claim immediately before them is providing some support.

**D. It is a premise that is intended to support the argument's main conclusion directly.** — Not Directly (23% of students)
The claim we're being asked about exists in a sentence that is prefaced by the phrase For instance. "For instance / for example" always indicate that sentence we're now reading is supporting the sentence we just read. We know the 3rd sentence is directly supporting the 2nd sentence, and the 2nd sentence is directly supporting the 4th sentence (the main conclusion).

**E. It is an example that is offered in support of a premise that is intended to support the argument's main conclusion directly.** — Correct (68% of students)
The claim we're being asked about exists in a sentence that is prefaced by the phrase For instance. We know the 3rd sentence is offering an example to support the 2nd sentence. 2nd: many entrepreneurs fail for lack of managerial skills. 3rd: for example, they can fail because they lack the managerial skills to adequately manage company growth. Is the 2nd sentence a premise intended to support the argument's main conclusion directly? Yes. The final sentence (the main conclusion) is directly supported by the first two sentences. Sentence 1 establishes that a lack of entrepreneurial skills can tank a business. Sentence 2 establishes that lack of managerial skills can also do so. **Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If we find out whether Selena's claim is true, we be able to know whether or not it's possible to have psychic powers.
- **Evidence:** Selena claims to have psychic powers.
- **Evaluate:** "Whether or not" is a binary, so the conclusion is saying "Whether we find out that Selena's claim is true or false, it will tell us whether it's possible to have psychic powers." We should think through that binary: If her claim is true, then she does have psychic powers. So that tell us whether it's possible to have psychic powers? Sure. If she has psychic powers, then it's definitely possible to have psychic powers. If her claim is false, then she doesn't have psychic powers. Does that tell us whether it's possible to have psychic powers? Not really. If she doesn't have psychic powers, how does that tell us whether it's possible?
- **Goal:** We need to patch up what happens if we live in the world where her claim is false and she doesn't have psychic powers. We need an answer to convince us that "If Selena doesn't have psychic powers, then it's impossible to have psychic powers."

**Answer choices**
**A. No one else has yet been found to have psychic powers.** — Unrelated to Goal (15% of students)
We need to know what happens if Selena doesn't have psychic powers.

**B. If it is possible to have psychic powers, then Selena has them.** — Correct (41% of students)
The contrapositive says If Selena doesn't have psychic powers, then it's impossible to have psychic powers. And if she is correct about having psychic powers, then it's clearly possible to have psychic powers. So this proves the conclusion that if can find out whether she does / doesn't have psychic powers, then we'll know definitively whether or not it's possible to have psychic powers. Either way you go in that binary (she does / she doesn't have powers), you still get to a point where you know for sure whether or not psychic powers are possible.

**C. It is possible to determine whether Selena has psychic powers.** — Unrelated to Goal (19% of students)
We need to know what happens if Selena doesn't have psychic powers. This answer tempts a lot of people because it contains language from the argument, but whenever the author's conclusion is a conditional statement, we don't care whether the "if" condition is / isn't true. I never said it was. Since you can't weaken my argument by denying the "if" condition, we're not strengthening my argument to affirm the "if" condition. Even if this answer were true, we wouldn't have proven the conclusion, because if it turns out that we determine that Selena doesn't have psychic powers, we have no idea what that means in terms of whether it's possible for anyone to have psychic powers.

**D. If Selena's claim turns out to be false, we will not know whether it is possible to have psychic powers.** — Opposite Right Side (8% of students)
If this said, If her claim is false, we will know whether it's possible, it would be a correct answer.

**E. We will not be able to determine whether it is possible to have psychic powers unless we find out whether Selena's claim is true.** — Negation of Conclusion (17% of students)
The conclusion was "if we find out, we will be able to determine" and this answer says "if we don't find out, we will not be able to determine"

## S4 · Q22 — Role

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Theorist: Hatred and anger, grief and despair, love and joy are pairs of emotions that consist of the same core feeling and are distinguishable from each other only in terms of the social conditions that cause them and the behavior they in turn cause. So even if the meaning of a given piece of music is the emotion it elicits, this can mean only that music produces the core of a given emotion, for music is merely sound and, therefore, by itself creates neither social conditions nor human behavior.

### Prompt
The claim that music is merely sound plays which one of the following roles in the theorist's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (so):** Even if the meaning of a given piece of music is the emotion it elicits, this can mean only that music produces the core of a given emotion.
- **Evidence (for):** There are pairs of emotions that have the same core feeling, but can be distinguished from each other only in terms of the social conditions that cause them or the behavior they cause.
- **Intermediate Conclusion (therefore):** Music by itself cannot create social conditions or cause human behavior.
- **Evidence:** Music is merely sound.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we do Role and Main Conclusion questions, we expect that 90% of the time the Main Conclusion will appear earlier than at least some of its support. The word "So" attached to the first claim of the 2nd sentence indicates that the 1st sentence is supporting the "So" claim. Thus, the main conclusion can't possibly be the first sentence. The word "for" (FABS: for, after all, because since) indicates support, so we know that the final two claims are there to support the "So" claim that begins the 2nd sentence. Whenever we see a structure like this: X is true. After all, Y is true and thus Z is true. We know that X was the main conclusion, and that the 2nd sentence serves to support X. The "thus" within the 2nd sentence tells us that Y supports Z. Similarly, the "therefore" in the final sentence tells us that "music is merely sound" supports "music by itself creates neither social conditions nor human behavior".
- **Goal:** They're asking us about the claim that "music is merely sound", which supports an intermediate conclusion, which then supports the main conclusion.

**Answer choices**
**A. It is a generalization a particular instance of which is cited by the argument in order to undermine the viewpoint that the argument is attacking.** — Out of Scope: generalization of instance / Too Strong: view the argument attacks (3% of students)
A generalization of a particular instance would be, "Eddie played guitar and the only thing that happened was vibrating sound waves. Thus, music is merely sound." We don't have any particular example of music only being sound, so this claim we're asked about is not a generalization of a particular instance. Furthermore, it's too strong or too much of a stretch to say the argument is attacking a viewpoint. The author is definitely saying that it would be incorrect to think that music conveys specific emotions like love vs. joy, but that idea hasn't been attributed to some viewpoint, so there's no one here for the author to "attack".

**B. It is a portion of the conclusion drawn in the argument.** — Wrong Role (8% of students)
It's not part of the conclusion. Yes, it's found in the same sentence as the conclusion, but that doesn't make it part of the conclusion. Consider this argument: We should order sushi, because Jen loves sushi. The conclusion is "we should order sushi" and the support is "Jen loves sushi". We can't say that "Jen loves sushi" is a portion of the conclusion. It's a portion of the argument, yes. The conclusion of this argument ends and the support for it begins at the support indicator "for".

**C. It is a claim that is offered as partial support for the argument's conclusion.** — Correct (69% of students)
This just says that our claim is one of the supporting ideas. We agree! If anyone rejected this because they were like, "Well, yes that's true, but ...", there's not really that sort of rationale for getting rid of Role answers. They're just true or not. There might be zero or almost zero cases in which we've eliminated an answer by saying, "That's true, but it's not the best true answer available". Pretty much always, if it matches we pick it.

**D. It is a generalization the truth of which is claimed to be necessary to establish the conclusion of the argument.** — Out of Scope: claimed to be necessary (19% of students)
The author never claimed that "music needs to be seen as merely sound" in order for it to be true that "the meaning of a piece of music is the core of the emotion it elicits". This answer isn't saying that "music is just sound" is necessary; it's specifically saying that the author claimed that "music is just sound" is necessary, which definitely did not happen.

**E. It is a hypothesis that must be rejected, according to the argument, because it is inconsistent with certain evidence.** — Opposite: rejected (1% of students)
The author isn't rejecting the idea that "music is merely sound". The author is explicitly using it as support for the the conclusion. She argued, "So claim 1, for claim 2 and therefore claim 3".

This answer looks like a contrapositive of that, sorta: wasn't the case that a sea creature survived in new habitat after having been dumped by a ship → usually would not wreak havoc in the new habitat. The easiest way to distance ourselves from this is to notice that it's addressing a past tense historical fact. The author isn't assuming anything about the past. She's pitching us a supposedly viable plan we could try in the future, so her assumptions are about what would be true in the hypothetical world in which we tried her plan. This answer is about what has been true in the actual world we live in. If we fixed the tense (sea creatures will rarely, if ever, wreak havoc .... ), this answer still would struggle. When we negate it, we get this: Sea creatures have frequently wreaked ecological havoc in a new habitat, without the backstory of surviving in the new habitat after having been deposited there by ships. In simpler terms, the negation would be saying, "Sea creatures have frequently messed up a new habitat for a reason separate from the problem the author is trying to solve". Okay, would that weaken the argument? No, because the author isn't saying her midocean solution will prevent all or most cases of sea creatures messing up a new habitat they're in. She's just saying it's a viable way to address the part of that problem caused by ships dumping their ballast water.

**D. Currently, seawater is pumped into or out of the ballast tanks of oceangoing ships to maintain proper stability only when unloading or loading cargo.** — Too Strong: only / Only Thing Mentioned ≠ Only Thing (4% of students)
Trap answers on Necessary Assumption love to take the only thing an author mentioned and then act like it's the only thing that has that property. Like if I said, "There are some girls in my class that wear earrings", it would accuse me of assuming, "Only the girls in the class wear earrings". Here, it told us that "to maintain stability, water is pumped in and out of ballasts", so there's a trap answer saying, "the only time water is pumped in and out is to maintain stability". If we negated this and said that there are other times too when seawater is pumped in or out, that wouldn't hurt the author's argument at all.

**E. There are at least some oceangoing ships whose stability could be adequately maintained while emptying and refilling their ballast tanks in midocean.** — Correct (61% of students)
This is something that needs to be true for the author's Plan to be feasible, and therefore it's required in order for her plan to be "viable". If we negate this (contradicting at least one = none) There are no oceangoing ships whose stability could be adequately maintained while performing the move the author proposed. That doesn't sound like a viable plan to me anymore. There are no ships that can adequately maintain stability to pull it off.
