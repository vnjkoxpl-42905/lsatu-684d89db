## S1 · Q1 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Grecia: The survey that we are conducting needs to track employment status by age, so respondents should be asked to indicate their age.

Hidalgo: We don’t need results that provide employment status figures for every single age. So we should instead ask respondents merely to identify the age range that they fall into.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, most justifies Hidalgo’s stance?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (So):** We should just ask respondents to identify the age range that they fall into.
- **Evidence:** We don't need results that provide employment status figures for every single age.
- **Evaluate:** On Principle-Strengthen, we're usually just trying to prephrase a rule that sounds like "If Premise, then Conclusion".

  So in this case,
  "If we don't need results that provide figures for every single age, then we should merely ask for respondents' age range."

  If we wanted to go a little more abstract, we could say,
  "If you don't need a specific data point, then you should just ask for a fuzzy one."
- **Goal:** Look for a rule whose trigger would apply to our evidence (we don't need results that map to each single age) and whose outcome helps to support our conclusion (we should just ask for age range, not their specific age).

**Answer choices**
**A. Surveys gathering information for a specific purpose should not collect more detailed personal information than is necessary to achieve the purpose.** — Correct (99% of students)
This rule would look like this:
```
If survey is gathering      then should not
information for a        →   collect more detailed
specific purpose             personal info than
                             needed for purpose
```
Does the trigger apply? Is this survey gathering information for a specific purpose?

Yes, it's trying to track employment status by age group (not by individual age).

Okay, since the trigger applies, the outcome applies. This is saying that "the survey shouldn't collect more detailed personal information than is necessary to achieve the purpose of tracking employment status by age group".

Does that support our conclusion?

Yes, or at least it supports the implied rebuttal conclusion that "respondents should not be asked to indicate their age"!

Hidalgo is saying, "Let's not ask them their specific age (we don't need figures for every single age). That personal info is more detailed than what's needed for our purpose. Let's just ask them their age range, since that's as detailed as we need for our purpose."

**B. Survey respondents should not be asked a particular question if they are unlikely to answer accurately.** — Bad Trigger Match (0% of students)
This rule would look like this:
```
If respondent is        then should not
likely to answer     →  ask that question
question inaccurately
```
Hidalgo thinks that "we should not ask them their specific age".

Does the trigger apply? Were we told that respondents are unlikely to answer the question "what is your age" accurately?

No, we weren't told that. Since the trigger isn't clearly applicable to our situation, this rule goes nowhere.

**C. Sensitive personal information should be gathered only if a secure means of storing that information is available.** — Bad Trigger Match (0% of students)
This rule would look like this, once we contraposed it to get the "judgy" term on the right side.
```
If secured means        then sensitive personal
of storing personal  →  info should not be
info not available      gathered
```
Hidalgo thinks that "we should not ask them their specific age". Does that qualify as "sensitive personal info"? Not clearly, but maybe.

More importantly, does the trigger apply? Were we told that Grecia and Hidalgo have no secured means of storing personal info?

Not at all. Since the trigger doesn't clearly apply to our situation, this rule goes nowhere.

**D. Surveys should be allowed to gather any information that might be needed to meet their purposes.** — Bad Conclusion Match / Illegal Negation (0% of students)
This rule would look like this:
```
If information might     then survey should
be needed to meet     →  be allowed to gather
the purpose of survey    that info
```
Hidalgo's conclusion is that we "should ask only for age range / should not ask for specific age". He's not trying to conclude that the survey should be allowed to gather certain info. Since the conclusion doesn't really match either of the ideas Hidalgo is trying to conclude, this isn't going to work as well as the correct answer does.

This answer seems to be trying to bait people into picking it, because if we did an Illegal Negation, it would be pretty useful.
```
ILLEGAL NEGATION OF THE ANSWER
If information is        then survey should
not needed to meet    →  not be allowed to
the purpose of survey    gather that info
```
That rule would actually help strengthen: we don't need to ask people their specific age in order to achieve the purpose of the survey, so according to this altered rule the survey should not be allowed to gather people's specific age.

**E. Surveys should gather detailed personal information only if survey respondents are first told about how that information will be used.** — Bad Trigger Match (0% of students)
This rule would look like this, once we contraposed it to get the "judgy" term on the right side.
```
If survey respondents    then survey should
are not first told how →  not gather detailed
info will be used         personal info
```
Does the trigger apply? Are these survey respondents not going to be told how the information will be used, prior to being asked their age / age range?

We have no idea. For all we know the people conducting the survey are going to say, "hey, we're doing a survey tracking employment status by age. Can you tell us whether you're employed and how old you are?"

Since we don't know if the trigger applies, this answer goes nowhere.

## S1 · Q2 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
In 1893, an excavation led by Wilhelm Dörpfeld uncovered an ancient city he believed to be Troy, the site of the war described in Homer’s epic poem the *Iliad*. But that belief cannot be correct. In the *Iliad*, the Trojan War lasted ten years, but a city as small as the one uncovered by Dörpfeld’s team could not have withstood a siege lasting ten years.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** That belief cannot be correct.
  rephrase: The ancient city Wilhelm discovered was not the famous Troy, the site of the war described in 'the *Iliad*'.
- **Evidence:** In 'The *Iliad*', the Trojan War lasted ten years, but the ancient city Wilhelm discovered could not have withstood a siege lasting ten years.
- **Evaluate:** This one doesn't seem like a ripe contender for a Missing Link assumption, so let's go into Objection mode instead, and then we can think of Defender assumptions that rule out our objections.

  Given that ... the *Iliad* says the war lasted ten years but this city could not have withstood a war lasting ten years,

  How could we argue that ... this city Wilhelm found was Troy, the site of that war?

  The two premises are irreconcilable. The book says the war was ten years. Reality says this city is too small for a ten year siege.

  Do we have to assume the book is correct about the length of the war? Maybe Homer exaggerated the length for dramatic effect.

  Do we have to assume that in a ten year war, this city was under siege the entire ten years? Maybe this city is the site of the war, but only some of the ten year war was actually "a siege" on this city?
- **Goal:** Let's look for an answer ruling out one of these objections, saying something like:
  - Homer's estimate of the 10 year war is accurate
  - if the war were ten years, then this city would have necessarily been under siege that whole time

  The correct answer, when negated, will weaken the argument. In other words, it will allow us to argue that Wilhelm really did discover the Troy, the site of Trojan War.

**Answer choices**
**A. In 1893, scholars knew of no other ancient city that could have been Troy.** — Too Strong: no other (1% of students)
The author doesn't need to believe that in 1893 there not a single other contender city for Troy. Just because the one discovered by Wilhelm is the only one mentioned doesn't mean we get to act like it was the only one being considered by scholars.

**B. The *Iliad* does not provide any clues about the specific location of Troy.** — Too Strong: provides zero clues (2% of students)
This has the lovable ruling out "not / no" that so many correct Necessary Assumption answers have. If we negate it, does it weaken to say, "Hey, author -- you know the *Iliad* does provide some clues about the specific location of Troy."

No, that doesn't weaken or strengthen. Until we know whether those clues corroborate or undermine the city that Wilhelm found, we have no idea of clues in the book strengthens or weakens the idea that this city was Troy.

**C. Dörpfeld’s team found no evidence in the city they excavated that a siege had occurred there.** — Too Strong: found no evidence (8% of students)
This has the lovable ruling out "not / no" that so many correct Necessary Assumption answers have. If we negate it, does it weaken to say, "Hey, author -- you know Wilhelm did find some evidence of a siege there."

At first, yes, that does seem to weaken. But it doesn't in reality. After all, the author isn't denying that the city Wilhelm found was ever involved in a war. She's only saying it wasn't involved in any ten year war (too small) and thus wouldn't be a good contender for being Troy.

She could happily agree that, "Sure, Wilhelm found some evidence that this city was at some point under siege. But Wilhelm didn't find evidence that this city was under a ten year siege, and with good reason, since a city this small couldn't handle that long a siege."

**D. The city excavated by Dörpfeld’s team had many features that scholars of the time believed Troy had.** — Too Strong: many / Weakens (2% of students)
This is tempting since we would presume it's probably the case, if we were thinking about probably led up to Dorpfeld concluding that this city was Troy.

But it's not a necessary idea for the author. If we negate this and say, "The city did not have many features that scholars believed Troy had", that would actually strengthen the author's argument that this city was not Troy.

**E. The *Iliad* accurately represents the duration of the Trojan War.** — Correct (87% of students)
If we negate this, we can say, "Yo, author — I know the *Iliad* said it was a ten year war, and that's why you're disqualifying this city from contention, but the *Iliad* did not accurately represent the duration of the war. In reality, it was only a couple years long, and so a city this small could have withstood a siege of that length. And thus this city could be Troy, the site of the war."

The author is treating a detail from the *Iliad* as though it's a historical fact: "Since 'we know' that the war was 10 years, and since this city is too small for a ten year war, this city can't be the site of the war."

But we don't know the war was 10 years. That's just a detail from an epic poem. Who said it's accurate or even intended to be truthful?

## S1 · Q4 — Agree/Disagree

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Monroe: Our organization’s project has been a failure. Our stated goal was to reduce as much as possible the number of homes in the community that lack electricity. Now, at the project’s conclusion, approximately 2,000 homes are still without electricity.

Wilkerson: But before the project began, over 5,000 homes in the community had no electricity. Surely bringing electricity to around 3,000 homes counts as a success for the project.

### Prompt
Monroe and Wilkerson disagree over the truth of which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Monroe's Claims:**
  1. The organization's project has been a failure (the project failed to reduce as much as possible the number of homes in the community that lack electricity)
  2. Our stated goal was to reduce as much as possible the number of homes in the community that lack electricity.
  3. The project is over, and approximately 2,000 homes are still without electricity.
- **Wilkerson's Responses:**
  1. Disagrees - She says that the project has not been a failure. It's been a success!
  2. No comment on what the stated goal was.
  3. She implicitly agrees with this. She is acting like the current number is 2,000 (since she thinks they've reduced from 5,000 by 3,000 homes).
- **Evaluate / Goal:** Since we found an explicit disagreement, we can probably head to the answers looking for an answer that talks about whether or not the project has been a failure or a success.

**Answer choices**
**A. Approximately 2,000 homes in the community are still without electricity.** — Agree (0% of students)
They seemed to agree about this claim. Monroe explicitly says it and Wilkerson does 5,000 - 2,000 math to arrive at her 3,000 number.

**B. Before the organization’s project began, over 5,000 homes in the community had no electricity.** — Unsupported Disagree Position (0% of students)
Wilkerson clearly agrees with this, since she explicitly says so.

We have no reason to think that Monroe disagrees. He doesn't say anything about the specific number of homes that had no electricity when the project began.

**C. The organization’s project must be considered a failure if any home in the community has no electricity.** — Unsupported Agree Position (7% of students)
This is such a strongly worded claim that neither person would likely agree with this.

Wilkerson already thinks the project was not a failure (and there are still 2,000 people without electricity), so Wilkerson definitely disagrees with this answer.

But we couldn't say Monroe agrees with this. He thinks that "if we didn't reduce the number of homes without electricity as much as possible, then we failed".

He doesn't necessarily think, "If we didn't reduce the number to zero, then we failed".

**D. The stated goal of the project was to reduce as much as possible the number of homes in the community that lack electricity.** — Unsupported Disagree Position (1% of students)
We know that Monroe agrees with this because he says it explicitly, but we have no reason to think that Wilkerson disagrees with it.

She never talks about what the stated goal was.

**E. Leaving approximately 2,000 homes in the community without electricity at the conclusion of the project counts as a failure for the project.** — Correct (92% of students)
Both people accept that the current number without electricity is about 2,000.

Monroe would agree with this answer, because he tells us that 2000 homes still lacking electricity indicates that this project was a failure.

Wilkerson would disagree with this, because she thinks the fact that the project brought electricity to 3,000 homes counts as a success.

Researchers asked 100 fifty-year-olds and 100 twenty-year-olds whether they gave blood. Because nearly twice as many fifty-year-olds as twenty-year-olds reported that they sometimes gave blood, the researchers concluded that, on average, fifty-year-olds are more altruistic than twenty-year-olds. But there is reason for skepticism. Many people hesitate to admit that their behavior does not conform to societal expectations.

### Prompt
The reasoning above calls into question a conclusion drawn from statistical data by

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** There is reason for skepticism about the conclusion that "on average, 50 year olds are more altruistic than 20 year olds."
- **Evidence:** Many people hesitate to admit that their behavior does not conform to social expectations.
- **Evaluate:** There was a study with a Curious Fact (an asymmetric comparison):
  why is it that twice as many 50 year olds as 20 year olds reported that they sometimes give blood?

  The researchers concluded one potential Explanation:
  50 year olds are more altruistic than 20 year olds (obviously assuming that being more altruistic is causing them to be more likely to give blood).

  This author is pushing back against that conclusion by suggesting an Alternate Explanation:

  "maybe it's not that the 50 year olds are more altruistic; maybe they're just lying. They give blood as infrequently as 20 year olds do, but while 20 year olds don't mind admitting to that, 50 year olds feel social pressure to conform to societal expectations, so they were twice as likely to lie and say 'Sure, I give blood! That's what good citizens do, after all, heh, heh'."
- **Goal:** We're being asked how our author is calling into question the researchers' conclusion. We'll take any answer that seems descriptively true, but we'd probably anticipate language like, "Suggesting an alternate explanation for the observed difference in age groups".

**Answer choices**
**A. showing that the data are based on an unrepresentative sample** — Out of Scope: unrepresentative sample (6% of students)
The author's only objection to the logic is, "Not so fast -- remember, many people hesitate to admit their behavior doesn't conform to societal expectations."

Nothing in that sentence seems to allude to an unrepresentative sample. The author isn't implying that the 50 year olds in the study, or the 20 year olds, were atypical for their age group.

**B. offering an alternative explanation of some of the data** — Correct (83% of students)
The author's objection to the logic was, "Not so fast -- remember, many people hesitate to admit their behavior doesn't conform to societal expectations."

This objection is suggesting that maybe the reason the 50 year olds were twice as likely to say "I sometimes give blood" is not because they are more altruistic but because they are more afraid to admit that they don't give blood.

After all, society thinks it would be proper and helpful for able-bodied people to give blood, so a 50 year old might be hesitant to admit that they're shirking their societal duties.

Some people might be reading this answer thinking, "All the last sentence says is that many people hesitate to admit to non-conforming behavior. The author never said that 50 year olds were more reluctant to admit to that behavior than 20 year olds. So how do we know that the author is offering it as an alternate explanation for the study's skewed results?"

That's a fair question, with the "unfair" answer of, "Cuz you just have to figure out the storyline the author is presumably selling. The test writer is leaving a lot of blanks for our common sense to try to fill in. If someone is resisting a causal interpretation of a study and saying, 'maybe we shouldn't be so hasty in concluding that. After all, consideration X', then that person is either suggesting that X is an alternate causal interpretation for the data, or suggesting that X makes the researchers' conclusion seem implausible."

**C. showing that one cannot directly observe altruism** — Out of Scope: can't directly observe (9% of students)
The author's only objection to the logic is, "Not so fast -- remember, many people hesitate to admit their behavior doesn't conform to societal expectations."

Nothing in that sentence seems to suggest that it's impossible to directly observe altruism.

**D. criticizing the motives of the researchers** — Out of Scope: researchers' motives (1% of students)
The author's only objection to the logic is, "Not so fast -- remember, many people hesitate to admit their behavior doesn't conform to societal expectations."

Nothing in that sentence seems to criticize the motives of the researchers. That last sentence is talking about the motives of the survey respondents.

**E. offering a specific counterexample** — Out of Scope: counterexample (2% of students)
The author's only objection to the logic is, "Not so fast -- remember, many people hesitate to admit their behavior doesn't conform to societal expectations."

Nothing in that sentence offers a counterexample. A counterexample to the conclusion that "50 year olds are more altruistic than 20 year olds" would be talking about a specific 20 year old who was at least as altruistic as a specific 50 year old.

Or it could cite a different situation in which the younger crew was more altruistic than the older crew (with roughly the same ages).

But the author's objection isn't talking about altruism at all.

  2. There's little demand for rugs in Glendale.
  3. People planning to open a new business in Glendale should avoid going into the rug-selling business.
- **Renate's Response?:**
  1. Did Renate argue "It's false that to say that Glendale's only rug store went out of business"? That would mean he's either arguing that the store that went out of business wasn't the only rug store in Glendale, or he's arguing that the only rug store in Glendale didn't go out of business..

  2. Did Renate argue, "there much demand for rugs in Glendale?"

## S1 · Q10 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Anthropologist: In an experiment, two groups of undergraduates were taught how to create one of the types of stone tools that the Neanderthals made in prehistoric times. One group was taught using both demonstrations and elaborate verbal explanations, whereas the other group learned by silent example alone. The two groups showed a significant difference neither in the speed with which they acquired the toolmaking skills nor in the level of proficiency they reached. This shows that Neanderthals could just as well have created their sophisticated tools even if they had no language.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the anthropologist’s argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (this shows that):** Even if Neanderthals didn't have language, they could just as well have created their sophisticated tools.
- **Evidence:** In an experiment, one group of undergrads was taught how to create a Neanderthal tool using both demonstrations and elaborate verbal explanations. The other group was taught how to use the tool by silent example alone.

  The two groups didn't show any difference in terms of how fast or how well they learned how to use the tool.
- **Evaluate:** This is an Anti-Causal conclusion, claiming that possessing language would not have assisted Neanderthals in terms of the sophisticated tools they created.

  The evidence is seeming to show that language didn't make a difference when it came to learning how to use one of the tools Neanderthals used.

  The author seems to assume a missing Link like, "if language doesn't affect the speed or proficiency at which you learn how to use a tool, then it doesn't have a meaningful role in creating the tool".

  We can prime our brain for objections by thinking,

  Given that ... the group that learned by silent example learned just as quickly and just as well as the group that learned by demonstrations and verbal explanations,

  How can we argue that ... Neanderthals could not have created their sophisticated tools just as well if they had no language?

  Were the two groups in the experiment fair to compare, or was the "silent example" group comprised of people who are especially good at learning how to use a tool without verbal explanations?

  Is it possible that you can learn how to use a tool without language being necessary, but creating a sophisticated tool would benefit from language?
- **Goal:** Look for a way to argue that even though this study is true, Neanderthals still would have needed language to create their sophisticated tools the way they did.

**Answer choices**
**A. Apart from the sophistication of their stone tools, there is a great deal of evidence suggesting that Neanderthals possessed some form of language.** — No Impact (6% of students)
The author was never claiming that Neanderthals didn't have language. So we can't weaken this argument by saying, "Hey, Neanderthals had language!"

The author was just claiming, in a hypothetical world where Neanderthals didn't have language, that they still could have created their sophisticated tools just as easily.

**B. The students who were taught with verbal explanations were allowed to discuss the toolmaking techniques among themselves, whereas the students who learned by silent example were not.** — Strengthens, if anything (3% of students)
This answer gives another advantage to the group that had language. They were not only taught using language but they were also free to discuss what they were learning using language.

Meanwhile, the other group was taught without language and wasn't allowed to use language to discuss the tools with their fellow group members, and yet they still acquired skills in using the tool just as fast and just as well as the first group.

This would only help the author to argue even further than language is not providing benefit to the group that's using it.

**C. The tools that the undergraduates were taught to make were much simpler and easier to make than most types of tools created by Neanderthals.** — Correct (82% of students)
This de-values the relevance of the study as a way of assessing whether someone can learn a sophisticated Neanderthal tool without using language.

The conclusion is about whether language was needed / helpful when it came to creating sophisticated Neanderthal tools.

If the tools in the study were much simpler and easier and most Neanderthal tools, then the study wasn't actually testing any of the sophisticated tools that Neanderthals made.

Thus, the fact that in the study undergrads could learn these super simple tools without the benefit of language becomes much weaker evidence that they could have learned a sophisticated Neanderthal tool without the benefit of language.

I could probably teach you how to use a simple tool like a hammer without language, but it would be much harder to teach you how to use a sophisticated tool like a sewing machine without language.

**D. The instructor who taught the group of students who learned by silent example alone was much less proficient at making the stone tools than was the instructor who taught the other group of students.** — Strengthens (5% of students)
This answer is similar to (B), in its effect.

The author is looking at the fact that both groups learned tool usage equally well, regardless of whether language was involved, and concluding "it must be because language isn't needed to learn these tools".

An alternate explanation for why the two groups ended up having similar success could have been the opposite of (B) (yes, the teacher couldn't talk, but the students still talked to each other) or it could be the opposite of this answer (the reason the silent group did as well as the talking group is because the silent group had a much better teacher than the talking group had).

But this actually rules out that explanation, so it strengthens.

Like (B), it's saying that the silent group had yet another obstacle (teacher didn't talk / students couldn't talk amongst themselves / teacher was worse). And yet if the students still learned the tool skills just as well, that helps the author argue that language is not important to learning.

**E. The tools created by Neanderthals were much less sophisticated than the tools created by anatomically modern humans who almost certainly possessed language and lived at the same time as the Neanderthals.** — No Impact (4% of students)
This answer compares the sophistication of Neanderthal tools to that of modern humans living at the same time.

Old humans who were contemporaneous with the Neanderthals have nothing to do with our conversation.

The only way to make them relevant would be an answer like, "The tools created by anatomically modern humans who lived at the same time as the Neanderthals were much less sophisticated than those made by the Neanderthals and those humans could not have created such tools if they had had no language".

## S1 · Q14 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Ethicist: The general principle—if one ought to do something then one can do it—does not always hold true. This may be seen by considering an example. Suppose someone promises to meet a friend at a certain time, but—because of an unforeseen traffic jam—it is impossible to do so.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption required by the ethicist’s argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** There are exceptions to the principle that “if one ought to do something, then one can do it”. (i.e. at least sometimes, you ought to do something but can’t do it)
- **Evidence:** Some promises to meet a friend at a certain time, but they can’t (an unforeseen traffic jam thwarted them).
- **Evaluate:** For the author to successfully show a counterexample to the general principle, she must clearly establish a case in which
  a) you ought to do X
  b) you can’t do X

  In her example, have both things been established?
  X = meet someone at a certain time

  It’s established that our person can’t do X (they can’t meet their friend at the agreed-upon time because of the traffic jam).

  Has it been established that our person ought to do X? No. We just know that the friend promised to do X.
- **Goal:** The author is assuming “if you promised to meet your friend, you ought to do it.”

**Answer choices**
**A. If a person failed to do something she or he ought to have done, then that person failed to do something that she or he promised to do.** — Too Strong: all failures come from promises (30% of students)
This confusingly can look like a contrapositive of what we wanted. We wanted,
```
promised to do X → ought to do X
~ought to do X → ~promised to do X
```
That contrapositive is saying, “If a certain action wasn’t something you ought to have done, then it wasn’t something you promised to have done”.

The rule in this answer is saying “If you failed to do something you ought to have done… “

There’s a big difference between “Action X wasn’t something you ought to have done” and “Action X was something you ought to have done, but you failed to do so”.

If you think about this answer conversationally, rather than symbolically, it’s easier to hear how this is way too strong an idea.

The author’s assumption is a pretty moderate real world principle:
if you promised to do X, you should do X.

This answer is a crazy extreme real world idea:
If you should have done X but failed to do so, you must have promised to do X.

Huh? I should have brushed my teeth this morning, but failed to do so. That means that I promised to brush my teeth?

I should have filed my taxes on time, but I failed to do so. According to this rule, that proves that I promised to file my taxes on time?

**B. Only an event like an unforeseen traffic jam could excuse a person from the obligation to keep a promise.** — Too Strong: only traffic jams (5% of students)
Our author isn’t assuming anything about traffic jams being a special, unique type of thing. It was just a random example she plucked out of the thin air.

This would look like this …
excused from promise —> traffic jam

Is that a reasoning move the author made? No.

In fact, is the author even thinking this reversed version?
if there’s a traffic jam, then you’re excused from the promise

No, the opposite. The author is assuming that this is an example of someone who should still be keeping their promise.

**C. If there is something that a person ought not do, then it is something that that person is capable of not doing.** — Out of Scope: ought not to do (11% of students)
Nothing in this argument deals with things we ought not to do. There are three possibilities: you ought to (call your mom), you ought not to (ignore your mom), or other things (tapping your foot to the song on the radio (we’re not saying you ought to or that you ought not to).

The author’s argument is only about that first category of actions, whether or not things you ought to do only consist of things you can do.

If we looked at this conditionally, it’s saying
```
ought not to do X → not capable of doing X
capable of doing X → ought to do X
```
This is an illegal reversal of the principle that the author is arguing against.

**D. The obligation created by a promise is not relieved by the fact that the promise cannot be kept.** — Correct (53% of students)
If we negate this answer, it says “the obligation created by a promise is relieved by the fact that the promise cannot be kept”.

Does that weaken the argument? Yes! Remember the author was assuming, “If you promised to meet your friend, then you ought to keep that promise.”

Negating this answer is saying, “No, you don’t need to keep that promise. Your obligation to keep that promise was relieved by the fact that the promise cannot be kept. We moral judges wouldn’t be looking at you in the traffic jam, thinking, ‘How dare he. He should be keeping his promise’.”

Answers on Necessary Assumption that have the word “not” are ripe for the Negation Test. When we remove the word “not”, does it turn into an Objection? If so, pick it!

If you prefer thinking about the positive version of this answer, remember that our author thinks that “If you promised to meet your friend at a certain time, then you ought to do so.”

This answer is just reinforcing that: “Don’t think that you’re off the hook just because of the traffic jam. The obligation created by your promise doesn’t disappear just because you can’t keep the promise anymore.”

**E. If an event like an unforeseen traffic jam interferes with someone’s keeping a promise, then that person should not have made the promise to begin with.** — Out of Scope: should not have made promise (2% of students)
The author is never discussing a situation in which someone shouldn’t have made a promise. We’re talking about a situation in which someone should keep a promise, but can’t.

The first half of this conditional could work, but the second half would have to be tweaked:
```
traffic jam interferes     person still ought
with person             →  to keep promise
keeping promise
```

In order to relieve traffic congestion, the city of Gastner built a new highway linking several of the city’s suburbs to the downtown area. However, the average commute time for workers in downtown Gastner increased after the new highway opened.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the increase in average commute time?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that ...:** The city built a new highway linking several suburbs to the downtown area, in order to relieve traffic.
- **How can it be that ...:** Since the new highway opened, the average commute time for workers downtown has increased?
- **Evaluate / Goal:** We thought the new highway would relieve traffic congestion, thereby reducing commute times. But for some people (workers in downtown), the commute has gotten worse.

  So we need to hear something else that's changed with the new highway, that would make commuting slow down for people who work downtown.

**Answer choices**
**A. Most people who work in the downtown area of Gastner commute from one of the city’s suburbs.** — Deepens Paradox (8% of students)
If most downtown workers are commuting from the 'burbs, then the effect of the new highway should be making their lives / commutes better.

After all, the highway's purpose was to better link suburbs to downtown. So this answer makes us even more confused how commute time has gotten worse for these workers.

**B. The location of the new highway is most convenient for people who commute to and from Gastner’s largest suburbs.** — Deepens Paradox, if anything (8% of students)
The fact that the highway is most convenient to people in the largest suburbs means that we can expect a lot of people will want to use this new highway. That should decrease traffic congestion on the roads people used to use to get from suburbs to downtown, and thus commuting times should be going down.

This doesn't give us any reason to explain why commuting got worse for downtown workers. It seems to either not have anything to with them, or it sounds like it's a positive for them.

**C. Shortly after the new highway was opened, several suburban roads connecting to the new highway were upgraded with new stoplights.** — No Impact (11% of students)
This is a tempting answer for a lot of us, because it shows something else that's changed since the highway was opened, and the idea of new stoplights sounds like something that could explain increased commuting time.

But it sounds from the phrase upgraded with new stoplights like these intersections already had stoplights, and those were swapped out and replaced with new ones.

In order for commuting time to be made worse, we want to believe that there used to be no stoplight at an intersection, and now there is one. But replacing a stoplight with an upgraded, new one doesn't have any common sense connection to slowing down traffic flow.

**D. At the same time the new highway was being built, road repair work was being done on important streets leading to downtown Gastner.** — Wrong Timeframe (9% of students)
This is talking about stuff prior to the highway opening (when it was still being built). We're looking for something else that changed once the highway opened.

**E. In Gastner’s downtown area, traffic on the roads near the new highway became more congested after the new highway was opened.** — Correct (64% of students)
This is telling us something else that changed once the highway was opened. That something, "traffic became more congested", definitely sounds like it could cause increased commute times.

It's not a very compelling correct answer, but it's hard to make an argument that any other answer choice does more than this one to help explain the increase in commuting time.

I think the test writers want us to picture lower Manhattan, where traffic near the Lincoln and Holland tunnels (the highways leading out of downtown) gets really, really congested.

This answer is also intended, I think, to allow for the idea that people coming from the suburbs might now be able to get to downtown faster, but for the workers in the downtown area, the local traffic situation has gotten worse, because now there are these big queues of cars waiting to get on / off the highway.

1st                 2nd

doesn't have

## S1 · Q19 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Science teacher: An abstract knowledge of science is very seldom useful for the decisions that adults typically make in their daily lives. But the skills taught in secondary school should be useful for making such decisions. Therefore, secondary school science courses should teach students to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues, such as health and public policy, instead of or perhaps in addition to teaching more abstract aspects of science.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption the science teacher’s argument requires?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (therefore):** Secondary school science courses should teach students to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues (instead of, or in addition to abstract science).
- **Evidence:** Secondary schools should be teaching skills that are useful for the decisions that adults typically make in their daily lives.

  Abstract science doesn't do that.
- **Evaluate:** There's a big New Concept in the Conclusion:
  teaching students to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues.

  Usually when the conclusion introduces a brand new concept, we should be able to articulate what the author is assuming about this new idea.

  Consider the assumption in this argument:
  Joe isn't funny. But the guy that Lisa dates should be funny. Therefore, Lisa should date Fernando, instead of or perhaps in addition to Joe.

  What are we assuming about Fernando? Why are we suggesting that Lisa date him?

  We're assuming that Fernando is funny.

  Similarly, in this argument, we heard:
  Abstract science isn't useful to adults. But what we teach should be useful. Therefore, we should teach students to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues.

  What are we assuming about "learning to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues"?
- **Goal:** We're assuming that "Learning to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues" is "useful for adults".

**Answer choices**
**A. Secondary schools should teach only those skills that are the most useful for the decisions that adults typically make in their daily lives.** — Too Strong: only / most useful / Contradicted (5% of students)
Not only does extreme language like "only those skills / most useful" scare us on Necessary Assumption, but this is even contradicted in the conclusion.

The conclusion is saying that schools can still teach abstract science, even though the argument established that abstract science is not most useful.

**B. Teaching secondary school students the more abstract aspects of science is at least as important as teaching them to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues.** — Opposite, if anything / Too Strong: at least as important (3% of students)
The author's conclusion is allowing for the possibility of still teaching some abstract science, but it also suggests the possibility that secondary schools teach no abstract science.

So there's no reason we would say the author thinks that teaching abstract science is at least as important as teaching them to evaluate science-based arguments.

If I say, "We should do X instead of Y, or in addition to Y", then we're doing X either way.

Y, on the other hand, is optional. So it seems like X is more important than Y.

**C. Adults who have an abstract knowledge of science are no better at evaluating science-based arguments regarding practical issues than are adults who have no knowledge of science at all.** — Too Strong: no better (6% of students)
The author says that abstract science knowledge is very seldom useful for adults, but she doesn't say that it's never useful.

So she might think that adults with an abstract knowledge of science could be somewhat better at evaluating science-based arguments than are people with no knowledge of science at all.

Negating this answer and saying that, "adults with an abstract knowledge of science are better at evaluating science-based arguments than are adults with no knowledge of science at all" isn't really damaging to the argument.

**D. No secondary school science courses currently teach students how to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues.** — Too Strong: no courses (6% of students)
The author is recommending that secondary schools do X. That doesn't mean she's assuming that no secondary schools currently do X.

**E. The ability to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues is sometimes useful in making the decisions that adults typically make in their daily lives.** — Correct (80% of students)
This supplies the Missing Link we anticipated. It connects the New Concept in the Conclusion to the Evidence.

The author is saying that "We should teach something useful. Abstract knowledge isn't useful, so instead we should do X".

That is Assuming a Difference. That's assuming that X is useful.

In this case, X is "evaluating science-based arguments regarding practical issues". If we negated this answer and said that the ability to evaluate science-based arguments is never useful in making typical adult decisions, then we would make the author's recommendation sound totally stupid.

## S1 · Q21 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Most kinds of soil contain clay, and virtually every kind of soil contains either sand or organic material, or both. Therefore, there must be some kinds of soil that contain both clay and sand and some that contain both clay and organic material.

### Prompt
The pattern of flawed reasoning in which one of the following arguments is most parallel to that in the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:**
```
Most soil has clay              Most A are B
Most soil has sand              Most A are C or D
or organic material
```
- **Conclusion:**
```
Some soil is clay and sand      Some A are C
and                             and
Some soil is clay and org       Some A are D
```
- **Evaluate:** Because we see “most” in one premise and “some” in the conclusion, we want to think of “virtually every” (i.e. “almost all”) as providing us another “most” claim.

  Why? Well, this argument comes close to making the famous and legal “most + most” inference, which looks like this:
```
Most A’s are B
+
Most A’s are C
--------------------
Some B are C
```
  With the ingredients we were provided here, we could have made this inference:
```
Most S’s have C
+
Most S’s have SD or OM
-------------------------------
Some C are (SD or OM)
```
  What the author is doing illegally is thinking he can break that up into two different takeaways: Some C are SD, and Some C are OM.

  Conversationally, the problem with the conclusion is that it’s possible that sand never overlaps with clay, or that organic material never overlaps with clay. It’s not possible that both of them never overlap. At least one of them does need to overlap with clay, but both of them don’t have to.

  If you crave numbers for seeing this flaw, let’s say there are 11 types of soil.
  6 clay, 5 no clay (most soil has clay)
  8 sand, 2 org material, 1 neither (almost all has sand or org material)

  Does there have to be soil that has clay and sand? Yes. 6 + 8 add up to 14, but there are only 11 types of soil, so we know that 3 of them overlap.

  Does there have to be soil that has clay and organic material? No. It’s very possible that the 2 types of soil that have organic material are 2 of the 5 types of soil that don’t have clay.
- **Goal:** The cool thing about Parallel Flaw is that we don’t have to thoroughly understand the original argument’s flaw in order to match the argument, when the original argument has conditional or quantified logic.

  We need two Most premises, and one of them needs to have an “or” outcome. The conclusion needs to make two Some claims, one for each member of that “or” outcome.
```
Premise 1: Most A’s are B
Premise 2: Most A’s are C or D
Conclusion: Some A’s are C, and Some A’s are D
```

**Answer choices**
**A. Most pharmacies sell cosmetics. Virtually every pharmacy sells shampoo or toothpaste, or both. Therefore, if there are pharmacies that sell both cosmetics and toothpaste, there must also be some that sell both cosmetics and shampoo.** — Bad Conclusion Match (14% of students)
This conclusion is conditional. We want a conclusion that makes two Some claims. The premises did match:

Most pharmacies sell cosmetics.
Most pharmacies sell shampoo or toothpaste.

But the conclusion would have needed to be “Some places that sell cosmetics also sell shampoo, and some places that sell cosmetics also sell toothpaste”.

**B. Undoubtedly, most pharmacies sell cosmetics, for almost all pharmacies sell either shampoo or toothpaste, or both, and there are some pharmacies that sell both cosmetics and shampoo and some that sell both cosmetics and toothpaste.** — Bad Conclusion Match (8% of students)
This conclusion is a Most claim. We want a conclusion that makes two Some claims. The words “for, after all, since, because” (F.A.B.S.) directly indicate premises and indirectly indicate conclusions. Ideally, we don’t read any of this answer once we’ve already seen that the first claim was the conclusion and that the conclusion was a mismatch.

**C. Most pharmacies sell cosmetics. Nearly all pharmacies sell shampoo or toothpaste, or both. Therefore, unless there are some pharmacies that sell both cosmetics and toothpaste, there must be some that sell both cosmetics and shampoo.** — Bad Conclusion Match (4% of students)
This conclusion is conditional. We want a conclusion that makes two Some claims. The premises did match:

Most pharmacies sell cosmetics.
Most pharmacies sell shampoo or toothpaste.

But the conclusion would have needed to be “Some places that sell cosmetics also sell shampoo, and some places that sell cosmetics also sell toothpaste”.

**D. Virtually every pharmacy that sells shampoo also sells toothpaste. Most pharmacies sell cosmetics. Therefore, there must be some pharmacies that sell both cosmetics and toothpaste and some that sell both cosmetics and shampoo.** — Weak Premise Match (17% of students)
This conclusion makes two Some claims, so it’s worth reading. The problem is that the first premise isn’t saying “Most P’s are S or T”, it’s saying “Most P’s that are S are also T”.

In the original argument, both Most claims were about the same group: “types of soil”. This answer would only match had the original argument said,

Most pharmacies that sell shampoo also sells toothpaste.
Most sandy soil has organic material.

Consider the difference in meaning between these two claims:

Most people have an iPhone or an Android phone.
vs.
Most people that have an iPhone also have an Android phone.

**E. Nearly all pharmacies sell either shampoo or toothpaste, or both. Therefore, since most pharmacies sell cosmetics, there must be some pharmacies that sell both cosmetics and toothpaste and some that sell both cosmetics and shampoo.** — Correct (57% of students)
This has two Some claims in the conclusion, so it's worth reading.

Premise 1: Most pharmacies sell cosmetics.
Premise 2: Most pharmacies sell shampoo or toothpaste.

Conclusion: Some cosmetics sell shampoo, and some cosmetics and toothpaste.

### Stimulus
In 2005, an environmental group conducted a study measuring the levels of toxic chemicals in the bodies of eleven volunteers. Scientifically valid inferences could not be drawn from the study because of the small sample size, but the results were interesting nonetheless. Among the subjects tested, younger subjects showed much lower levels of PCBs—toxic chemicals that were banned in the 1970s. This proves that the regulation banning PCBs was effective in reducing human exposure to those chemicals.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (this proves that):** The regulation banning PCBs was effective in reducing human exposure to those chemicals.
- **Evidence:** The regulation banning PCBs happened in the 1970s.

  In 2005, they measured the levels of toxic chemicals in the bodies of 11 volunteers. Among the subjects tested, younger subjects showed much lower levels of PCB's.
- **Evaluate:** The author's conclusion is offering a causal interpretation of the study's finding, so we can use our Explain Curious Fact framework.

  Curious Fact ... why did the younger subjects have lower levels of PCB's in their bodies than the older subjects did?

  Author's Explanation ... PCBs were banned in the 1970s --> banning PCBs effectively reduced human exposure to those chemicals --> the older subjects (who may have been alive pre-ban) had more exposure to PCBs than the younger subjects did.

  As always, the flaw is that the author is far too certain of one possible explanation. ("This proves that ..")

  And as always, we're supposed to remind the author that there might be other possible ways to explain that data. For example, maybe the younger subjects grew up in countries that never used PCBs. Maybe the livers of younger people are better at processing and expelling PCBs from the bloodstream.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer complaining that the author is too sure of this one possible causal interpretation.

**Answer choices**
**A. takes an inconsistent stance regarding the status of the inferences that can be drawn from the study** — Correct (74% of students)
The word "inconsistent" basically means "contradictory". To be logically inconsistent is to believe both "A" and "not A".

In the context of Flaw questions, we know that one of the 10 Famous Flaws is Self-Contradiction, and the most common language used in a Self-Contradiction answer choice is "inconsistent / incompatible". We also know that this answer is almost always wrong.

Almost always.

We still need to be flexible and ask ourselves questions. Does the author take an inconsistent stance regarding what inferences can be drawn from the study?

Does the author take any stance? Let's research.

Yes, the author says in the 2nd sentence that "Scientifically valid inferences could not be draw from the study (because it's only 11 people)", but then the author goes on and draws an inference from the study.

Is the author drawing what she thinks is a 'scientifically valid inference'? Yes, that's implied by the fact that she says "This proves that".

This answer is thus descriptively true, and it brings up an issue worthy of criticism. "Yo, author -- why are you drawing some big, broad inference about the effects of regulation banning PCBs from this puny sample of 11 people, which you yourself acknowledge is not enough data to make such an inference?"

Wow. A genuine Self-Contradiction example. It's like seeing a bald eagle in the wild.

**B. overlooks the possibility that two or more chemicals produce the same effects** — Not an Objection (2% of students)
Since this answer begins with fails to consider / overlooks the possibility, we can ask ourselves whether the idea that follows would weaken.

Can we object to the author by saying, "You know ... it's possible that two or more chemicals produce the same effects"?

No, that doesn't go against anything the author was thinking. Was she assuming that only one chemical can produce a certain effect?

Not at all. We're talking about PCBs, which are toxic chemicals (plural). And we never hear about any of their effects.

**C. concludes that a generalization has been proven true merely on the grounds that it has not been proven false** — Not Unproven vs. Proven False (7% of students)
This answer describes the famous Unproven vs. Proven False flaw.

Does the author conclude that a generalization has been proven true?

Sure, we could make that work, since saying "banning PCBs was effective in reducing human exposure to those chemicals" is somewhat of a general statement. And the author definitely thinks that this claim has been proven true.

Is the premise the idea that "it's never been proven false that banning PCBs reduced human exposure"?

No, the premise was the blood measurements of 11 volunteers.

**D. takes something to be the cause of a reduction when it could have been an effect of that reduction** — Not a Coherent Objection (10% of students)
This is attractive if we were looking for an answer complaining about the overly confident causal conclusion.

But this doesn't end up making a plausible objection.

The author takes the "ban of PCBs" to be the cause of a reduction "in human exposure to those chemicals".

Can we object by saying, "Actually, author, the ban on PCB's might have been the result of a reduction in human exposure to those chemicals"?

No, that's crazy talk.

How would reduced exposure to PCBs cause a ban on PCBs?

**E. does not consider the possibility that PCBs have detrimental effects on human health several years after exposure** — Not an Objection (7% of students)
Since this answer begins with fails to consider / overlooks the possibility, we can ask ourselves whether the idea that follows would weaken.

Can we object to the author by saying, "You know ... it's possible that PCBs have detrimental effects for several years after exposure"?

Not really. The author wasn't assuming that detrimental effects go away shortly after exposure stops. After all, PCBs were banned in the 1970s, but 30 years later in 2005, the younger subjects showed lower levels of PCBs, not no levels of PCBs.

So the author definitely accepts that PCBs can stay in your system for years after exposure. The argument doesn't get into specifics about any detrimental effects.

## S1 · Q23 — Parallel

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A spy fails by being caught, and it is normally only through being caught that spies reveal their methods. The successful spy is never caught. So the available data are skewed: One can learn a lot about what makes a spy fail but very little about what makes a spy succeed.

### Prompt
Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its reasoning to the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Available data on spies are skewed: you can learn a lot about bad spies but very little about good spies.
- **Evidence:** You only really get data on spies if they're caught, and only bad spies get caught.
- **Evaluate:** Although there are some hints at Conditional Logic here because of the "only through" and "never", the conclusion is not trying to derive anything conditionally, so I think I would resist trying to diagram this and think more about the gist-y principle behind it.

  The author is essentially saying that our data set on spies is inherently biased in a certain way based on the nature of collecting data on spies. It's almost like the data we have on spies is a self-selecting sample of data on "bad spies".

  By definition of us having data on you, you're a bad spy.
- **Goal:** Because this argument seems to be working more on a conversational level of "unrepresentative sample", I wouldn't be as stringent about forcing the answer choice to have all the same structural similarities.

  I basically want to hear about a situation in which our data is skewed because the nature of how we collect the data makes it very likely that we'll see one subpopulation overrepresented.

**Answer choices**
**A. Of those who participated in the marathon, some succeeded and others failed. But those who did not participate at all neither succeeded nor failed, since both success and failure require participation.** — No Skewed Sample (1% of students)
This is a rather amusing argument that is just showing how if you enter a competition, then labels like success / failure can be meaningfully applied. If you don't, then they can't.

There's no skewed sample of data in the end.

(there's an expression people sometimes say, "you know, you miss 100% of the shots you don't take", and this argument would say "No, that's not true. If you don't take a shot, then you neither make nor miss it, since both making and missing require shooting.")

**B. People who are aware of their motives can articulate them. But unconscious motives are usually impossible to acknowledge. So people are more likely to hear about other people’s conscious motives than their unconscious ones.** — Correct (69% of students)
There is a skewed data set here -- we're more likely to have data on people's conscious motives than their unconscious ones.

Is the skewed data a result of the fact that the nature by which we obtain this data makes it way more likely to get one type than another?

Yes, we obtain this data by hearing other people speak about their motives, and for the most part, people can only speak about conscious motives, not unconscious ones.

We obtain data about spies by catching them, and for the most part, only bad spies get caught, not good ones.

**C. It is unclear whether the company’s venture succeeded, because the criteria for its success are undefined. But if the venture had had a measurable goal, then it would have been possible to judge its success.** — No Skewed Sample (3% of students)
This argument has nothing to do with ending up with a skewed impression because of a selection-bias in our data gathering.

This argument is saying we ended up with an ambiguous impression because we had no precision in defining our goals.

**D. A teacher is someone who teaches. In addition, there are people who teach but are not called teachers. So while the number of those called teachers is large, the number of those who teach is even larger.** — Bad Premise Match (3% of students)
We do end up with a comparative conclusion, which is a good structural match. But we don't get there via any selection-bias in data collection.

This is just a mathematical argument saying,
if X, then Y.
but there are also things ~X that are Y.

Thus, there are more Y's than X's.
(after all, the group of Y includes all the X's and then some of these other fools who are Y as well)

That sort of argument doesn't match the selection-bias data gathering argument of the original. This argument is more of a group-membership / Venn diagram type of conversation.

**E. Because someone intervened in the conflict, the effects of that intervention can be discerned. But since no one can investigate what does not happen, it is impossible to discern what would have happened had someone not intervened.** — Weak Premise/Conclusion Match (25% of students)
This ends with a conclusion saying, "It is impossible to discern what would have happened in this situation".

That's a pretty poor match for "we know a lot about this type of thing but very little about this other type of thing".

This argument feels closer than other trap answers to me because it involves a similar sense of "by the nature of this thing, we can't know about it".

But in the original argument, it was like "because a good spy doesn't get caught, we can't learn about their methods".

In the correct answer, it was like "because people can't articulate their unconscious motives, we can't learn about those motives."

This one is more like "because this thing never occurred, we can't know what would have happened if it had occurred".

In addition to that being a weaker parallel for "by the nature of this thing, we can't know much about it", this is also stronger (it's IMPOSSIBLE to know about it), whereas the original argument was saying we normally can't learn about methods of good spies, and the correct answer said that unconscious motives are usually impossible to articulate.

Finally, there's no conclusion here that compares the volume of info we have on one thing to the volume of info we have on another thing.

### Stimulus

The argument requires assuming which one of the following principles?

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (thus / should):** Parents with underage children should be given additional votes to cast on behalf of their underage children in order to achieve fair representation for their families.
- **Evidence:** These types of families make up much of the population, but because only adults can vote, lawmakers pay little attention to the interests of these families.
- **Evaluate:** Most correct answers on questions with Principles in Answers sound like "If Evidence, Then Conclusion" linking ideas.

  But that feels like a weird fit here. We could maybe say that the author is assuming, "if parents can vote on behalf of their kids, then that achieves fair representation".

  This conversation overall involves a Plan / Recommendation. Authors who endorse a plan are usually assuming several things:
  - the plan is feasible
  - people will cooperate with the plan as intended
  - the plan won't backfire
  - the plan addresses the main source of the problem

  It seemed like the author was concerned with the problem that lawmakers in democracies pay too little attention to the interests of families with underage children, because only the adults can vote.

  So the author seems to be assuming that a result of her plan would be that lawmakers would pay more attention to these families.

**A. The amount of attention that lawmakers give to a group’s interests should be directly proportional to the number of voters in that group.** — Too Strong: directly proportional / Unrelated to Conclusion (11% of students)

A family with 5 voters has 25% more voters than does a family with 4 voters. Is this author saying that a lawmaker should give 25% more attention to that larger family's interests?

No, that's too precise. The author is sad that lawmakers pay too little attention to families with underage children, but she isn't assuming an extreme principle that says that # of voters = amount of attention.

Also, this principle would support a conclusion about how much a lawmaker should pay attention.

The actual conclusions are saying, "parents should be given extra votes / this would achieve fair representation". They have nothing to do with instructing lawmakers on how much attention to pay.

**B. Parents should not be given responsibility for making a decision on their child’s behalf unless their child is not mature enough to decide wisely.** — Opposite (2% of students)
This author wants parents to be given extra votes to cast on behalf of their children. So our author thinks that parents should be given responsibility for making a (voting) decision on their child's behalf.

This answer is saying,
```
If the child is mature      → parents shouldn't be
enough to decide wisely       resp for deciding
```

**C. The parents of underage children should always consider the best interests of their children when they vote.** — Too Strong: always / Bad Conclusion Match (3% of students)
The word "always" is super dangerous when we're asked what must be true/assumed.

The author merely is saying that parents should be given extra votes to cast on behalf of their kids. The author never offers any guidance as to how parents should use these votes, so we definitely can't accuse her of thinking that in 100% of cases, they parents should consider the children's best interests.

The conclusions weren't instructing parents how to vote; they were suggesting that we give parents extra votes.

**D. It is not fair for lawmakers to favor the interests of people who have the vote over the interests of people who do not have the vote.** — Weak Conclusion/Evidence Match (9% of students)
The author does seem to think that the current situation is not fair representation for families with underage children.

But is that because lawmakers are favoring the interests of voters over non-voters?

Not quite. Families with underage children still have voters in the family (the parents). The argument was just saying that lawmakers pay too little attention to the interests of these families.

In addition to the fact that the background isn't really about voters vs. non-voters, the conclusion isn't trying to say "unfair", so it doesn't seem like a good match for either part of the argument.

This is a Defender style Principle, which is very rare.

This answer is ruling out that objection.

The author is saying that a family with underage children would get fair representation if the parents had extra votes to cast on behalf of their children. Those families are groups, where some members (the parents) would be voting on behalf of others (the underage children).

So, yes, the author clearly thinks that parents voting on behalf of children can still be considered fair representation.

If we negated this answer, it would be a huge objection: "If some members of a group are voting on behalf of other members, then we can't say that a group is fairly represented".

## S1 · Q25 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Critic: The Gazette-Standard newspaper recently increased its editorial staff to avoid factual errors. But this clearly is not working. Compared to its biggest competitor, the Gazette-Standard currently runs significantly more corrections acknowledging factual errors.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the critic’s argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The move to increase editorial staff to avoid factual errors is clearly not working (i.e. there are at least as many factual errors since the increase in editorial staff was enacted).
- **Evidence:** Compared to its biggest competitor, this paper runs significantly more corrections acknowledging factual errors.
- **Evaluate:** This evidence is a Comparison, and the conclusion is Anti-Causal (the new staff is not causing a reduction in factual errors).

  Usually, when we're weakening an Anti-Causal argument, we're saying that the Comparison evidence presented is not a fair comparison (they had different initial reference points / they have different realities that make it unfair to compare along these lines).
- **Goal:** We can prephrase any Weaken question by thinking, "Even though [evidence], how could I still argue [anti-conclusion]".

  So, "Even though this paper runs way more corrections acknowledging factual errors than does their biggest competitor, how could we still argue that this paper's move to add editorial staff is helping us to avoid factual errors?"

  But in particular we might look for an answer that makes the Evidence less compelling; some way in which this paper and its biggest competitor are unfair to compare.

**Answer choices**
**A. The Gazette-Standard pays its editorial staff lower salaries than its biggest competitor pays its editorial staff.** — Strengthens, if anything (1% of students)
How much either paper pays its employees is not that relevant. If we were to invent a somewhat dubious common sense premise that "the less they're paid, the more mistakes they'll make", then that would only strengthen the author's notion that the G-S paper is not doing well when it comes to making errors.

**B. The Gazette-Standard has been in business considerably longer than has its biggest competitor.** — No Impact (1% of students)
This is a distinction between the two papers, but it doesn't seem to make them unfair to compare. If anything, we would probably assume that the longer a paper has been in business, the better practices it will have evolved for cutting down on errors.

So if Gazette-Standard is the longer running paper and is still running way more corrections than its competitor, it reflects negatively on Gazette-Standards ability to avoid errors.

**C. The Gazette-Standard more actively follows up reader complaints about errors in the paper than does its biggest competitor.** — Correct (81% of students)
This establishes a difference that does make the evidence feel like more of an unfair comparison.

Say that I told you that Iowa has more documented COVID cases per capita than Nebraska, and thus concluded that Nebraska was doing a better job of containing COVID.

It could be that Nebraska just doesn't do as much COVID testing as Iowa does. If you don't conduct as many tests, you won't publish as many positive cases.

Similarly, it might be that the Gazette-Standards biggest competitor has as least as many cases of factual errors, as Gazette-Standard does, but if they're not "testing" for them by following-up on reader complaints, then they won't detect as many cases of errors, and thus won't publish as many corrections.

Because the two papers have different commitment levels to finding and acknowledging factual errors, it's unfair to look at the number of corrections each paper publishes as a metric for gauging how many actual errors took place in the paper.

**D. The Gazette-Standard’s articles are each checked by more editors than are the articles of its biggest competitor.** — Strengthens, if anything (13% of students)
If the Gazette-Standard paper has more editors fact check, but still ends up publishing way more factual errors, then that reflects pretty poorly on the editorial staff, which therefore would support the author's conclusion that increasing the editorial staff is not helping.

**E. The increase in the Gazette-Standard’s editorial staff has been offset by a decrease in the reporting staff at the newspaper.** — Strengthen, if anything (4% of students)
We probably should consider anything about the reporting staff to be out of scope. But it seems like if we were to try to take this answer anywhere, a decrease in reporters would probably leave the remaining staff stretched more thin than before. When you're stretched thin, you don't have as much ability to thoroughly check your work.

So fewer reporters might lead to more factual errors, which would just strengthen the author's argument.

## S2 · Q1 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
For the first few weeks after birth, the dunnart has such poor control over its respiratory muscles that it cannot use them to breathe. Instead, this tiny marsupial breathes through its thin skin, which gradually thickens as the dunnart matures inside its mother's pouch. ### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps explain how newborn dunnarts manage to survive despite their thin skins?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Given that...:** Warm-blooded animals need thick skin throughout their lives to maintain body temperature and reduce water loss.
- **How can it be that...:** Unlike other warm-blooded animals, for the first few weeks after birth the dunnart breathes through its thin skin, which gradually thickens as the dunnart matures inside its mother's pouch.
- **Evaluate:** As with many Paradox questions, this one involves a comparison. It compares newborn dunnarts, which have thin skin for the first few weeks after birth, with other warm-blooded animals which need thick skin to survive throughout their entire lives.

  We're being asked to explain how newborn dunnarts manage to survive with such thin skin. It seems like there must be something unique about dunnarts. The stimulus does tell us that a dunnart lives inside its mother's pouch while its skin thickens. It's not too-far fetched to think that living in a pouch might help a thin-skinned newborn dunnart to maintain body temperature and reduce water loss.
- **Goal:** A correct answer will describe something unique about dunnarts which allows the newborns to survive in spite of their thin skin. Expect an answer to involve living in the mother's pouch, but keep an open mind in case the correct answer brings in some new, unexpected detail which does a good job of explaining the situation. Beware of answers that seem relevant at first glance, but don't actually explain why newborn dunnarts are able to survive.

**Answer choices**
**A. The dunnart’s respiratory muscles begin to develop a few days after birth.** — Restates Paradox (2% of students)
This answer adds an additional detail to the story, but doesn't explain how a newborn dunnart maintains body temperature and reduces water loss with an unusually thin skin.

**B. The dunnart's body temperature is higher than that of many other warm-blooded animals.** — Cheats Paradox (3% of students)
This answer is trying to imply that the higher body temperature somehow helps keep the dunnart warm. But it doesn't explain how a newborn dunnart is able to maintain its body temperature without a thicker skin. Humans have higher body temperatures than snakes, but this doesn't mean that a human can survive outdoors in freezing temperatures wearing nothing but a bathing suit. Something has to help humans and dunnarts maintain their body temperature, whatever that temperature might be.

**C. Adult dunnarts experience more heat and water loss through their skin than other adult marsupials do.** — No Impact / Thin Ice (0% of students)
This difference between adult dunnarts and other adult marsupials doesn't help explain how newborn dunnarts survive with such thin skin.

This answer might seem appealing if we assume that the heat and water lost by a dunnart mother is absorbed by the newborn in the mother's pouch. But that's assuming a significant amount of detail that isn't supported by the stimulus or this answer choice. By comparison, the correct answer doesn't require this kind of assumption.

**D. Its mother's pouch keeps a newborn dunnart warm and reduces water loss through its skin.** — Correct (95% of students)
We were already told that dunnarts live inside their mothers' pouches during the time that their skin is thickening. This answer explains how the pouch thin-skinned newborn dunnarts survive.

**E. Some dunnarts live where daytime temperatures are high and the climate is dry.** — Cheats Paradox (0% of students)
High daytime temperatures could help explain how newborn dunnarts maintain their body temperature. But what happens at night? And this answer only states that some dunnarts live in this type of climate. What about the ones that don't?

Even more significantly, an animal living in a dry climate has an even greater need to reduce water loss. This would mean an even bigger problem for newborn dunnarts. This answer might explain one small aspect of the situation, but leaves too many questions unanswered.

### Stimulus

### Prompt
The conclusion of the reviewer's argument can be properly drawn if which one of the following is assumed?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** Almost all books that offer management advice are written from the perspective of the CEO.

  Most managers aren't CEOs and don't have the same perspective as CEOs.
- **Conclusion (So):** The advice in management books is of limited use for most managers.
- **Evaluate:** Taken together, the first two sentences in the argument tell us that almost all books that offer management advice are not written from the perspective that most managers have.

  The conclusion states that the advice in these books is of limited use for most managers. Advice being "of limited use" is a new idea in the conclusion. It's different from the ideas that were mentioned in the evidence. The evidence describes this advice as not being written from the perspective that most managers have.

  For the conclusion to be properly drawn from the evidence, we want the evidence to directly connect to the conclusion. We can do this with statement that says, "if advice is not written from the perspective that most managers have, then that advice is of limited use for most managers."
```
~managers' perspective → of limited use
```
- **Goal:** Look for an answer which matches the statement above, connecting the evidence to the conclusion. Since this is a conditional statement, the contrapositive would also be correct:

  "If advice is of more than just limited use for most managers then it will be written from the perspective that most managers have."
```
~of limited use → managers' perspective
```
  Beware of answers which present illegal reversals or illegal negations of this statement and answers which fail to properly connect the evidence to the conclusion.

**Answer choices**
**A. Advice books rarely take the perspective of their intended audience.** — Premise Support / Unrelated to Goal (2% of students)
The evidence already states that books offering management advice aren't written from the perspective that most managers have. This answer just expands on that idea, describing it as a common feature of advice books. This answer doesn't connect the evidence to the claim in the conclusion that these types of books are of limited use to managers.

**B. Most people who read management advice books aspire to be CEOs.** — Opposite (if anything) / Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
If anything, this answer could support the idea that the advice offered in management books might be quite useful for managers. The books aren't written from a managers perspective, but they could still be very useful in helping managers achieve their future career goals. If we view the answer from this perspective then it weakens the argument, which is the opposite of what we're trying to do.

**C. Almost all CEOs have experience as lower level managers.** — Opposite (if anything) / Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
If anything, this answer could support the idea that the advice offered in management books might be quite useful for managers: even though the books are written from a CEOs perspective, this answer suggests that most CEOs have also experienced a manager's perspective. If we view the answer from this perspective then it weakens the argument, which is the opposite of what we're trying to do.

**D. Advice is of limited use unless it is offered from the perspective of the recipient.** — Correct (93% of students)
```
~recipient's perspective → of limited use
```
The word "unless" is a conditional logic indicator that translates to "if not." We can reword this answer as "if advice is not offered from the perspective of the recipient then it is of limited use." This applies to our argument, where the managers are the intended recipients. This answer connects the evidence in the argument to the conclusion.

**E. Most managers prefer to read books that they think will be useful to them in their work.** — Out of Scope (3% of students)
Neither the evidence nor the conclusion involves managers' preferred reading material. What they "prefer" to read is beyond the scope of this argument. ## S2 · Q13 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A six-month public health campaign sought to limit the spread of influenza by encouraging people to take precautions such as washing their hands frequently and avoiding public places when they experience influenza symptoms. Since the incidence of influenza was much lower during those months than experts had predicted, the public evidently heeded the campaign.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The public evidently heeded the campaign (translation: the public washed their hands frequently and avoided public places when they were experiencing flu symptoms).
- **Evidence (since):** The incidence of flu was much lower during the six months of the campaign than experts had predicted.
- **Evaluate:** This argument as a common structure:
  - This policy / this campaign / this plan went into effect
  - Since then / during then, we observed X.
  - Thus, it's clear that X resulted from that policy/plan/campaign.

  These all belong to the broader category of Explain a Curious Fact.

  Curious Fact ... why was the incidence of flu much lower than we predicted it would be during those 6 months?

  Author's Explanation ... people heeded the campaign -> they started washing their hands more frequently / avoiding public places when they had flu symptoms -> this contributed to lower spread of the flu than there would have been otherwise

  We need to consider
  1. Alternate Explanations for the Curious Fact
  2. the Plausibility of the Author's Explanation

  What could be a different reason why the rate of flu was lower than expected? Maybe the predictions were off for some reason. Maybe there were fewer visitors (i.e. germ vectors) to the area than normal during those months. Maybe most workplaces installed better air filtration systems during those months (which helps to cut down on contagious people infecting others in the workplace).

  How would we assess the plausibility of the author's explanation? We would just want any evidence we can get that people were aware of the campaign and started changing their behavior because of it (washed their hands more than they usually would / avoided public places when they felt flu symptoms more than they usually would).
- **Goal:** Since this is Strengthen, we either need an answer to rule out a different explanation for why the rate of flu was lower than expected, or to boost the plausibility of the story that people started washing their hands and staying home when sick more than before.

**Answer choices**
**A. The incidence of food-borne illnesses, which can be effectively controlled by frequent hand washing, was markedly lower than usual during the six-month period.** — Correct (78% of students)
This adds plausibility to the story that people were washing their hands / staying home when sick more than usual.

If they were washing their hands more than usual, then we would expect to see other things connected to hand-washing (such as food-borne illnesses) affected. This answer corroborates that rates of food-borne illnesses were very different during this six-months.

Another correct answer performing the same function could have said,
"Sales of hand moisturizers, which are usually used when people are frequently washing their hands, was markedly higher than usual during the six-month period".

**B. During the six-month period, the incidence of the common cold, which has many of the same symptoms as influenza, was about the same as usual.** — Weakens, if anything (3% of students)
If people were really heeding this campaign's advice, they would stay home when they thought they might have the flu.

But since the cold has many of the same symptoms, there would be a lot people staying home with a cold (thinking they are staying home with the flu).

If people were increasingly staying home when they had a cold, then transmission of colds should be lower than usual. But this answer is saying transmission of colds was just as high as ever.

So that somewhat weakens the plausibility of the story that people heeded this campaign and stayed home when they experienced cold/flu symptoms.

**C. There were fewer large public gatherings than usual during the six-month period.** — Weakens (12% of students)
This provides an Alternate Explanation for why the rate of flu was lower than usual.

It wasn't because people heeded the advice of this public health campaign. There just weren't as many super-spreader events as there typically would be during a flu season.

**D. Independently of the public health campaign, the news media spread the message that one's risk of contracting influenza can be lessened by frequent hand washing.** — Weakens (4% of students)
This provides an Alternate Explanation for why the rate of flu was lower than usual.

It wasn't because people heeded the advice of this public health campaign. It was because they heeded the advice of their preferred news media (cable news / newspapers / twitter / etc.).

**E. In a survey completed before the campaign began, many people admitted that they should do more to limit the spread of influenza.** — Weaker than Correct Answer (3% of students)
This somewhat strengthens the plausibility of the author's story, because it demonstrates that there were many people who were potentially willing to do more to limit the spread of the flu.

We don't know specifically whether they were willing to wash their hands more or to more often stay home when feeling sick. So, the connection to the campaign's specific actions is very weak.

And the quantifier many is not impressive. If there were even 10 people on this survey who said they should do more, we can say "many people said they'd do more".

## S2 · Q14 — Main Conclusion

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A study at a company found that most meetings showed diminishing returns after 30 minutes, and little could be expected after 60 minutes. Moreover, the most productive meetings were those for which a clear time frame was established. For a meeting at the company to achieve maximum productivity, then, it needs to have a clear time frame and be no more than 30 minutes long.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately expresses the conclusion drawn in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** For a meeting to achieve max productivity, it should have a clear time frame and be no more than 30 mins long.
- **Evidence:** According to a study, for most meetings ...
  - after 30 min, diminishing returns, and by 60 mins pretty much worthless.
  - most productive meetings were ones with a clearly established time frame.
- **Evaluate:** When we do Main Conclusion questions, we know that the conclusion will almost never be in the final sentence. (It's usually the First Sentence or a But/Yet/However rebuttal in the middle)

  However, LSAC hears us test prep companies shouting that pattern from the hilltops, so on some recent tests they've been trying to vary it up a little.

  The one keyword we have available to help us here is the word "then". It's a very infrequently used Conclusion keyword, but it functions much like a "then" in a "if / then" conditional statement.

  All these authors are implicitly thinking, "IF my evidence is true, THEN it proves my conclusion".

  "IF we only get diminishing returns after 30 minutes and IF the most productive meeting have a clear time frame, THEN the most productive meetings would have a clear time frame and go no longer than 30 minutes."

  We always check our conclusions with the Support test. If you think Claim X might be the conclusion, then ask yourself Why should I believe that? and make sure that the argument provides at least one explicit premise to support Claim X.
- **Goal:** Once we've properly located a conclusion, all the hard stuff is usually done. Now we just go find a meaning match in the answers. So we just want the best meaning match for the final sentence.

**Answer choices**
**A. In general, a meeting at the company that is no more than 30 minutes long and has a clear time frame will achieve maximum productivity.** — Reversal (27% of students)
It's weird to be dealing with this issue on Main Conclusion, but it's definitely no accident that this incredibly tempting answer was placed in spot (A).

This sounds an awful lot like the conclusion, but the conclusion presented "clear time frame" and "30 min limit" as requirements of a maximally productive meeting (it needs to have X and Y), not as a guarantee of a maximally productive meeting.

Last sentence:
maximally productive meeting → Clear Timeframe and < 30min

This answer:
Clear Timeframe and < 30min → maximally productive meeting

**B. Most meetings at the company show diminishing returns after 30 minutes, according to a study.** — Premise (1% of students)
The phrase "according to a study" will pretty much guarantee something can't be a Conclusion, because it will just be some background or supporting fact, not a supported Author opinion.

If we were tempted by this and asked
why should we believe that
"according to a study, most meetings showed diminishing returns after 30 mins"
we wouldn't be able to point at either of the remaining ideas in the paragraph as support for that claim.

**C. A meeting at the company will be maximally productive only if it has a clear time frame and lasts no more than 30 minutes.** — Correct (70% of students)
For a meeting to be Z, it needs to have Y and Z
is equivalent to
A meeting can be Z only if it has Y and Z

They both would diagram like this,
Z → Y and Z

If we applied the Support Test to this answer and asked,
why should we believe that
"meeting will be max productivity only if they have clear time frame and stay 30 mins or less"
we could point to the other two ideas as support:

They'll only be maximally productive if they have those two qualities because a study found that the most productive meetings had a clear time frame and that most meeting showed diminishing returns when they went longer than 30 mins.

**D. According to a study, meetings at the company were the most productive when they had clear time frames.** — Premise (2% of students)
Anything prefaced by "moreover" is guaranteed to be another Supporting point, not a Conclusion.

**E. A study of meetings at the company says that little productivity should be expected after the 60-minute mark.** — Premise (0% of students)
This is just the second half of the first sentence. The findings of a study will never be a Conclusion. What we can takeaway from those findings will be a conclusion, and that's our last sentence.

## S2 · Q15 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Nutritionist: Most fad diets prescribe a single narrow range of nutrients for everyone. But because different foods contain nutrients that are helpful for treating or preventing different health problems, dietary needs vary widely from person to person. However, everyone should eat plenty of fruits and vegetables, which protect against a wide range of health problems.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the nutritionist's statements?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:** Most fad diets - narrow range of nutrients.
  but
  People: vary widely in terms of what nutrients they need.

  (Reconcile what's before/after the pivot ... Infer: most fad diets work work better for some people than for others, since the narrow range of nutrients they prescribe would better match some people's dietary needs than someone else's dietary needs)

  Everyone needs fruits and veggies, which help ward off health problems.

  (infer: maybe it would be good for most fad diets to include fruits and veggies in their range of nutrients suggested, since that's a type of nutrient that doesn't vary widely from person to person. Everyone benefits from those.)
- **Goal:** Find a safely worded answer, possibly reinforcing the idea that most fad diets are going to work better for some than others, or reinforcing the idea that if you're gonna prescribe a narrow range of nutrients, fruits and veggies would be a good way to go, since everybody needs those.

**Answer choices**
**A. Most fad diets require that everyone following them eat plenty of fruits and vegetables.** — Unsupported: which nutrients are prescribed (4% of students)
While we have reason to think it would make good sense for most fad diets to require fruits and veggies, we have no evidence that this is what they currently are recommending.

**B. Fruits and vegetables are the only foods that contain enough different nutrients to protect against a wide range of health problems.** — Too Strong: the only / Only One Mentioned ≠ Only One (8% of students)
Just because "fruits and veggies" are the only type of food mentioned that protects against a wide range of health problems doesn't mean we're allowed to infer that "fruits and veggies" are the only type of food.

**C. Any two people have different health problems and thus different dietary needs.** — Too Strong: all people are unique (17% of students)
This is enticing people into a mis-read of "dietary needs vary widely from person to person", which does not mean that every person has a unique set of dietary needs, it just means that there's a high variance within the test.

If I proctored a practice LSAT for 100 students and said "the scores varied widely from person to person", that wouldn't mean that "any two people had different LSAT scores".

**D. Most fad diets fail to satisfy the dietary needs of some people.** — Correct (68% of students)
This is derivable from the first two claims. It's a "Reconcile the Pivot" sort of inference, synthesizing the ideas of
Most fad diets = narrow range of nutrients
People = vary widely in nutrients needed

If we said "most fad LSAT programs cater to a very narrow part of the test" and "people vary widely in terms of what part of the test they need help with most", then we could infer that "most fad LSAT programs fail to satisfy the needs some LSAT students".

Remember, "some" just means "at least one", so contradicting this claim would mean we think it's possible that "most fad diets successfully satisfy the needs of 100% of people".

**E. There are very few if any nutrients that are contained in every food other than fruits and vegetables.** — Too Strong: very few, if any (3% of students)
This is making a strong, out of scope statement. It's saying there are very few nutrients that would be contained in every non-fruit / non-veggie food.

I think they're trying to entice people into thinking, "if dietary needs vary widely from person to person, then there must not be many common denominator ingredients."

We just don't know. If we supposed there were 100 nutrients out there, and all non-fruit, non-veggie foods have nutrients 1 thru 20, that would invalidate this answer but it would still allow the paragraph to be true. Foods could vary a lot in terms of which of the remaining 80 nutrients they have or don't have, and people could vary a lot in terms of which of those remaining 80 nutrients they need or don't need.

In other words, it's possible for there to be many common denominator nutrients, while there is still a lot variety to other nutrients, and perhaps people are relatively similar when it comes to these common denominator nutrients but are more widely divergent when it comes to these more intermittent nutrients.

## S2 · Q16 — Evaluate

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
The caffeine in coffee stimulates the production of irritating acid in the stomach. But darker roasts of coffee, produced by roasting the coffee beans longer, contain more N-methylpyridinium (NMP) than lighter roasts, and NMP tends to suppress production of acid in the stomach. Therefore if you drink caffeinated coffee, darker roasts will irritate your stomach less than lighter roasts.

### Prompt
The answer to which one of the following questions most helps in evaluating the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If you drink caffeinated coffee, darker roasts will irritate your stomach less than lighter roasts.
- **Evidence:** Caffeine stimulates the irritating acid in the stomach. But darker roasts have more NMP than lighter roasts, and NMP tends to suppress acid production.
- **Evaluate:** Given that darker roasts have more NMP, and NMP suppresses the irritating acid in your stomach, how could we still argue that lighter roasts are easier on your stomach?

  There would to have to be something else different between darker and lighter that tips the scales back in favor of lighter roasts.

  This argument is kind of like a Part vs. Whole argument. Since one part of darker roasts (the higher NMP) is better on your stomach, the author assumes/concludes that darker roasts overall are better on your stomach.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that addresses a potential difference between darker and lighter roasts, so that we might be able to argue that lighter roasts are actually easier on your stomach.

**Answer choices**
**A. Does extending the roasting time of coffee beans increase the amount of caffeine present in the brewed coffee?** — Correct (54% of students)
This points to a potential difference between darker and lighter. Since we were told already that darker is roasted longer, if roasting time increases the amount of caffeine, then darker might have more caffeine.

We were told that caffeine is what stimulates the irritating acids. So even if darker has more NMP (which suppress the acids), it might also have more caffeine, creating more acid in the first place.

This gives us our potential weakener. We can argue that even if darker roasts have more acid-quelling NMP, lighter roasts are actually easier on the stomach because they have less acid-producing caffeine to begin with.

(p.s. as a coffee lover, I happen to know that lighter roasts are more caffeinated, so if anyone else had that outside knowledge, this objection can feel counterintuitive, since we know the actual answer is No. But since a Yes answer would weaken, it's an important question to be resolved)

**B. Does a reduction in acid production in the stomach have an adverse effect on stomach function?** — Unclear Impact (25% of students)
If the answer to this question is YES, then darker roasts, which have more NMP, which suppresses acid production, might have some adverse effect on stomach function.

Could that be our way to argue that lighter roasts will irritate the stomach less?

Not quite. An adverse effect on stomach function is too broad to be reliably about stomach irritation. It might mean that when acids are reduced, the speed of digestion and metabolism is slowed down. But all we care about is the truth value of the conclusion, which is exclusively about stomach irritiation.

If the conclusion were, "thus, darker roasts are better for your stomach", then the discussion would be broad enough that adverse stomach function would be relevant. But since we only care about irritation, it's not clear whether this question would be addressing that.

**C. Would coffee drinkers who drink caffeinated coffee increase their coffee consumption if the coffee they drank contained less caffeine?** — Out of Scope (2% of students)
We're comparing caffeinated dark roasts to caffeinated light roasts.

This answer wants to compare the quantity of higher-caffeinated coffee consumed to the quantity of lower-caffeinated coffee consumed.

We have no idea if or how darker and lighter roasts differ in terms of caffeine, so a question about more caffeine vs. less caffeine isn't clearly going to tell us anything about the potential difference between caffeinated dark and caffeinated light.

**D. Do some coffee drinkers who switch from lighter to darker roasts of coffee increase their daily coffee consumption?** — Too Weak / Irrelevant (12% of students)
First of all, the lead-in "if you drink caffeinated coffee, darker > lighter" implicitly means for a given quantity of coffee, darker > lighter.

It's not saying "dark coffee drinkers have less stomach irritation than light coffee drinkers", where we might find it relevant how much each type of coffee drinker is consuming.

So whether someone switching from lighter to darker would increase their coffee consumption is not our concern. We're only attempting to judge "when it comes to caffeinated coffee, which would irritate your stomach more: x mL of darker roast or x mL of lighter roast?"

Finally, even if we were trying to use the YES answer as an objection, it's only got the strength of "some". If the answer is YES, then at least one person who switched from lighter to darker increased their coffee consumption by at least 1%. Big whoop.

**E. Do lighter roasts of coffee have any important health benefits that darker roasts of coffee lack?** — Out of Scope: health benefits (7% of students)
The conclusion wasn't a global evaluation of "which is better for you: darker or lighter?" It is narrowly about "which causes more stomach irritation: darker or lighter?"

Even if we knew that lighter roasts had important health benefits that darker roasts lack, that wouldn't help us to argue that lighter roasts actually cause less irritation (we'd have to also know that these important health benefits include reducing acid irritation in your stomach).

## S2 · Q17 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Film historians often find it difficult to determine typical audience members' responses to particular films, especially those from the early twentieth century. Box office figures help little, for they indicate only a film's financial success or failure; they do not show what audiences found funny, or frightening, or moving. These historians also find that newspaper and magazine reviews fail to provide much insight.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the statements above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:** It's hard for film historians to figure out typical audience reactions to certain films, especially from the early 20th century.

  Newspaper and magazine reviews fail to provide much insight.

  So do box office figures. They just show whether a film was a financial success or failure. They don't show what audiences found funny, frightening, or moving.
- **Evaluate:** Did we see any Conditional, Causal, Mathematical, or Pivot language that would suggest which ideas to combine?

  The closest we have is some anti-causal wording.

  Box office figures don't help us figure out the typical audience reaction to films.
  Newspaper and magazines reviews don't provide much insight into typical audience reaction to films.

  We could potentially derive statements that reinforce that anti-causal relationship:
  - newspaper and magazine reviews don't usually mention the typical audience reaction, and they can't be trusted to reflect the typical audience reaction.

  - box office figures don't indicate typical audience reactions
  - financial success or failure doesn't reveal typical audience response

  The middle sentence seems to have some utility. If we say,
  "A doesn't help determine X. It only shows Y, not Z"
  then we seem to be implying that
  "Y does not help us determine X"
  and that "Z is part of determining X".

  (we already covered "Y doesn't help us determine X" when we said that "financial success or failure doesn't help us determine typical audience reaction")

  (Z is part of determining X)
  - what audiences found funny, frightening, or moving is part of determining the "typical audience response".
- **Goal:** Hard to know exactly what they're going for because there are several ways to spell out what does and doesn't help us determine typical audience reactions.

**Answer choices**
**A. Newspaper and magazine reviews of films are usually written in advance of a film's general release.** — Too Strong: usually / Out of Scope: in advance (2% of students)
It's definitely supported that newspaper and magazine reviews didn't reveal typical audience reactions, but that doesn't mean it's because they were usually written in advance.

Maybe they were usually written once the film had been released, but the opinion of a movie reviewer just isn't influenced by or representative of the typical audience opinion.

**B. Typical audience members' responses to films from the latter part of the twentieth century are easy to determine.** — Out of Scope: late 20th century / Too Strong: easy (1% of students)
The passage stressed that films from the early 20th century were especially difficult, but that can mean that films from the latter half of the 20th century were just regular ol' difficult.

We can't categorically say that typical audience reactions to every film from 1950-2000 is easy to determine.

**C. The box office success of a film does not depend on its viewers finding it funny, frightening, or moving.** — That vs. What (29% of students)
It doesn't get any more lawyerly that parsing between the meaning of these two ideas:

1. Box office success doesn't show that audiences found it funny, frightening, or moving

2. Box office success doesn't show what audiences found it funny, frightening, or moving

This answer choice is talking about #1. We can't derive that from the passage. It may be true that the box office success of a film, its financial success, convinces us that audiences did find that film funny, or frightening, or moving.

(i.e. it must have stirred some sort of emotion if that many people came to see it)

But the passage only talked about #2. We might know that a film was massively popular, but not know why.

When we're talking about trying to ascertain typical audience responses, it would be granular stuff like "What parts of the film were funny to them? What was frightening? What was moving?"

The box office success may depend on a movie fulfilling at least one of those categories (funny / frightening / moving), but that wouldn't instruct historians about which specific parts of the movie typical audiences found funny, scary, or moving.

**D. Film historians do not believe that film reviews in newspapers and magazines reveal typical film audience members' views.** — Correct (67% of students)
Suppose we say that "Ben is finding it difficult to find the recipe for his mom's old fudge recipe. His sister Grace failed to provide much insight".

We could infer that "Ben does not believe that Grace has adequately conveyed to him his mom's old fudge recipe".

That's all we're doing to derive this answer:
Film historians can't figure out typical audience members' responses.
Newspaper and magazines fail to provide much insight.
So, newspaper and magazines to not adequately convey typical audience members' responses.

**E. Films from the early part of the twentieth century were not usually reviewed in newsspapers or magazines.** — Too Strong: usually not reviewed (1% of students)
This is just like choice (A), speculating a reason why newspapers and magazines failed to provide much insight into typical audience reactions.

(A) said, "It's cuz the reviews come out before the film is released."
(E) says, "It's cuz most films weren't reviewed in newspapers or magazines."

We don't know if either of those are the reason why. It could be that films were always reviewed, after the film had been released, and historians just don't think that the review of a critic is representative of the typical audience members' reactions.

## S2 · Q18 — Role

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
The consensus among astronomers, based upon observations of the surfaces of pulsars, is that pulsars are spinning balls of neutrons compressed into a sphere some 10 kilometers in diameter with a mass roughly equal to that of our sun. However, their observed properties are also consistent with some pulsars actually being filled with quarks, the building blocks of neutrons. Because the core of a quark-filled pulsar, unlike a neutron-filled one, would have an overall positive charge, it would attract a layer of negatively charged particles that could support a crust of neutrons.

### Prompt
The statement that the core of a quark-filled pulsar would have an overall positive charge plays which one of the following roles in the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The observed properties of pulsars are consistent with the possibility that some pulsars are filled with quarks.
- **Evidence:** Based on observations, astronomers think they are balls of neutrons. But if they were filled with quarks, they would have an overall positive charge, which would attract a layer of negatively charged particles that could support a crust of neutrons.
- **Evaluate:** This argument is tricky to understand at first. From what astronomers can observe, they think pulsars are balls of neutrons. The author is suggesting an alternate interpretation (she's not insisting on it, just saying that it's also a possible interpretation).

  Her alternate interpretation is essentially, "Sure, sure, we can tell the outer layer is neutrons. But maybe it's not neutrons all the way through. Maybe the inside is actually quarks. If pulsars had a quark-filled center, it's plausible that they would end up covered by a crust of neutrons and so outwardly it would look like a ball of neutrons."

  We're being asked about the claim "the core of a quark-filled pulsar would have a positive charge". That claim was part of the author's attempt to show us how a pulsar filled with quarks would still end up resembling a neutron ball. She was saying, the core of quarks would give a positive charge, which would attract a layer of negatively charged particles, which could support an outer crust of neutrons.

  When we see the word Because preface the claim we're asked about, it means that the claim is either a supporting premise for the other clause of that sentence. Ot it means that the claim describes a causal factor that results in the other clause of that sentence.

  In this case, it seems to make more sense to interpret this as a Causal Because (as opposed to a Reasoning Because).
- **Goal:** Look for an answer saying narrowly that this claim helps to explain why a quark-filled pulsar could potentially have an outer crust of neutrons.

  Or look for an answer more broadly saying, "this is part of how the author defends that a quark-filled pulsar would ultimately resemble, from the outside, a ball of neutrons, which shows why the quark-filled pulsar is compatible with observed outer properties".

**Answer choices**
**A. It helps explain how pulsars could have neutrons on their surface even if they were not entirely made up of neutrons.** — Correct (73% of students)
This seems descriptively true. That final sentence is showing us how a quark-filled pulsar would still look from the outside like a ball of neutrons, because the outer crust would still be covered in neutrons.

**B. It forms part of a challenge to the claim that some pulsars may be made up of quarks.** — Opposite (6% of students)
The author isn't challenging the idea that some pulsars may be made up of quarks. The author is selling us on the idea that pulsars may be made up of quarks.

**C. It helps explain why some pulsars would not be readily recognized as such by astronomers.** — Out of Scope: not readily recognized (4% of students)
This argument is never saying that sometimes a pulsar is observed by astronomers but they don't realize they're looking at a pulsar.

This argument is saying, when astronomers observe a pulsar, they see that it's all neutrons on the outside, and so they assume it's just a big ball of neutrons, when it's possible that it's got something different inside (like quarks) and is just covered with an exterior shell of neutrons.

**D. It presents a new finding that challenges the consensus view of the structure of pulsars.** — Out of Scope: new finding (15% of students)
There are no new findings here. The author is just looking at the same observational data and saying, "Are we being hasty in assuming the entire pulsar is made up of neutrons? Maybe it's just the outer shell. Here's a causal story that seems possible, wherein the center is made up of quarks but then the positive charge of the quark-core gives way to an outer shell of neutrons."

**E. It points out a problem with the view that pulsars have a mass roughly equal to that of our sun.** — Wrong Role (2% of students)
At no point is the author pushing back on the theory that pulsars have a mass roughly equivalent to that of our sun.

The author is only pushing back on the idea that a pulsar is entirely made of neutrons.

## S2 · Q19 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Analyst: Any new natural-gas-powered electrical generation station needs to be located close to a natural-gas pipeline, a large body of water for cooling, and transmission lines. It also must be situated in a region where residents will not oppose construction. Our country has an extensive system of transmission lines, but our natural-gas pipelines run in the vicinity of only three of our large bodies of water, and residents would oppose any significant construction projects near these bodies of water.

### Prompt
The analyst's statements if true, most strongly support which one of the following statements about the analyst's country?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:** conditional:
```
close to gas pipeline,
large body water,
new natural gas   and transmission lines
power station   → and
                  in a region where residents
                  won't oppose construction
```
  facts:
  We've got the transmission lines.
  But, we only have transmission lines near three bodies of water, and residents would oppose construction in all those locations.
- **Evaluate:** When we're given a Conditional rule, we're often expected to make an inference based on facts that trigger the contrapositive.

  If we're lacking any of those 4 required ingredients, then we can't construct any new natural-gas powered stations.

  It sounds like we're lacking some of those ingredients, since the places that have 3 of the 4 are lacking the last thing, cooperative residents.
- **Goal:** They seem to want us to say, "We're screwed"? "Can't build any new stations?"

**Answer choices**
**A. Future electrical needs will have to be met by alternatives to natural-gas-powered generation.** — Too Strong: have to be met (14% of students)
This captures the "we're screwed" sentiment, but it goes too far. (It is no accident that it is very similar to what we were looking for, but wrong, and placed in the position of answer choice A. There are a lot of Inference questions with a very tempting trap answer A.)

If we say "we're screwed; we can't build any new natural-gas powered stations", that doesn't allow us to infer that in the future we will have to meet our energy needs with non-gas alternatives.

A) Do we already have existing natural gas stations? If so, then we could still meet some of our future energy needs with natural gas. We're just saying we're screwed when it comes to building new stations.

B) Are we already needing more energy than those existing natural gas stations can provide? If not, then future energy needs could continue to be met with our existing natural gas stations.

For multiple reasons, this harshly worded prediction does not need to be true.

**B. If a new natural-gas-powered electrical station is built in a region, many residents will move away from that region.** — Out of Scope: move away (1% of students)
We only know that residents near three large bodies of water would oppose construction. We wouldn't even be able to say that many of THEM would move way. We just know they would oppose.

**C. No site would be suitable for constructing a natural-gas-powered electrical station unless the existing system of natural-gas pipelines is expanded.** — Correct (63% of students)
Unless = if not, so we can think about this as,
if the existing system of pipelines is NOT expanded, then no site for a new station is suitable.

This also resonates with our "We're screwed" prediction, but why is it bringing up "if we don't expand our existing system"?

Well, it turns out we weren't as hopeless as we may have thought in a first read of the stimulus. We knew we needed four things:
- transmission lines / pipeline / large body of water nearby
- cooperative residents

We thought we were screwed, because currently, the only place where we can get pipeline and large body of water to be near each other is in places where residents would oppose.

We can't change how residents near these three large bodies of water would feel. We can't invent new large bodies of water, but there might be other large bodies of water that already exist.

The language was tricky: our pipelines run in the vicinity of only three of our large bodies.
That's not saying "our pipelines run in the vicinity of our only three large bodies of water."

Only three ≠ conditional. It's like me whining, "Mom you only gave me three pancakes?" It's using "only" as an adverb that means a paltry, meager amount.

It's possible that our country has many other large bodies of water. We already have an extensive system of transmission lines. If we expanded our pipelines so that a pipeline was near one of these other large bodies of water, then we might have cooperative residents (or no residents) nearby.

So there is still a CHANCE that we could find a suitable site to build a new station.

But, as this answer says, if we're stuck with the existing pipelines, then we're screwed.

**D. There currently is no natural-gas-powered electrical generation station near any of the three largest bodies of water.** — Out of Scope: current stations / Out of Scope: largest bodies (19% of students)
We only got information about building new stations. We don't know anything about existing stations. And we only got information about three of our large bodies of water. We don't know anything about "the three largest bodies of water".

**E. Many residents who would oppose the construction of a new natural-gas-powered electrical station in their region would not oppose the construction of new transmission lines there.** — Unsupported: speculating opinion / Only One Mentioned ≠ Only One (4% of students)
We only know that residents in these three areas would oppose building a natural gas station.

The fact that the passage only mentioned their opposition to natural gas stations doesn't tell us that natural gas stations are the only thing they oppose.

## S2 · Q20 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Voting records regularly show that people over 65 vote in the highest percentages while young adults are least likely to vote. This indicates that citizens are becoming increasingly disconnected from the political system with each passing generation.

### Prompt
The argument's reasoning is questionable in that the argument

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (this indicates that):** Citizens are becoming increasingly disconnected from the political system with each passing generation.
- **Evidence:** People over 65 vote in the highest percentages while young adults are least likely to vote.
- **Evaluate:** The author's evidence presents a Curious Comparison -- why is it that people over 65 have the highest percentage of voting whereas young adults have the lowest percentage of voting?

  His conclusion assumes one possible Causal Explanation -- each new generation is less connected to the political system, so they don't feel as strong an urge to vote.

  We always react to this Curious Fact / Explanation type of argument with the same two pressure points:
  - Alternative Explanations for the curious fact (how else could we explain why higher percentages of older people than younger people vote?)

  - Plausibility of the Author's Explanation (do we have any other evidence that today's generation is less connected to the political system than previous generations were?)

  When we see Curious Fact / Explanation arguments on Flaw, the correct answer is almost always about Alternate Explanations.

  A different way to explain the voting difference between old and young might just be that the old people are retired and can watch cable news all day and have no problem voting on random Tuesdays, whereas young people are busy and have to go to work or have enough of a social life that they don't pay any attention to politics.

  The conclusion isn't saying "as you get older, you get more politically engaged". It's saying, "The Baby Boomers were more politically engaged than Gen X. Gen X is more engaged then Millennials. Millennials are more engaged than Gen Z."
- **Goal:** Look for an answer complaining that the author failed to consider some alternate explanation for the difference in voting habits.

**Answer choices**
**A. compares an early stage of one generation to a later stage of another** — Correct (53% of students)
An alternate explanation for the schism in voting behavior is simply that people get more politically active as they get older. It's not a generational thing; it's just an aging thing.

Maybe 80% of Baby Boomers vote, while only 30% of Gen Z votes, but if you were to rewind the clock 50 years, when the Baby Boomers were in their early 20s, you'd find that only 30% of Baby Boomers voted back when they were in their early 20s.

This answer choice is tiptoeing around that objection. It's saying, "It's not fair for you to compare how the Boomers vote in their 70s to how Gen Z votes in their 20s. If you're trying to compare the political engagement of one generation vs. that of another, you need to make a fairer comparison: what % of Boomers voted when they were in their 20s vs. what % of Gen Z votes right now in their 20s."

Otherwise, it's hard to tell whether the difference in voting is because different generations had different political values or just because as we get older we're more likely to have the time and interest to vote.

**B. fails to take into account the relative sizes of the generations compared** — % vs. # (16% of students)
The relative size of the generations is irrelevant. It doesn't make a difference which generation is more populous, because we're only talking about the percentage of people from each generation who vote.

**C. provides evidence for a phenomenon without providing an explanation of the phenomenon** — Not a Flaw (4% of students)
It's not objectionable to provide evidence for a phenomenon without providing an explanation of the phenomenon.

I might present evidence that "my mom really loves Justin Bieber" by pointing out she has all his albums, and has been to his concerts, and has an ankle tattoo that says Belieber.

With this evidence, I have built a strong case that my mom loves Justin Bieber. For me to be right, I don't need to also explain why she likes him.

**D. confuses the cause of an effect with the effect itself** — Not an Objection / Always Wrong (7% of students)
There is a correlation in the evidence between being in an older generation and voting more. The author concludes that being in this older generation causes its members to vote more.

This answer choice is saying the author failed to consider that "voting more is actually causing them to be in this generation". That's nonsensical, of course.

SIDE NOTE:
When authors present a correlation between X and Y and conclude that X causes Y, we want to raise the possibility that Y might cause X.

But we can never say with surety that Y causes X. So whenever we see a Flaw answer choice saying, with certainty, that "the author confused cause and effect" it's wrong.

If they say it's possible the author confused cause and effect, it could still be correct.

**E. overlooks the possibility that voting patterns among age groups will change in the future** — Not an Objection (19% of students)
Does it weaken the argument to say,
"Hey, author, voting patterns among age groups will change in the future"?

Nope, because the author has already committed himself to that very idea.

He thinks that each passing generation is increasingly disconnected (and thus will vote in lower percentages).

So when Gen Z reaches their 30s, and now the Remote Learning generation is in their 20s, this author expects people in the age group of 20-29 to vote at lower rates than they did when Gen Z was filling up the 20-29 age group.

Our objection was "what if voting habits change as you age", not "what if voting patterns for a given age group change in the future".

This is definitely requiring some flexible rephrasing of the original argument, and some charitable interpretation of ingredients to make them line up, but it's ultimately the best match we've got.

## S2 · Q22 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
In a recent study, one group of participants watched video recordings of themselves running on treadmills, and a second group watched recordings of other people running on treadmills. When contacted later, participants in the first group reported exercising, on average, 1 hour longer each day than did the other participants. This shows that watching a recording of yourself exercising can motivate you to exercise more.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Watching a recording of yourself exercising can motivate you to exercise more.
- **Evidence:** In a study, the people who watched a recording of themselves running on a treadmill reported exercising, on average, 1 more hour per day than the people who instead watched other people run on a treadmill.
- **Evaluate:** This is a typical Curious Fact / Explanation kind of setup.
  Curious Fact: How come the ones who saw themselves in the video report exercising more than 1 hr more than those who saw other people exercising?

  Author's Explanation: Watching yourself on video exercising motivates you to exercise more, so these people who watched themselves during the study had new motivation to exercise, increased their exercise regimen, and now average 1 hr more of exercise.

  You'll notice I'm filling in a lot of blanks for the Author. That's a good habit when we're trying to understand the Causal story that they're selling. We always react to Curious Fact arguments with the same two questions:

  How ELSE could we explain the Curious Fact?
  What could help us assess the plausibility of the Author's Story?

  Most correct answers that weaken a Causal argument provide a different way to explain the background data. Why else could the group that watched themselves be exercising 1 hour more? Maybe that group was just already exercising 1 hour more on average. The language of the argument doesn't say that they've increased their amount of exercise. Maybe that group did increase their exercise, but not as the result of this experiment. Maybe a disproportionate number of that group recently hired personal trainers, and that is the reason why they're exercising 1 hour more. Maybe they live in a climate that's more conducive to exercise.

  If we wanted to weaken the plausibility, we could say stuff like, "People are so mortified by how they look when exercising that it makes them want to exercise less" / "in another study, people who exercised in front of a mirror exercised less than people in front of a wall"
- **Goal:** Pick an answer that gives us a different way to explain the Curious "1 hr extra" Fact,
  or an answer that undermines the story that watching yourself exercise motivates you to exercise more.

**Answer choices**
**A. In another study, people who watched recordings of themselves lifting weights exercised for more time each day than did people who watched recordings of themselves running.** — Irrelevant Comparison: lifting vs. running (2% of students)
We care about the distinction between watching yourself exercise and not-watching yourself, but this answer would help us evaluate the difference between watching yourself lift vs. watching yourself run.

We're not interested in that distinction.

**B. Another study's members exhibited an increased willingness to give to charity after hearing stories in which people with whom they identified did so.** — Weakly Strengthens (4% of students)
This is too distant a situation to have much impact, but it actually seems to strengthen the plausibility that seeing / hearing about yourself (or someone you identity with) doing X leads to an increased motivation to do X yourself.

**C. Participants who were already highly motivated to exercise did not report exercising for any longer each day than they had before the study.** — No Impact (42% of students)
Since we don't know how highly motivated participants were initially divided up into the experimental groups, learning about what highly motivated participants are / aren't doing doesn't help us clarify anything about the possible differences between the two groups we care about: those who watched themselves and those who watched others.

**D. In studies of identical twins, participants who observed their twin read overreported by a significant amount how much time they themselves spent reading in the days that followed.** — Correct (49% of students)
Oh, hello, Nightmare! You're going into the vault of correct answers I detest. This definitely cracks the Top 20 for me.

Since the causal-difference maker in the Author's Hypothesis was whether you watched yourself vs. someone else, LSAT feels like if we're talking about studies of identical twins, watching an identical twin is essentially a fair proxy for watching yourself.

(I definitely think any identical twin you ask would say, "Sure it's similar in some ways, but it's VERY different from watching myself". But LSAT just needs it to be relevantly similar)

If you swap out "observed their identical twin" with "watched themselves", you get an answer saying, "in other studies, participants who watched themselves read tended to significantly overreport how much time they read in the near future."

This suggests an alternate explanation for the data in the exercise study: the reason that the group that watched themselves run a treadmill is now reporting, on average, 1 hr more of exercise per day than the group that watched others isn't because watching yourself motivates you to actually exercise more; it's because watching yourself inclines you to overreport how much you do something in the days or week that follows.

This answer does do the expected: it provides an alternate explanation for the Curious Fact. It just does so in such a stupid, overwrought LSAT way that it's like they knew they needed to create a Level 5 question.

**E. A third group of participants who watched recordings of themselves sitting on couches afterwards reported being sedentary for more time each day than did the other participants.** — Strengthens (4% of students)
This reinforces the author's causal story, since it offers more data points in which watching yourself do X inclined you do to more X.

## S2 · Q23 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Environmentalist: Efforts to attain an overall reduction in carbon use by convincing people to focus on their personal use of fossil fuels cannot achieve that goal. Even if most people changed their behavior, changes in personal use of fossil fuels cannot produce the needed reductions in carbon use. Only government policies can produce change on the required scale.

### Prompt
The environmentalist's argument requires assuming which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Convincing people to focus on their personal use of fossil fuels cannot achieve an overall reduction in carbon use.
- **Evidence:** Even if most people changed their personal use, it wouldn't produce a reduction on the scale we need. Only government policies can produce change on that scale.
- **Evaluate:** Given that "only government policies can produce big enough change", how could we argue that "convincing people to focus on their personal use COULD achieve the goal of an overall reduction"?

  Well, the nitpicky objection would be that if the goal is literally just a reduction in carbon use, then if most people reduce their carbon use then there will be an overall reduction. The author is saying, "Yeah, but it won't be reduced enough", but she never stipulated that the goal was "a certain amount of overall reduction in carbon use". So she is assuming, somewhat nonsensically, that "if we don't reduce it enough to produce the needed reductions, then we didn't achieve the goal of an overall reduction."

  But, where I would assume this is actually going is the idea that maybe having individuals change their behavior could trigger governments changing their policies. After all, the government is made up of individuals. Maybe if the citizenry becomes more engaged in conserving carbon use, then their representatives in government will shift policies accordingly.
- **Goal:** I would look for an answer that rules out our objection: "changing personal behavior would not lead to the government changing its policies".

**Answer choices**
**A. Convincing most people to focus on their personal use of fossil fuels would not lead to their successfully pressuring the government into implementing policies that reduce carbon use.** — Correct (81% of students)
Whenever we see the word "not" connected to the verb, in a Necessary Assumption answer choice, we can just negate the answer by removing the word "not" and see if it becomes an Objection.

If we said, "convincing most people to focus on personal fossil fuel use would lead to their successfully pressuring the government into policy change that reduces carbon", then that would be a huge objection. We would essentially have just proven that the plan the author is rejecting would succeed at its goal.

If we love formal logic, the original conclusion was
```
Convince people
to change ------------------> ~Goal
```
and the premise was
```
~change      ---> ~Goal
to government
policy
```
so the missing idea is
```
Convince people --> ~change
to change           to government
                    policy
```

**B. The calculations needed to determine how best to minimize one's personal use of fossil fuels are too difficult for individuals to carry out on their own.** — Out of Scope: calculations too difficult (4% of students)
The author doesn't need to assume anything about personal calculations. Even if individuals are capable of calculating how to best minimize their personal use of fossil fuels, the author's premise still remains that "who care what individuals do? It's only change on a governmental scale that will have much impact".

**C. Efforts to convince people to focus on reducing their personal use of fossil fuels have been made only by those who are not currently involved in framing government policy.** — Too Strong: only (7% of students)
The author isn't committed to thinking that 100% of people who are trying to convince people to change their personal fossil fuel habits are removed from framing government policy.

If we negate this, we get "at least one person who has been trying to convince people to reduce their use of fossil fuels is currently involved in framing government policy".

Is that a big objection to the argument? No. It's a very, very tiny objection. It starts to move in the direction that (A) does, when negated: what if personal changes lead to government changes?

But it doesn't get us anywhere near the strength of negating (A), in which we learn that "personal changes will lead to successful changing of govt policy".

If an answer choices is phrased very strongly, then negating it always gives a very weak idea. That's one way to explain why we have a general aversion to strong language in Necessary Assumption answers.

**D. It is easier to convince the government to change its policies on carbon use than to convince people to reduce their personal use of fossil fuels.** — Out of Scope: easier (6% of students)
The author isn't digging her heels into a comparison between which is easier. She's simply making an anti-causal claim that doing X won't lead to Y, because only Z can lead to Y.

Making such a claim doesn't presume that doing X is easier than doing Z. They could be equally hard or Z could be harder. It wouldn't make any difference to the logic of "doing X won't lead to Y, because only Z can lead to Y".

The author is just assuming that "doing X won't lead to Z".

**E. People who are concerned about environmental issues are more likely to support political candidates who support environmental issues.** — Out of Scope: concern / support / Weakens (1% of students)
The author never talked about people who are / aren't concerned with the environment. This answer is just inviting people to make their own assumptions ("if someone is going to reduce their fossil fuel usage, they must be concerned about the environment?").

The author also never discussed supporting / not supporting political candidates. This is just loosely associated with the concept of whether or not the government will change policies regarding fossil fuel usage.

We could easily kill this answer because of all the out of scope stuff it's talking about, but even if we played along with those term shifts, this idea would actually weaken, if anything.

It starts to make it seem like people who reduce personal fossil fuel (concerned w/ environment) would support political candidates who would then change government policy (support environmental issues).

## S2 · Q24 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
There are only two plausible views about where the aesthetic value of a painting lies: either in its purely formal qualities or in what the painting means. But there exists no compelling general account of how a painting could derive its value from its purely formal characteristics. Therefore, the aesthetic value of a painting lies in what it means.

### Prompt
The pattern of questionable reasoning in the argument above is most similar to that in which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** There are only two plausible views about where the aesthetic value of a painting lies:
  1. in its purely formal qualities,
  2. in what the painting means.

  There exists no compelling general account of how a painting could derive its value from its purely formal characteristics.
- **Conclusion:** The aesthetic value of a painting lies in what it means.
- **Evaluate:** The basic form of the argument might look valid at first glance:

  Evidence: Either A or B
  Not A

  Conclusion: B

  For the argument to actually follow this pattern, the final premise would need to state that a painting does not derive its value from its purely formal characteristics. The actual premise only states that there is no "compelling general account" of how this could be. This basically means there's no good explanation for it.

  This is very similar to an "Unproven vs. Proven False" flaw. Saying "there is no good way to explain why X is true" is completely different from saying "X is not true." Fun fact: for a very long time, scientists could not explain exactly how anesthesia works. It still worked, and doctors still used it all the time.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer choice containing an argument that makes this same invalid shift from stating "there is no good way to explain why X is true" to assuming "X is not true."

**Answer choices**
**A. This cardiac patient could be treated with surgery or angioplasty, among other methods. But his weak condition would make recovery from surgery a very long process. Therefore, the doctors ought to perform angioplasty.** — Bad Evidence Match (3% of students)
This argument contains a whole different gap between the evidence and the conclusion. Stating that a patient "could" be treated a certain way isn't the same as stating that a patient "ought to" be treated one way or the other.

Also, the evidence indicates that there are "other methods" besides surgery or angioplasty. It's not a strict dichotomy like the original argument.

To be any kind of match for the original argument, the final premise would need to state that there isn't evidence that surgery would work. It only states that there would be a long recovery.

**B. Should the company be outbid on the new project, it will either have to lay off workers or find new business. But it does not expect to find new business in the foreseeable future. Therefore, it must be expecting to win the bid on the new project.** — Bad Evidence Match (4% of students)
The structure of this argument is different from the original. The evidence here contains a conditional statement, where a third factor—being outbid—triggers the either-or situation.

Most importantly, this argument assumes that one possibility—laying off workers—will not happen, or at least is not expected to happen. It never states that there is no convincing evidence that the company will need to lay off workers.

**C. History is driven primarily by economic forces or primarily by political forces. But no historian has shown convincingly that history is driven mainly by economic forces. Therefore, it is driven primarily by political forces.** — Correct (89% of students)
The evidence only states that "no historian has shown convincingly" that economic forces have primarily driven history. This doesn't prove that economic forces haven't primarily driven history.

**D. Some analysts are forecasting that if the economy expands, the inflation rate will rise or the unemployment rate will fall. But the unemployment rate promises to remain stable. Therefore, the inflation rate will not change either.** — Bad Evidence Match (2% of students)
This is similar to choice B in that the evidence introduces an additional element, the economy expanding, which triggers the either-or condition. That's different from the original argument.

This argument also lacks the flaw seen in the original. The evidence doesn't explicitly state that there is no convincing evidence for X and then assume, based on that, that X is not true.

**E. If the party does not change its policies, it will lose heavily in the next election. But if it changes its policies, some people who support those policies will be upset and will sit out the next election. Therefore, it is impossible for the party to win in the next election.** — Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
This argument has a different structure from the original, with two conditional statements as evidence:

Evidence: ~A → B
A → (B is more likely)

Conclusion: B

This doesn't contain the same type of flaw where "X has not been proven" is assumed to mean "X is not true."

Substantial economic growth must be preceded by technological innovations that expanding industries incorporate into their production or distribution procedures. Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the argument's reasoning?

- **Evidence:** Economic booms ---require--> tech innovations.

  X must be preceded by Y.

This answer choice describes Circular Reasoning, in which the author doesn't really offer any premise; she just believes what she believes. But this argument definitely had two premises that are distinct from the conclusion.

**B. The argument attempts to establish the falsehood of a proposition by criticizing the reasoning of those who assert its truth.** — Wrong Flaw (1% of students)
This answer choice basically sounds like Unproven vs. Proven False, in which we say "Since your argument for X was crappy, that proves that X is false."

**C. The argument attempts to establish a conclusion on the basis of stronger evidence than the conclusion requires.** — Never a Flaw (3% of students)
Haha. I don't think I've ever seen this answer (it would never be correct). There's nothing wrong with one's evidence being too good.

- said no judge, ever

Tech innovations were identified as necessary for the phenomenon of economic boom to occur, and the author is acting like as long as we know that a global ban on fuel would lead to tech innovations, we know that it would lead to an economic boom.

# Section 3 (Logical Reasoning)

## S3 · Q1 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A nonprofit organization concerned with a social issue sent out a fund-raising letter to 5,000 people. The letter was accompanied by a survey soliciting recipients’ opinions. Of the 300 respondents, 283 indicated in the survey that they agreed with the organization’s position on the social issue. This suggests that most of the 5,000 people to whom the letter was sent agreed with that position.

### Prompt
The argument is most vulnerable to criticism on which one of the following grounds?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (This suggests that):** Most of the people who received the nonprofit letter agreed with their position on the social issue presented.
- **Evidence:** 283 of the 300 people who responded to the letter agreed with the position.
- **Evaluate:** We seem to be looking at one of the Top 10 famous flaws, Sampling.

  We know that most of the respondents agreed with the nonprofit's position on the social issue. The author is trying to conclude that most of the recipients agreed with the position on the social issue.

  So it's a question of whether we think that "people who responded" are a fairly typical cross-section of recipients. Are they a sample from which we can extrapolate general conclusions?

  Our concerns with Samples usually are that the sample is too small (like under 20), the respondents didn't provide truthful responses (out of fear of shame or retaliation), or most commonly that the people who comprise our sample population are self-selecting / biased / skewed / atypical in some way.

  A sample size of 300 out of 5,000 total letters is a pretty respectable size. It could always be more robust with bigger numbers, but anything over 100 is nothing LSAT would ever complain about.

  Are these 300 respondents likely to be an atypical sample of the underlying population of recipients?

  Maybe. This is where our outside common sense comes into play. We might be well aware of "the Yelp phenomenon", the idea that people generally only take the time to write a review if they're really motivated (loved it or hated it), so you see mainly 5-star and 1-star reviews.

  Similarly, it could be that because of the effort it takes to respond to a survey that showed up in your mail, most people wouldn't respond whether they agreed or disagreed. It would mainly be the zealots who write back.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer objecting that it's not safe to assume that this sample of 300 respondents is representative of the underlying 5000 recipients, because respondents are a self-selecting group of people who had really strong reactions.

  In general, if a nonprofit you don't agree with mailed you something, you'd pitch it in the recycling bin, thinking "not for me". You probably would only write the organization if you were super aligned with the cause and wanted to get involved. So we could even say that the low response rate overall (only 300 out of 5000) could be evidence that people mostly weren't aligned with that position.

**Answer choices**
**A. It draws a conclusion about a population from observations of a subgroup that is quite likely to be unrepresentative of that population in certain relevant respects.** — Correct (93% of students)
This is the Sampling flaw answer we were looking for.

Some of the abstract language here definitely matches up: the author did draw a conclusion about a population (the 5,000 recipients) based on observations of a subgroup (the 300 respondents).

Can we say that the sample of respondents is quite unlikely to be unrepresentative of the overall population of recipients?

That seems to be overdoing it a little, but this is our best available answer. Does LSAC just think that "respondents" on any mailed out survey are an inherently unlikely sample?

Yes, apparently, and probably with good reason. As we covered before, it's a well documented phenomenon with product reviews / teacher evaluations and such that the people most likely to respond are the ones with the most extreme opinions.

We can also enlist our common sense -- if you got a mailer from a nonprofit you didn't agree with, would you fill out their survey and mail it back?

No, of course not. Why would you waste your time helping out this organization whose cause you don't even support? So LSAC seems to be counting on our real world common sense to appreciate that people would be way more likely to take the time to fill out a survey and mail it back if they already support that nonprofit's cause.

**B. It takes for granted that most individuals do not vary significantly in the opinions they would express on a given issue if surveyed regarding that issue on different occasions.** — Not Assumed (0% of students)
This answer is kind of tricky. Because it starts with takes for granted / presumes / fails to establish, we treat the idea that follows as if we're reading a Necessary Assumption answer choice.

If we negated this and said, "Yo, author -- most people vary significantly in their opinions on a given issue, if you ask them the same question on some other occasion", it seems like that would hurt the argument.

How can the author argue on the basis of this survey that most of the recipients agree with the organization's position if most people don't stay attached to one position over time? They might have once agreed with the org's position, but if you asked them again tomorrow, most of them would disagree.

However, that line of objection doesn't actually work. The conclusion isn't present tense. It isn't saying that the recipients presently agree with organization's position, just that they agreed when the letter was sent out.

Beyond that technicality, we should be worried about this answer on a common sense level. It's a very reasonable assumption that most people don't change their opinions on big social issues significantly from occasion to occasion. It would be weird for the correct answer on Flaw to be chastising the author for a very reasonable assumption.

**C. It relies on the accuracy of a survey made under conditions in which it is probable that most of the responses to that survey did not correctly reflect the opinions of the respondents.** — Wrong Sampling Flaw (3% of students)
This is alluding to a Sampling problem we sometimes, but rarely, see, in which people are afraid to tell the truth.

For example, if your boss took a survey and asked, "Do you ever take any office supplies home? How much computer time do you spend on social media?" you probably wouldn't provide truthful responses.

We don't have any reason to suspect with this nonprofit's mailed out survey that the respondents would be afraid to provide truthful responses.

**D. It uses evidence about an opinion held by the majority of a population in an attempt to justify a conclusion regarding the opinion of a small part of that population.** — Reversed Conclusion / Evidence Match (3% of students)
The conclusion is about the majority of a population (the 5,000 recipients), and the evidence is about a small part of that population (the 300 people who responded).

This answer switches those and says the evidence is about the majority and the conclusion is about the small part.

**E. It takes for granted that the fund-raising letter had some influence on the opinions of most of the people who received it.** — Out of Scope: influenced opinions (1% of students)
The author doesn't need to assume that the letter influenced the opinion of the recipients. She is only claiming that most recipients agreed with the position.

There's no reason the letter needed to change anyone's opinion. If most people agreed with that position on a pre-existing basis before they received this fund-raising letter, the argument still works.

## S3 · Q4 — Role

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
According to economists, people’s tendency to purchase a given commodity is inversely proportional to its price. When new techniques produced cheaper steel, more steel was purchased. Nevertheless, once machine-produced lace became available, at much lower prices than the handcrafted variety, lace no longer served to advertise its wearers’ wealth and the lace market collapsed. Obviously, then, there are exceptions to the economists’ general rule.

### Prompt
The claim that more steel was purchased when it could be manufactured more cheaply plays which one of the following roles in the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (Obviously, then):** There are exceptions to the economists' general rule.

  economists' rule: Tendency to purchase is inversely proportional to price.

  Conclusion-translated: Sometimes people's tendency to purchase a given commodity is not inversely proportional to its price
  (i.e. the price rises and more want to buy it, or the price drops and fewer people want to buy it)
- **Evidence:** Sure there's an example that fits the economists rule, where price and purchasing were inverted: the price of steel went down, and the amount purchasing went up.

  But, there's a counterexample to the rule, where price and purchasing were aligned: the price of lace went down, and the purchasing of lace went down (if you can't rub it in the poor's face that they can't afford lace, then what's the point?)
- **Evaluate:** The arguments on Main Conclusion and Role are intentionally more opaque. They don't want to make finding the conclusion an easy part of the process, because it's the main event on these questions. So any time we're doing Main Conclusion or Role, we're anticipating the conclusion probably won't be at the end with an obvious Therefore / Thus indicator word. But sometimes it will be last, usually on easier ranked problems like this Q4.

  Instead, the Conclusion will usually be one of these two:
  - the first claim in the paragraph
  - a rebuttal

  In this case the conclusion is at the end of the paragraph, and it does have some indicator words, although they're less obvious ones.

  Obviously / evidently / clearly / apparently are frequently used on LSAT to show us an opinion, and of course when we're searching for the Main Conclusion we're searching for the opinion that the author supports.

  Then / consequently / as a result are used to show the terminal end of a chain of thought, i.e. the conclusion of a train of thought.

  This final sentence is the author's rebuttal, so if we're familiar with looking for Rebuttal Conclusions and First Sentence Conclusions on Main Conclusion and Role, we'll hopefully gravitate towards the rebuttal, since the first sentence contained an attribution to "economists".

  The claim we're being asked about is the author's concession to the occasional validity of the economists point of view.

  The four claims here go like this:

  1. Attributed claim
  "according to economists, X"

  2. Author's conceding some validity
  "here's an example about steel that matches X"

  3. Author pivoting into premise
  "Nevertheless, here's a counterexample to X"

  4. Author's rebuttal conclusion
  "Obviously, then, the economists are wrong"
- **Goal:** They're asking us about #2, so we want something like, "The author is acknowledging that there are some data points that do fit the economists' general rule (even though the rule does not universally hold)."

**Answer choices**
**A. It is described as inadequate evidence for the falsity of the argument’s conclusion.** — Out of Scope: described as inadequate (1% of students)
It isn't described as anything, so that's reason enough to consider this answer inaccurate.

The argument's conclusion is saying "there are exceptions to Rule X" and the claim we're looking at is an example of a data point that conforms to Rule X.

If an author is trying to prove that "there are exceptions to Rule X", a data point that matches rule X isn't evidence for the conclusion's falsity. It's really just irrelevant. If we're trying to prove that there are exceptions, our argument has nothing to do with whether or not there are conforming examples.

**B. It is described as an exception to a generalization for which the argument offers evidence.** — Out of Scope: described / Opposite: exception (3% of students)
It isn't described as anything, so that's reason enough to consider this answer inaccurate.

The argument's conclusion is saying "there are exceptions to generalization X", but the claim we're looking at is an example of a data point that conforms to the generalization.

**C. It is used to illustrate the generalization that, according to the argument, does not hold in all cases.** — Correct (86% of students)
Is there a generalization that, according to the argument, does not hold in call cases?

Yes, the economists' general rule is a generalization. And according to the author's conclusion, there are exceptions to it; it does not hold in all cases.

Okay, was this 2nd claim used to illustrate the economists' general rule?

Yes! It was the author conceding that some data points do match that rule.

**D. It is the evidence that, according to the argument, led economists to embrace a false hypothesis.** — Out of Scope: led economists to embrace (8% of students)
Is there something that, according to the argument, led economists to embrace a false hypothesis?

No, the argument never tells us a story about what caused the economists to espouse a general rule that has exceptions (calling that a false hypothesis is also a big stretch)

**E. It is cited as one of several reasons for modifying a general assumption made by economists.** — Wrong Role / Out of Scope: modifying (1% of students)
Nothing in the argument ever talks about modifying any assumption or rule made by the economists. The author is only proving that exceptions to the rule do exist. That doesn't mean you have to get rid of the rule or modify the rule.

We can tolerate the existence of exceptions and still find a rule worthwhile. This is just descriptively, neutrally saying exceptions exist.

This answer is also going on the wrong track as soon as it says "one of several reasons". That makes it sound like this was a Premise, but it was more like an Opposing point than a Supporting one. This is a concession to the economists' rule, because it's highlighting a case in which the rule applies.

## S3 · Q7 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
The Kuna, a people native to several Panamanian islands, generally have a low incidence of high blood pressure. But Kuna who have moved to the Panamanian mainland do not have a low incidence of high blood pressure. Kuna who live on the islands, unlike those who live on the mainland, typically drink several cups of cocoa a day. This cocoa is minimally processed and thus high in flavonoids.

### Prompt
Of the following, which one is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Information:** asymmetric comparison (Curious Fact)

  Kuna who were born and live on the islands generally have a low incidence of high blood pressure, whereas Kuna who moved to the mainland don't have this low incidence of high blood pressure.

  asymmetric comparison

  Kuna on the islands typically drink several cups of high-flavonoid cocoa a day, whereas Kuna who live on the mainland don't.
- **Evaluate:** This looks ripe for a "One Difference Explains the Other" type inference.

  Suppose we said, "Bob has improved at LSAT much less than Jane has over the course of the three months they've both been studying. Jane, unlike Bob, does mindfulness breathing after every study session."

  Can we prove that the mindfulness breathing is the causal difference-maker that has allowed Jane to improve more than Bob?

  Of course not. But can we support that idea? Yes.

  On Most Supported questions, we're sometimes asked to speculate that Difference 1 explains Difference 2.
- **Goal:** We would anticipate that "drinking several cups of high-flavonoid cocoa" is a reason that the island Kuna have lower blood pressure than the mainland Kuna.

**Answer choices**
**A. Foods high in flavonoids are not readily available on the Panamanian mainland.** — Too Speculative: not available (5% of students)
Yes, we think that the island Kuna's blood pressure is lower than mainland Kuna's because the island Kuna are drinking several cups of high-flavonoid cocoa each day

And if we think that consuming this high-flavonoid cocoa is the causal difference-maker, then we are thinking that mainland Kuna are not imbibing as much flavonoids.

But it's going one step further here to speculate why they're not imbibing as much flavonoids.

Maybe it's because high-flavonoid foods aren't readily available. Or maybe there are high-flavonoid foods readily available, but they don't have that class island cocoa. In other words, if the Kuna lived on the islands, they'd drink the traditional cocoa several times a day and (perhaps unwittingly) consume a lot of flavonoids.

For the Kuna on the mainland, there might not be readily available places to find this cocoa. Since they aren't drinking the cocoa, they aren't consuming a lot of flavonoids, even though there are high-flavonoid foods still readily available on the mainland.

We have no reason to think that the Kuna are aware of the benefit of consuming flavonoids and will seek them out if there are high-flavonoid foods readily available.

**B. Kuna who live on the islands drink cocoa because they believe that it is beneficial to their health.** — Too Speculative: beliefs (1% of students)
Yes, we think that the island Kuna's blood pressure is lower than mainland Kuna's because the island Kuna are drinking several cups of high-flavonoid cocoa each day

But it's too speculative to add on something about what the Kuna know or believe. They might be drinking the cocoa because it's delicious or because it's a traditional drink, having no idea that the flavonoid content is actually helping their health.

**C. The Kuna have a genetic predisposition to low blood pressure.** — Contradicted (1% of students)
The Kuna who move to the mainland are genetically the same as the Kuna who live on the island, yet the former do not have a low incidence of high blood pressure.

If a genetic predisposition were causing the low blood pressure, then both island Kuna and mainland Kuna would have low blood pressure.

**D. Kuna who live on the Panamanian mainland generally have higher blood pressure than other people who live on the mainland.** — Out of Scope: non-Kuna mainlanders (4% of students)
We have no grounds for making any comparison between Kuna on the mainland and other people on the mainland, since we received no information about non-Kuna mainlanders.

**E. Drinking several cups of flavonoid-rich cocoa per day tends to prevent high blood pressure.** — Correct (89% of students)
This is by no means a Must Be True idea, but it is the causal connection most suggested by the available details.

We're provided with one curious difference -- "How come island Kuna have lower blood pressure than mainland Kuna?"

And then we're provided with one other contrast -- "The island Kuna drink several cups of high-flavonoid cocoa per day, but the mainland Kuna don't".

And so that leads us to this speculation ... "Hmmm, maybe the high-flavonoid cocoa is the causal difference-maker for why the island Kuna have lower blood pressure!"

### Stimulus
Numerous studies suggest that when scientific evidence is presented in a trial, jurors regard that evidence as more credible than they would if they had encountered the same evidence outside of the courtroom context. Legal theorists have hypothesized that this effect is primarily due to the fact that judges prescreen scientific evidence and allow only credible scientific evidence to be presented in the courtroom.

### Prompt
Which one of the following would be most useful to know in order to evaluate the legal theorists’ hypothesis?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Curious Fact:** Why do jurors think of evidence as more credible when they see it inside the courtroom vs. outside of a courtroom?
- **Hypothesis:** Judges prescreen scientific evidence and only allow credible scientific evidence to be presented in the courtroom (so jurors are more likely to trust what they see in a courtroom).
- **Evaluate:** We always evaluate Curious Fact / Explanation situations through two prisms:
  1. can we think of an Alternate Explanation for the curious fact?

  What could be a different reason why jurors are more likely to regard evidence as credible when it's presented in a courtroom? (maybe it's because people are wearing suits and sounding like experts / maybe it's because people swear in on a bible before they testify)

  2. how plausible is the Author's Explanation for it?

  What would need to be true for this hypothesis to even make sense? (we would have to assume that jurors are aware that the evidence has been pre-screened for credibility / we have to assume that jurors trust this pre-screening process to filter out dubious stuff / we have to assume a difference, that when people see scientific evidence outside of a courtroom, it hasn't gone through some filtering process to weed out the dubious stuff)
- **Goal:** Look for an answer either asking whether or not an Alternate Explanation could be true, or for an answer asking a question that's central to whether or not we'd consider the author's hypothesis plausible.

  One subtle but important feature of the hypothesis is that they're saying the trusting effect is primarily due to the fact that judges prescreen evidence. Because of that word, they're allowing for the idea that other things might also make a difference.

  So an Alternate Explanation here will only weaken if we learn that it is the primary reason why people are more trusting of evidence in the courtroom. Thus, we might be more likely to get a correct answer attacking the plausibility of the Author's Hypothesis.

**Answer choices**
**A. whether jurors typically know that judges have appraised the scientific evidence presented at trial** — Correct (88% of students)
On Evaluate, we're basically looking for a question / toss-up that could be answered in a way that would Weaken the argument.

Here, if we say, "No, jurors typically do not know that judges have appraised the scientific evidence for accuracy" then that would basically blow the hypothesis to bits.

If your friends are hypothesizing why you're so into that new bartender and their hypothesis is, "He likes her because of her Bob Marley tattoo", then it would totally unravel their hypothesis if you told them, "Oh, did she have a Bob Marley tattoo? I didn't even see it!"

Similarly, there's no way to believe that jurors are more trusting of evidence in a courtroom because it's been pre-screened if jurors have no idea that it's been pre-screened.

If they don't realize there's a difference between accuracy-filtered courtroom evidence and unfiltered scientific evidence outside of the courtroom, then how would that explain their different mentality?

**B. whether jurors’ reactions to scientific evidence presented at trial are influenced by other members of the jury** — Weak Impact (3% of students)
This one feels somewhat tempting to me because it looks like it might be hinting at an Alternate Explanation -- jurors don't think of scientific evidence in the courtroom differently because they trust that the judge has pre-screened it for accuracy, they think of it differently because their fellow jurors convince them to think of it differently.

Here's the problem with that ... if we stick to the actual wording of the answer choice, it's not leading us far enough into that story we were just telling.

We can either say
NO, jurors' reactions to sci evid isn't influenced by other members of the jury at all.

## S3 · Q15 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A good manager must understand people and be able to defuse tense situations. But anyone who is able to defuse tense situations must understand people. Since Ishiko is able to defuse tense situations, she must be a good manager.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the argument is flawed in that it

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Ishiko must be a good manager
- **Evidence (since):** Ishiko is able to defuse tense situations. Anyone who is able to defuse tense situations must understand people. (infer: Ishiko can understand people) A good manager must understand people and be able to defuse tense situations.
- **Evaluate:** This argument is functionally the same as this one: A Supreme Court justice must be able to speak in complete sentences and read things written in English. My buddy T-bone can read things written in English and speak in complete sentences. Thus, T-bone must be a Supreme Court justice. There's a big difference between satisfying some necessary requirements for being X and definitely being X. The first sentence doesn't say that the only things a good manager must be able to do is understand people and defuse tense situations. They might also need to be able to enter payroll information into the proper government forms, for example. Does Ishiko know how to do that? We don't know. We are told a couple things required of being a good manager, and we're told that Ishiko meets those requirements. But meeting a couple requirements of being a good manager (or a SCOTUS justice) doesn't prove you are a good manager. The formal name for this flaw is Necessary vs. Sufficient, which just means that the author presented conditional logic in her evidence, but then she tries to apply that conditional rule in backwards or opposite fashion. If she presents a rule that says "X → Y", then the argument will either make the flawed argument that, "Since Ishiko is Y, she must be X" (illegal reversal) or the flawed argument that "Since Ishiko is not X, she must not be Y" (illegal negation). The first premise here looks like this: Good manager → Und ppl and Defuse tense. Then the author argues an illegal reversal: Ishiko can understand people and defuse tense situations. **Answer choices**
**A. confuses a quality that shows an understanding of people with a quality that is necessary for understanding people** — Wrong Conditional (6% of students)
This answer is pretending that the conditional rule the author messed up was the one in the 2nd sentence, about a quality (able to defuse tense situations) that that shows an understanding of people. But the author was fine in regards to the 2nd sentence: able to defuse tense → understand people; Ishiko is able to defuse tense, (so Ishiko understands people). There was nothing wrong with that part of the argument. The author messed up the logic of the rule in the 1st sentence. She confused qualities required to be a good manager with qualities that would show you are a good manager.

**B. confuses a quality that usually correlates with being a good manager with a quality that results from being a good manager** — Wrong Flaw: not Causal (6% of students)
This answer choice accuses the argument of confusing a correlated factor with a causal factor. That is a famous flaw, but not the one being committed here. There isn't a correlation given (we could pretend the first sentence could play the role of a correlation, but it is not presented that way). Correlations sound like this: Good managers tend to be X; People who are good managers are more likely than those who aren't to have quality X; Quality X is associated with being a good manager. And there is definitely nothing in this argument that is acting like being a good manager caused Ishiko to have a certain quality (that resulted from her being a good manager).

**C. confuses qualities necessary for being a good manager with qualities that guarantee being a good manager** — Correct (85% of students)
The first sentence lists out two qualities required for being a good manager: 1. understand people 2. able to defuse tense situations. The argument establishes that Ishiko possesses both those qualities and then acts like that proves that she must be a good manager. In doing so, the author is confusedly acting like those qualities guarantee that you're a good manager. Confusing "necessary" with "guarantee" is the same as Necessary vs. Sufficient.

**D. overlooks the possibility that different managers defuse tense situations in different ways** — Not an Objection (1% of students)
When we see Flaw answer choices that begin with "fails to consider / overlooks the possibility" we can ask ourselves, "(assuming they didn't address this issue), would it weaken the argument, if true?" Would it weaken this argument to say, "Hey, author, different managers defuse tense situations in different ways"? No. That would only hurt his argument if he had been assuming that all managers defuse tense situations the same way, but he wasn't assuming anything like that.

**E. takes for granted that because all good managers have a certain quality, Ishiko must have that quality** — Bad Conclusion Match (2% of students)
When a Flaw answer choice is structured like "takes for granted that because X, Y" we ask ourselves whether X matched the evidence and Y matched the conclusion (or some assumption the author made en route to the conclusion). Was there a premise that said, "All good managers have a certain quality"? Yes, they all have the quality of "Understand people and can defuse tense situation". Did the author conclude that Ishiko must have that quality? No the author presented half of that as a premise and the other half as an implied premise. The conclusion was that Ishiko must be a good manager. We could fix this answer somewhat by saying: (E) takes for granted that because all good managers have certain qualities, that anyone possessing those qualities is a good manager.

If the babblers have the means to escape predators by just flying away, then why are they doing any of this barking in the first place? Usually, if a species already has one adaptive response to a predator (fly away), it doesn't involve a second one for the same purpose (bark & hide?) Someone might like this answer thinking, "Look, they bark to warn each other to take cover, but they're not worried about giving away their position or barking long after they've taken cover, because they could always fly away." Fair enough, but that would basically explain why they can get away with continuing to bark. It doesn't provide any reason for why they are continuing to bark in the first place.

**B. Babblers' predators are generally intimidated by large numbers of babblers.** — Correct (70% of students)
This helps us understand the utility of the whole barking routine. It's not (just) meant as a way of saying, "yo, fellow babblers! There is a predator afoot. Everyone get to cover!" Instead, the whole group of babblers starts barking shrilly together, in order to reveal to the predator just how many of them are up there in the tree canopy. We were told that they live in large cooperative groups, so if they all start barking, the predator will generally be intimidated and run away. This helps make sense of why the barking continues even once they've all taken cover -- the barking don't stop 'til the predator hits the road.

**C. There is more than one type of predator that preys upon babblers.** — No Impact (3% of students)
Whether they only have one predator or have many types, we still don't have any explanation for why they bark (which gives away their position) nor why they continue barking even once they've all taken cover.

**D. Babblers' predators have very good eyesight but relatively weak hearing.** — No Impact / Cheats the Paradox (15% of students)
A lot of us are tempted by this, thinking, "If the predators have relatively weak hearing, then maybe they can't even hear the babblers' barking, and thus it's not so surprising that the babblers are unafraid to continue barking." Two problems with that: 1. "relatively weak" is not strong enough for us to start thinking, "They're deaf". Humans have a relatively weak sense of smell compared to most animals, but that doesn't mean we can't smell a skunk's spray when it tries to ward us off. It's too much of a leap to go from "relatively weak hearing" to "the predators can't hear the barking", especially when the barking is described as loud and shrill, both of which would be the one thing someone hard of hearing could still notice. 2. If we were to think, "maybe the predators don't even hear the barking", we would be cheating the background information of the paradox. It tells us that, "these predators generally become aware of the presence of babblers only because of their shrill barks", so we KNOW that the predators definitely hear the barks.

**E. Animals that live in close proximity to babblers are also preyed upon by the predators that prey upon babblers.** — Less Impact than Correct Answer (8% of students)
We might keep this around on a first pass, thinking, "oh here is some utility to the excessive barking -- it alerts other animals down the way that there's a predator around." But it's not really common for animals to develop evolutionary behaviors in order to help out other species, so it's a little far-fetched to think they evolved this barking routine in order to help some other species. That's not how evolution works (unless it's a symbiotic relationship like when birds ride atop ox, and the birds eat bugs out of the ox's fur while the ox dissuades the birds' predators from coming near). More importantly, the correct answer doesn't involve this sort of stretch. It explains the babblers' barking in away that benefits the babblers. We have to prefer that story over one in which the barking benefits other species.

## S3 · Q21 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Journalist: People whose diets contain a relatively large amount of iron are significantly more likely to develop Parkinson's disease than are those whose diets contain less of this mineral. Limiting one's intake of meats, seafood, and other foods rich in iron should thus reduce one's chances of contracting this disease.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the journalist's reasoning?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (should thus):** Limiting one's intake of meats, seafood, and other foods rich in iron should reduce one's chance of contracting Parkinson's disease.
- **Evidence (people who are X more likely Y):** There's a correlation between diets contain large amount of iron | more likely to get Parkinson's.
- **Evaluate:** When an author presents a Curious Comparison like a correlation, they will almost always then make some causal assumption about one side causing the other. In this case we can tell the author is assuming that eating diet with lots of iron leads to higher chance of Parkinson's, because her conclusion is acting like avoiding a diet high in iron would help lower chance of Parkinson's. Whenever an author assumes some causal explanation for a curious fact, we react with two different questions: could there be some Alternate Explanation for the curious fact? how plausible is the Author's Explanation?
- **Goal:** Since this is Strengthen, we want to either rule out a different way to explain why a diet high in iron is correlated with higher Parkinson's risks (i.e. "frequent blows to the head, which is known to increase Parkinson's risks, are not much more likely for people who eat high iron diets than for those who don't") or increase the plausibility that high iron leads to higher Parkinson's risk, or that eating less meat / seafood / iron-rich food would help lower one's Parkinson's risk.

**Answer choices**
**A. Most people who have a genetic predisposition to Parkinson's disease have no more iron in their diets than people without the predisposition.** — Correct (65% of students)
This rules out an alternate explanation. Whenever we're presented with a correlation between X and Y, and the author concludes that X causes Y, we'll come back at him with two classic Alternate Explanations: what if Y causes X (reverse causality), or what if Z is the reason Y and X are correlated (third factor). In this argument, reverse causality would be nonsense, because "more likely to develop" Parkinson's implies that Parkinson's came later. We can't argue that Parkinson's came first and it led to a higher iron diet. But we can still argue that some third factor is causing the higher Parkinson's risk, and it's either causing or correlated with higher iron diet, which is why there appears to be a correlation between Parkinson's and high iron. Having a lot of money could simultaneously cause you to eat caviar and play golf. If we cited a correlation that "people who play golf are more likely than those who don't to eat caviar", we wouldn't want to assume that playing golf is what's causing you to eat caviar. Similarly, a certain genetic profile might lead to both higher risk of developing Parkinson's as well as some form of anemia (iron-deprivation). That sort of person would both have to eat a high iron diet and be at high risk of developing Parkinson's. Thus, there would be a correlation between high iron and higher rate of Parkinson's, even though neither one is causing the other. When a Strengthen answer is ruling out an alternate cause, it's "controlling" for some other variable, so we frequently hear the language "were no more likely to" or something equalizing like that. If people with a genetic bent towards Parkinson's are no more likely to have a high iron diet, then it rules out the possibility that high iron is just a coincidental factor that's "riding on the coattails" of the real causal factor ... genetic predisposition.

**B. Many of the vegetables regularly consumed by vegetarians who do not contract Parkinson's disease are as rich in iron as meat and seafood.** — No Impact (5% of students)
When authors list examples, as this one did by citing 'meat, seafood, and other foods rich in iron", the named examples are not important at all. But LSAC likes to write trap answers trying to get people to care. The logical force of meat / seafood / veggies consumed by vegetarians is all the same: high iron. Being told that some veggies are also high in iron means nothing. It doesn't help us investigate whether these high iron diets (whether that iron is coming from meat / fish / veggies) is the causal factor creating a higher risk of Parkinson's.

**C. Children and adolescents require a much larger amount of iron in their diets than do mature adults.** — Irrelevant Comparison (4% of students)
The fact that kids are more likely to eat iron than adults doesn't have any clear impact on whether high iron has a causal effect on Parkinson's risk. We're trying to compare people at the same stage of life in terms of more iron / less iron in their diet. The author is thinking, given two 30 year old people (or two 40 year old's, or two 10 year old's), the person limiting their intake of iron-rich foods will be less likely to develop Parkinson's.

**D. The iron in some foods is much less easily absorbed by the body than the iron contained in other foods.** — Too Weak (23% of students)
This is such a weak idea. When you woke up today, did you think that "Every single food in the world was identical in terms of how quickly the body can absorb its iron content"? Of course not. So this answer is only telling you what you already knew from common sense: differences and fluctuations exist.

**E. The amounts of iron-rich foods consumed by people starts to decline beginning at age 50.** — Irrelevant Distinction (4% of students)
This is similar to (C) in the sense that we aren't interested in comparing one age group to another, since the argument never cared about that. We're only interested in comparing people of any given age group with an iron-rich diet to people of that same age group with a less iron-rich diet, in relation to whether or not they have or ultimately develop Parkinson's.

**Answer choices**

**D. A work of world literature is always influenced by works outside of the writer's national tradition.** — Out of Scope: influenced by (13% of students)
There's nothing in any of these necessary conditions for world literature that talks about the work being influenced by foreign works. We only talk about this work influencing domestic readers and international readers.

**E. A work is not part of world literature if it affects the development of only one national tradition.** — Correct (60% of students)
This, unlike most correct answers on Most Supported and Must Be True, can be derived from one claim: the first sentence. That rule stipulates a necessary quality for world literature: World literature → Received and interpreted within author's national tradition and within external national traditions. That right side means that to be world literature, you have to affect at least 2 national traditions. "Being received and interpreted within a national tradition" qualifies as "affecting the development of the national tradition". So this answer choice is just doing a contrapositive of the first sentence. If you're only affecting one national tradition, then you can't be affecting both domestic and international traditions, and so you can't be world literature.

## S3 · Q25 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
At Morris University this semester, most of the sociology majors are taking Introduction to Social Psychology, but most of the psychology majors are not. Hence, there must be more sociology majors than psychology majors enrolled in the class.

### Prompt
The flawed pattern of reasoning in the argument above is most similar to that in which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** There must be more sociology majors than psychology majors enrolled in Intro to Social Psychology.
- **Evidence:** Most sociology majors are taking it. Most PSY majors are not taking it.
- **Evaluate:** Because this argument has conditional/quantified terms, we could potentially get it right without understanding the flaw, as long as we convert it into a symbolic recipe. Most A's are in place X. Most B's are not in place X. Thus, there are more A's than B's in place X. The flaw revolves around the fact that we're making two Most claims, but they're about different groups. You can't really compare such claims without knowing about the relative size of those different groups. For example, let's say at Morris University that there 100 sociology majors and 1000 psychology majors. Most sociology majors are in this class, so at least 51 sociology majors are there. Most psychology majors are not in this class, so at most 499 psychology majors are there. Have I convinced you that this class has more sociology majors than psychology majors? Of course not. There might be way more psychology majors present, if the group of all psychology majors is bigger than the group of all sociology majors.
- **Goal:** Look for two Most premises, and a conclusion saying one number is bigger than another. Look for something resembling this recipe: Most A's are in place X. Most B's are not in place X. Thus, more A's than B's in place X. Or look for an argument similarly vulnerable to the objection of, "What if one of those groups is much bigger than the other?"

**Answer choices**
**A. Most of the paintings on display at the Metro Art Museum are from the twentieth century, but most of the paintings the Metro Art Museum owns are from the nineteenth century. It follows that the museum owns few if any of the twentieth-century paintings it displays.** — Bad Conclusion Match (4% of students)
We do have two Most premises, but the conclusion isn't saying that one quantity is bigger than another, so there's no reason to dig deeper here.

**B. In an opinion poll of Silver Falls residents, more said they were in favor of increased spending on roads than said they were in favor of increased spending on parks. So most Silver Falls residents must be in favor of spending more on roads but opposed to spending more on parks.** — Bad Premise / Conclusion Match (4% of students)
There's only one premise, so that's an immediate deal breaker. The conclusion also isn't saying that one quantity is bigger than another (it's saying, "most people prefer one thing over another").

**C. In the San Felipe city arboretum, most of the trees are of local rather than exotic species. Therefore, in the San Felipe area, there must be more trees of local species than of exotic species.** — Bad Premise Match (11% of students)
There's only one premise, so that's an immediate deal breaker. The conclusion, to its credit, is lovingly saying that one quantity is bigger than another.

**D. Most of the vegetables available at the Valley Food Co-op are organic, but most of the vegetables available at the Jumbo Supermarket are not. Thus, more organic vegetables are available at Valley Food than are available at Jumbo.** — Correct (64% of students)
From a bird's eye view, it's got two Most premises and a conclusion that compares quantities. Here's how it would match with the original argument. Most SOC are taking Intro to SP / Most available veggies at VCF are organic. Most PSY are not taking Intro to SP / Most available veggies at JS are not organic. So, more SOC than PSY are taking Intro to SP / More VFC veggies than JS veggies are organic. Both arguments are vulnerable to the objection, "What if one of those groups is much bigger?" If Jumbo Supermarket sells 1000 vegetables, but Valley Co-op sells 100, Valley Co-op sells a minimum of 51 organic. Jumbo sells a maximum of 499 organic. So there might be more organic vegetables available at Jumbo.

**E. The Acme Realty website has photos of most of the houses, but of fewer than half of the condominiums, that Acme is offering for sale. So Acme must have more houses than condominiums for sale.** — Weaker Match (16% of students)
This one is very close. There are two Most premises (if we hear the first two claims as "most houses for sale have a website photo" and "most condos for sale do not have a website photo"), and the conclusion is indeed a quantitative comparison. And this is also vulnerable to the "what if one group is much bigger" objection. If there are 1000 condos for sale, and only 100 houses for sale, then at least 51 of the houses would have photos on the site and at most 499 condos would have photos on the site. A matching conclusion, though, would have said, "So there must be more photos of houses on the website than there are photos of condominiums." This conclusion went one level farther by talking about the total number of houses / condos for sale. This argument is also vulnerable to a different kind of objection, one that isn't mathematical in nature, but Causal: Hey, author, maybe there aren't fewer condo photos on the website because there are fewer condos for sale but just because website pictures of condos aren't as sexy as website pictures of houses.

## S3 · Q26 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Film director: Although the production costs of my latest film are very high, there is little risk that the film studio will not recover these costs. Even if the film is unpopular, much of the money is being spent to develop innovative special-effects technology that could be used in future films.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The film studio will make its money back despite high production costs.
- **Evidence:** A significant portion of the film's budget is dedicated to developing special effects technology, which can be utilized in future films, reducing the risk of financial loss.
- **Evaluate:** The author seems to be saying, "Yes the studio is spending on lot on this film, but since part of that money will go towards inventing special FX technology that could be used in other films, they'll still recover their costs." So the author is envisioning that these FX we invent for this film save us money later (the studio pays less than they would to make a future film) or make us money later (some other studio pays us to use our FX technology).
- **Goal:** Identify an answer choice that raises doubts about the studio's ability to make its money back by exploiting the special effects technology, either by questioning the future utility or marketability of this FX technology.

**Answer choices**
**A. Because the film studio owns the new technology, the studio will be able to control its use in any future films.** — Opposite (Strengthens) (1% of students)
This choice says the studio will control the use of the special effects in future films, potentially allowing them to sell rights and make money back, thus reinforcing the author's argument.

**B. Films that introduce innovative special-effects technologies generally draw large audiences of people who are curious about the new effects.** — Opposite (Strengthens) (2% of students)
This suggests films with new special effects tend to have large audiences, indicating the innovation might attract viewers, aiding in cost recovery.

**C. The production costs of this film are so high that, even if the film is popular, it is unlikely that the film's ticket sales will offset those costs.** — Weaker Impact (8% of students)
This posits that high production costs mean even popularity won't allow ticket sales to recoup costs. While it's a valid concern, it misses the author's main point that future financial recovery hinges on technology use, not immediate box office success. The author is already acknowledging that the movie itself won't be enough to recoup costs, so we're not really hurting her argument by reminding her of that possibility.

**D. In the past, many innovative special-effects technologies were abandoned after the films for which they were developed proved to be unpopular.** — Correct (88% of students)
This indicates that technology developed for films that flop is often abandoned because it becomes undesirable. It directly challenges the assumption that the special effects technology will have value and provides a plausible reason for why the studio might not make its money back if the film is unpopular.

**E. The use of the new special-effects technology would lower the production costs of other films that use it.** — Opposite (Strengthens) (2% of students)
This states that using the new technology will lower production costs for other films, which supports the author's claim that the technology will eventually recoup the expenses by saving money in the long run.
