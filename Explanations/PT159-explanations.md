## S1 · Q1 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus

Columnist: The dangers of drinking are greatly exaggerated in the medical press. We are always hearing about the extent to which alcohol can shorten one's life, but my grandfather drank heavily his entire life and lived to be 95.

### Prompt

The reasoning in the columnist's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** The dangers of drinking are greatly exaggerated in the medical press.
- **Evidence:** The columnist's grandfather drank heavily his entire life and lived to be 95.
- **Evaluate:** The columnist tries to challenge a general claim about how alcohol shortens lives by citing one person who drank heavily and lived a long life. A single example cannot refute a population-level claim, the claim is about averages and tendencies, not about every individual.
- **Goal:** Find the answer pointing out that the columnist relies on a single anecdote to attack a general claim.

### Answer choices

**A. does not address moral objections to alcohol consumption** — Bad Objection (0% of students)
The columnist's argument is about whether the medical press exaggerates the dangers of drinking. Moral objections to alcohol are a separate topic. Even if the columnist also failed to address ethical concerns, that would not be the reasoning flaw, the reasoning flaw is jumping from one example to a general claim.

**B. fails to consider factors other than alcohol consumption that can shorten one's life** — Bad Objection (3% of students)
This says the columnist fails to consider other factors that shorten life. But the argument's direction is the opposite, the columnist is trying to defend alcohol as not dangerous, and other life-shortening factors would not undermine that defense; they would be consistent with it. Even if there were many other factors, the central problem is that the columnist relies on a single example to attack a general claim.

**C. only addresses the length of a life, and not the quality of that life** — Bad Objection (6% of students)
The argument is specifically about whether drinking shortens life, so addressing only length of life is exactly what the argument needs to address. Quality-of-life concerns are a separate issue and not the reasoning flaw being committed.

**D. confuses the cause of a phenomenon with an effect of that phenomenon** — Causal Flaw (4% of students)
The columnist is not confusing cause with effect. There is no causal reversal in the argument, the columnist is simply citing one long-lived heavy drinker. The flaw is the use of an anecdote to challenge a general statistical claim, not a cause-effect mix-up.

**E. relies merely on anecdotal evidence to challenge a general claim** — Correct (86% of students)
This identifies the flaw exactly. The columnist's evidence is one anecdote: my grandfather drank heavily and lived to 95. The columnist uses that single example to challenge a general claim, the medical press's claim that alcohol shortens lives. General claims about how alcohol affects health are statistical; one outlier does not refute them. That is precisely the flaw of relying on anecdotal evidence against a general claim.

## S1 · Q5 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus

Researchers gave each of eighteen subjects a playing card, then offered them money to lie to a computer about the identity of the card while undergoing a scan by functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). When subjects lied, the scans revealed increased activity in several regions of the brain known to be stimulated by stress. This supports the idea that fMRI can eventually form the basis of an effective lie detector.

### Prompt

Which one of the following would, if true, most weaken the argument above?

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion (this supports the idea):** fMRI can eventually form the basis of an effective lie detector.
- **Evidence:** When 18 subjects lied about a playing card, fMRI scans showed increased activity in regions of the brain known to be stimulated by stress.
- **Evaluate:** The evidence tells us that the fMRI picked up on activity in stress-related brain regions. The conclusion is claiming the fMRI is detecting a lie. So we need to assume that activity in these regions clearly signals a lie. The author seems to be thinking, "when you lie, you feel stress. When you feel stress, these regions become active. So if we detect these regions are active, then we've detected that you're lying." But does lying cause stress? If not, this test could provide false negatives, because people would be lying but it wouldn't detect them. And what if other things cause stress, even though the person is telling the truth? Or what if other things besides stress cause these brain regions to light up? If so, this test could provide false positives, because it might look like they're lying even though they aren't.
- **Goal:** Since we want to weaken, let's look for a way to say that this test wouldn't be reliable. It would provide false negatives or false positives.

### Answer choices

**A. Existing methods of lie detection, including the polygraph, have not been shown to be reliable.** — No Impact (1% of students)
The fact that other lie-detection methods are unreliable does not bear on whether fMRI in particular can become an effective lie detector.

We're judging this method on its own merits, not based on whether other methods succeeded or failed.

**B. The majority of people regard lying in an experimental situation as unethical.** — No Impact (0% of students)
What people think about the ethics of lying in experiments is irrelevant to whether fMRI can detect lies.

**C. The amount of stress that accompanies lying varies widely from person to person and is also affected by the circumstances in which the lie is told.** — Unclear Impact (9% of students)
This might mean that we sometimes have false negatives, because certain people in certain circumstances might have low enough stress when they lie for the test to detect them.

But this doesn't provide nearly as much punch as the correct answer.

**D. Telling a carefully planned lie causes less stress than telling a lie spontaneously.** — Weaker Impact (3% of students)
You could possibly stretch this into a story about false negatives, where the lie detector doesn't pick up on carefully planned lies because those lies don't cause enough stress to be detected.

But this isn't nearly as incriminating as the correct answer.

**E. Stress reactions in the brains of subjects who are being truthful are similar to those that occur in the brains of subjects who are lying.** — Correct (87% of students)
This hurts the most. How could this test, which is trying to detect lies based on stress reactions in the brain, be a reliable indicator of lying, if you get the same stress reactions when you tell the truth?

The signal it's looking for doesn't at all differentiate between truth-tellers and liars!

## S1 · Q7 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus

Critic: It generally holds true of concert pianists that the more famous the musician, the greater the pleasure of his or her audience. In my view, this is only fair and should give no cause for cynicism about the ability of audiences to distinguish good playing from bad.

### Prompt

Which one of the following, if true, most strongly supports the critic's view?

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** Audiences' greater pleasure at performances by more famous pianists is fair and gives no cause for cynicism about audiences' ability to distinguish good playing from bad.
- **Evidence:** It generally holds that the more famous a concert pianist, the greater the pleasure of his or her audience.
- **Evaluate:** There's a big language shift from the terms of the evidence to the terms of the conclusion. The evidence is talking about how famous a pianist is and how must pleasure the audience feels. The conclusion is talking about good the playing is. We probably want an answer to patch up these missing links. We could interpret this as Explain Curious Fact, where the author is trying to interpret why audiences get more pleasure from more famous musicians. In the author's mind, it's because the pianist is playing better. But maybe the audience just loves seeing someone famous.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer connecting "more famous, play better" or for an answer connecting "more pleasure, play better". Or let's otherwise look for any answer that suggests that audiences can distinguish good playing from bad playing, or one that rules out the idea that the audience's pleasure comes from some other source besides the quality of playing (such as from the fame of the pianist).

### Answer choices

**A. The fame of concert pianists usually reflects their talent as musicians.** — Correct (82% of students)
This connects "more famous" from the evidence to "good playing" in the conclusion.

The author uses the fact that people enjoy famous pianists more to argue that people can tell that the playing is good.

For this argument to work, we need to know that the famous pianists are actually playing well.

**B. Some of the finest concert pianists are unknown to the general public.** — Opposite (4% of students)
This is doing the opposite of (A). It's disconnecting fame and good playing.

Some of the "finest" are "unknown".

**C. In general, the more famous the pianist, the larger the audiences that he or she can draw.** — No Impact (8% of students)
The size of the audience is irrelevant to this argument.

We know audiences, of whatever size, get more pleasure from famous pianists. And we're trying to assess whether that means that people are getting pleasure from observing "good playing".

This answer doesn't help us assess that at all.

**D. Even the finest concert pianists cannot play consistently well at every performance.** — No Impact (2% of students)
This is a very weak idea saying that all pianists have some bad nights.

If anything, that would weaken the intended link between "famous pianist" and "good playing".

**E. The very best concert pianists often perform works that are difficult for audiences to appreciate.** — Opposite (4% of students)
The author's intended argument was that people enjoy famous pianists because they enjoy hearing good playing.

This storyline makes it seem like good playing, from "the very best pianists", isn't actually enjoyed by audiences ("it's hard for them to appreciate it").

## S1 · Q13 — Method

**Correct answer:** B

### Stimulus

A cognitive psychologist has claimed that intelligence is the ability to figure out how things work in order to overcome obstacles. If, however, we were to become convinced that a particular being had acquired an understanding of how things work, we would certainly not deny that that being possessed intelligence, even if we were sure that its acquisition of this understanding was not prompted by the desire to overcome some specific obstacle. Clearly, then, the cognitive psychologist's definition is inadequate.

### Prompt

In the passage the author does which one of the following?

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion (clearly then):** The cognitive psychologist's definition of intelligence is inadequate. rephrase: Intelligence is not the ability to figure out how things work in order to overcome obstacles.
- **Evidence:** In a situation where someone acquired an understanding of how things work, we'd say they were intelligent, regardless of whether they acquired that knowledge in order to overcome an obstacle.
- **Evaluate:** The three most common moves on Method are Analogies, Faulty Implications, and Alternatives. This somewhat has Faulty Implications vibes, because the author is essentially saying: If your definition was correct, then someone who acquired an understanding of how something works just for the sake of learning it (not in order to overcome an obstacle), would not be considered intelligent. However we DO consider those people intelligent. Thus, your definition is wrong.
- **Goal:** The author is showing that the definition is wrong because it isn't broad enough to encompass cases of intelligence that don't involve overcoming obstacles. Find any answer that says something descriptively true.

### Answer choices

**A. rejects a definition on the grounds that the terms employed in the definition would make it impractical to determine which cases it covers** — Bad Evidence Match (14% of students)
The author does reject a definition, but not because it would be impractical to figure out who the definition applies to.

It was because the definition wouldn't apply to certain cases that we correctly think of as showing intelligence.

**B. uses a hypothetical application of a definition to argue that the definition would have unacceptably counterintuitive consequences** — Correct (50% of students)
The author does present a hypothetical situation and tries to apply the psychologist's definition.

The definition would imply that this hypothetical being is NOT intelligent, whereas "we would certainly not deny that this being possesses intelligence".

This is a variation of Faulty Implications. "Suppose you were right, Opponent. If so, that would mean this [crazy / wrong / absurd / counterintuitive] implication. Thus, you must be wrong."

**C. argues that a proposed definition does not specify the meaning of the concept being defined narrowly enough for the definition to be applied** — Bad Evidence Match / Opposite (16% of students)
The author thinks that the definition isn't broad enough; it's TOO narrow.

It leaves out cases of intelligence that don't result from a desire to overcome obstacles.

**D. rejects a definition on the grounds that if it were accepted as correct, the term being defined would apply to things to which it normally does not apply** — Bad Evidence Match / Opposite (19% of students)
This says the definition would apply the term too broadly — to things "intelligence" doesn't normally apply to. But the author's argument runs the opposite way: the definition would exclude a case we'd normally include (the being that understands without an obstacle).

The author is saying that with the psychologist's definition, the defined term would not apply to things to which it normally does apply.

**E. argues against a proposed definition on the grounds that those who support it do so at least partly on an inappropriate appeal to authority** — Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
The author is arguing against a proposed definition, but the evidence has nothing to do with talking about those who support the definition.

The evidence is a hypothetical case offered by the author. There's no commentary about supporters leaning on some illicit appeal to authority.

## S1 · Q15 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus

Faculty member: The university's financially minded president holds that some academic programs should be eliminated because they do not serve student demands. According to him, the university is a business and the students are consumers, and it is the responsibility of any business to satisfy consumer demand. But the education of students is not analogous to the providing of consumer goods. Therefore, the president is mistaken in claiming that academic programs should be tailored to suit student demand.

### Prompt

The faculty member's argument is most vulnerable to the criticism that it

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion (therefore):** The president is mistaken in claiming that academic programs should be tailored to suit student demand. rephrase: Academic programs shouldn't be tailored to suit student demand.
- **Evidence:** The president supports his claim by saying that, like any business, a university has to satisfy his customers. The author says educating students isn't analogous to providing consumer goods.
- **Evaluate:** When we're doing Flaw and see that it's a Rebuttal style argument, there are two famous flaws we want to leap to mind: 1. Ad Hominem (discrediting a view on the basis that the SOURCE of the view has an ulterior motive or conflicting past behavior) 2. Unproven vs. Proven False (concluding that someone's claim is wrong on the grounds that they lack evidence to prove it's true or that they made a faulty argument in support of it. This argument is doing the 2nd. The faculty member shows that the president's reasoning for the view is bad — the analogy fails. From "the president's reasons are bad," the faculty member jumps to "the president's conclusion is wrong." That is a leap. A view can be correct even if the particular argument given for it is flawed.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer getting at this Unproven vs. Proven False flaw.

### Answer choices

**A. argues for a view without showing that an argument against that view fails** — Bad Conclusion/Evidence Match (19% of students)
The faculty member is not arguing for a positive view (e.g., "academic programs should be set without regard to demand"). The faculty member is only arguing against the president's view.

And the argument's evidence DID try to show why the president's argument fails.

A correct description would say that our author
"argues against a view by showing that the argument for it fails"

**B. appeals to popular opinion to justify a conclusion** — Bad Evidence Match (popular opinion) / Famous Flaw Bait: Inappropriate Appeals (1% of students)
The faculty member does not appeal to popular opinion. To appeal to X means to have X as part of your evidence.

The argument hinges on the disanalogy between education and consumer goods, not on what most people believe.

This answer describes a famous flaw, Inappropriate Appeals, in which you'd try to prove that something is true just because most people think it's true.

**C. treats merely analogous things as identical** — Opposite (if anything) (19% of students)
Our author is rejecting an analogy, saying school and business are very different.

This answer accuses her of thinking two things are so similar that they're identical.

**D. improperly attacks the university president's moral character** — Bad Evidence Match: moral character / Famous Flaw Bait: Ad Hominem (2% of students)
The evidence has nothing to do with the president's moral character.

Had the author tried to dismiss the president's argument on the basis of the president's character, that would have been the famous Ad Hominem flaw.

**E. rejects a view on the grounds that someone has given inadequate reasons for accepting that view** — Correct (59% of students)
If we're familiar with Unproven vs. Proven False, then we just see this answer as that. (note: on older LSATs, the Unproven part usually came from a lack of evidence, but on more recent LSATs the Unproven part usually comes from an inadequate argument -- the opponent was relying on some faulty notion that the author points out.)

Doing X on the grounds that Y means that Y should match the Evidence and X should look like the Conclusion (or how we got there).

Was the evidence saying that "someone had given inadequate reasons for accepting a view"?

Yeesh. Yes, implicitly. The view is that "academic programs should be tailored to suit student demand".

The reasons given for that view were that, "the university is a business, students are the consumers, and all businesses must satisfy consumer demand."

When our author says, "No, I don't accept that analogy. Education and Consumption are different", she's implying that she's not buying what the president is selling. The president's cited reasons are not adequate, not acceptable, not convincing, not valid.

Our author is allowed to be unconvinced about whether "academics should tailor to student demand", because that analogy didn't convince her. But she isn't allowed to be convinced the view is wrong and reject it.

## S1 · Q20 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** B

### Stimulus

It should be illegal to patent an organism's genes, for a person should not be regarded as the inventor of something he or she merely discovered. Moreover, exploitation of genetic information for profit is selfish and irresponsible.

### Prompt

Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the argument?

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** It should be illegal to patent an organism's genes.
- **Evidence (for):** A person should not be regarded as the inventor of something they merely discovered. Also, exploitation of genetic information for profit is selfish and irresponsible.
- **Evaluate:** We know that the outcome (right side) of the principle should be something that sounds like "should be illegal to patent". The available ideas for the trigger of the principle are - you discovered it, you didn't invent it - exploiting this is selfish and irresponsible.
- **Goal:** Let's look for something like "if discovered rather than invented, should be illegal to patent", or something like "if selfish and irresponsible to exploit for profit, should be illegal". But ultimately we just want a rule whose trigger applies to patenting genes and whose outcome helps us say "should be illegal to do it".

### Answer choices

**A. Any person who exploits genetic information for profit should be considered selfish.** — Bad Conclusion Match (7% of students)
The trigger matches the evidence, but we're trying to conclude "should be illegal" not "should be considered selfish".

**B. Discovering something should not entitle one to patent it.** — Correct (60% of students)
Short and sweet!

This says "if you're discovering something, then you shouldn't be entitled (on that basis) to patent it".

We were told that people looking to patent an organism's genes had merely discovered something; they hadn't invented anything.

So according to this rule, they shouldn't be entitled to patent an organism's genes. That's in the right direction of "it should be illegal", even if they're slightly different.

**C. Discovering something does not always constitute inventing it.** — Bad Conclusion Match (25% of students)
This rule is saying, "If discovered, then didn't invent".

The argument isn't trying to conclude "you didn't invent an organism's genes". It's trying to conclude "it should be illegal to patent an organism's genes".

**D. No person should be allowed to profit from something that was invented by another person.** — Bad Evidence/Conclusion Match (4% of students)
This rule says,
If someone else invented it, then you shouldn't be allowed to profit from it.

The trigger doesn't apply to an organism's genes, because no one invented them.

And the outcome doesn't match the conclusion, because we're trying to conclude "it should be illegal to patent", rather than "you shouldn't be able to profit from it".

**E. A person or corporation who invents something should be entitled to patent it.** — Negation (4% of students)
This says,
If you did invent something, you are entitled to patent it.

We wanted
If you didn't invent something, you aren't entitled to patent it.

## S3 · Q5 — Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus

Researcher: Some scholars maintain that the first government other than China to issue any form of paper currency was the Massachusetts colony in 1690. But these scholars are incorrect. During a coin shortage in New France in 1685, its government was unable to use coins to pay soldiers stationed in Quebec. Instead, playing cards were assigned an official value and issued as pay. These cards, which could be used to buy goods, were convertible to coins, which later arrived on supply ships from France.

### Prompt

The researcher's conclusion is properly drawn if which one of the following is assumed?

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** The scholars who say Massachusetts (1690) was the first non-Chinese government to issue paper currency are wrong.
- **Evidence:** In 1685, New France assigned official value to playing cards and issued them as pay to soldiers, convertible to coin later. So New France did this five years before Massachusetts.
- **Evaluate:** The scholars' claim is specifically about paper currency. To refute them, the New France case must qualify as paper currency. Playing cards function as currency in the stimulus (assigned value, used to pay soldiers, convertible to coin), but for the conclusion to follow, the playing cards have to also be made of paper. Without that, the New France case might not be a "paper currency" example, and the scholars' claim could survive.
- **Goal:** Find the assumption that makes the New France playing cards qualify as paper currency — i.e., that the cards were made of paper.

### Answer choices

**A. The playing cards in use in Quebec in the late 1600s were made of paper.** — Correct (84% of students)
This is the bridge from "playing cards" to "paper currency." The researcher needs the New France case to qualify as paper currency in order to undercut the scholars' specific claim. If the playing cards in late-1600s Quebec were made of paper, then assigning them official value and issuing them as pay constitutes issuing paper currency. With this assumption, the conclusion goes through: New France issued paper currency in 1685, before Massachusetts in 1690, so the scholars are wrong.

**B. The Chinese government did not issue paper currency during the 1600s.** — Out of Scope (2% of students)
The scholars' claim is about the first government other than China to issue paper currency. Whether China issued paper currency in the 1600s is irrelevant to whether New France or Massachusetts came first among non-Chinese governments. The argument doesn't need this assumption.

**C. Playing cards were not available to inhabitants of the Massachusetts colony before 1690.** — Out of Scope (3% of students)
Whether playing cards were available in Massachusetts before 1690 is irrelevant. The argument doesn't need to claim anything about Massachusetts's pre-1690 currency situation. It only needs the New France case to count as paper currency. Massachusetts's playing-card history is beside the point.

**D. The Massachusetts colony's issue of paper currency was not prompted by a coin shortage.** — Out of Scope (2% of students)
Massachusetts's reason for issuing paper currency (whether or not a coin shortage prompted it) doesn't affect whether New France issued paper currency earlier. The argument doesn't need to draw any contrast based on motives.

**E. Every government that issued paper currency in the 1600s left records pertaining to that currency.** — Out of Scope (8% of students)
Whether other 1600s governments left records about their currency is irrelevant. The argument is between two specific cases (New France 1685 and Massachusetts 1690). Universal record-keeping isn't needed; we just need the New France case to count.

## S3 · Q14 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** D

### Stimulus

Generally, open discussion of a false and pernicious idea diminishes its appeal. Even in a flourishing democracy, however, certain misconceptions are so alluring that they tend to gain in popularity when they are debated.

### Prompt

Which one of the following principles is most clearly illustrated by the statements above?

### Argument breakdown

- **Task:** The uncommon stem makes this question hard to categorize. "Which of the following principles" is common phrasing for both Principle Strengthen and Principle Conform, but "most clearly illustrated by the statements above" is more typical of Proposition / Generalization questions, a subtype of Most Supported. The task for that subtype is to extract a proposition / generalization / principle from the reading, which often feels like articulating the "moral of the story." That will be the task for this question.
- **Given:** Generally, open discussion of bad and dangerous ideas makes them less appealing. However, even in democracies, certain bad ideas are so attractive that they get more appealing when they are discussed openly.
- **Evaluate:** Proposition / Generalization questions often revolve around an important pivot word that creates tension in the stimulus. Our job is to find an answer that does the best job articulating that tension. Our first claim is something that is generally true: open discussion lessens the appeal of wrong ideas. After the pivot (however), we get tension in the form of an exception: some bad ideas are so appealing they get more attractive when debated.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that articulates that tension. Beware strong language, new ideas, and unsupported relationships. Lean towards the weakest, most provable answer.

### Answer choices

**A. Democratic governments should be allowed on occasion to suppress debate regarding certain misconceptions.** — Unsupported Recommendation: governments should (2% of students)
Whenever a Most Supported answer presents a recommendation, be skeptical! If the stimulus didn't say that specific party should act in that specific way, the answer is almost certainly wrong. Additionally, the stimulus expresses a tension surrouding open debate, but it doesn't suggest how we should respond to that tension. This answer is too speculative because it recommends a specific, unsupported response.

**B. Sometimes it is better to openly discuss an idea even though doing so will increase the popularity of a misconception.** — Unsupported Comparison: Sometimes it is better (14% of students)
Whenever a Most Supported answer presents a comparison, be skeptical! If the stimulus didn't compare those two specific things on that one specific metric, the answer is probably wrong. Additionally, this answer has a similar problem as Answer Choice A: the stimulus expresses a tension surrouding open debate, but it doesn't suggest how we should respond to that tension. This answer is too speculative because it suggests one response is better than some unnamed alternative.

**C. Not all pernicious ideas are misconceptions.** — Unsupported Relationship: pernicious ideas / misconceptions (4% of students)
The weak degree of "not all" is attractive in Most Supported questions because it only requires one data point to prove. So, we ask ourselves: does this stimulus prove that there is at least one pernicious idea that is not a misconception? Nope. The only pernicious idea mentioned in the stimlus is "false and pernicious" which is by definition a misconception. While Answer Choice C may be true in real life, that doesn't mean it is well supported by this stimulus.

**D. Discussion and debate do not necessarily lead to a greater acceptance of the truth.** — Correct (69% of students)
This articulates the tension expressed in the stimulus and sounds like the "moral of the story" we're being told.

**E. One of the reasons some ideas are appealing is that they are false.** — Unsupported Causal Relationship: false / appealing (12% of students)
The weak degree of "one of the reasons" and "some ideas" are both appealing in a Most Supported question because they only require one data point to prove. So, we ask ourselves: does this stimulus prove that there is at least one appealing idea that is appealing because it's false? Nope. We're told that certain false ideas are appealing, but we can't infer the Causal Relationship that they are appealing because of their falsity. Whenever a Most Supported answer presents a causal relationship, be skeptical and investigate it!

## S3 · Q16 — Role

**Correct answer:** A

### Stimulus

Some have argued that once it becomes necessary for us to think about computers in terms of beliefs and desires in order to understand how they function, we can rightly say that computers have beliefs and desires. However, we already need to think about what computers believe and desire in order to understand how they function, and thus the suggested criterion for ascribing beliefs and desires to computers implies that the computers that exist now have beliefs and desires. So the suggested criterion is obviously flawed.

### Prompt

The claim that the computers that exist now have beliefs and desires plays which one of the following roles in the argument above?

### Argument breakdown

- **Opposing Point (some have argued):** Once it becomes necessary for us to think about computers in terms of beliefs and desires in order to understand how they function, we can rightly say that computers have beliefs and desires.
- **Evidence (however):** We already need to think about what computers believe and desire in order to understand how they function.
- **Intermediate Conclusion (thus):** The suggested criterion for ascribing beliefs and desires to computers implies that the computers that exist now have beliefs and desires.
- **Main Conclusion (so):** The suggested criterion is obviously flawed.
- **Evaluate:** The claim in question is part of the Intermediate Conclusion. We know this claim to be a conclusion because of the word thus, and we know it's intermediate because the next sentence begins with the conclusion indicator word so. Since the claim in question is just one part of the intermediate conclusion, we should expect the correct answer to get more granular and describe how that claim works. That's unfortunate in a Role question, but it's to be expected in the Gauntlet where the hardest questions lie! The opposing point is establishes a criterion that, once met, means we can say that computers have beliefs and desires. The evidence is that we've already met that criterion. The intermediate conclusion is that, taken together, the opposing point and the evidence would mean that computers today have beliefs and desires. From this, the author derives their main conclusion: that criterion is obviously bogus. Thus, this argument has classic Rebuttal archetype known as the Reduction to the Absurd: If X was true, it would imply Y. But Y isn't true. So X isn't true either.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that acknowledges the Rebuttal and the author's assumption that computers today do not have beliefs and desires.

### Answer choices

**A. It is held to be an unacceptable conclusion to which the suggested criterion would lead.** — Correct (67% of students)
This perfectly reflects the Reduction to the Absurd archetype in which a claim is said to be false because it would lead to clearly false conclusions.

**B. It is a view the argument assumes is uncontroversial.** — Bad Description / Opposite (2% of students)
The author assumes this view is unacceptable, not uncontroversial.

**C. It is an example given to clarify the criterion in question.** — Bad Description: not a clarifying example (4% of students)
It is a consequence of the criterion, not an example of it.

**D. It is the conclusion of the argument.** — Bad Description: not the conclusion (3% of students)
The conclusion is the rebuttal of the opposing point.

**E. It is a premise whose truth is necessary to the success of the argument.** — Bad Description / Opposite (24% of students)
It is something that must NOT be true if the argument is going to be successful.

## S3 · Q17 — Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** E

### Stimulus

Manufacturer: If our biggest competitor were to go out of business, some of the specialized suppliers that we both use would be bankrupted. So, in order for us to stay in business, our biggest competitor must stay in business.

### Prompt

The conclusion drawn above follows logically if which one of the following is assumed?

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** In order for the manufacturer to stay in business, its biggest competitor must stay in business.
stay in ----------------------------→ biggest competitor
business stays in business
- **Evidence:** If the biggest competitor went out of business, some specialized suppliers shared by both companies would be bankrupted.
biggest competitor -------------------→ some spec supp
not stay in business shared go bankrupt
- **Evaluate:** When we compare our conclusion to our evidence, the overlapping term about the biggest competitor isn't lined up, so we need to either contrapose our evidence or our conclusion (doesn't matter).
If we contrapose the conclusion, we're trying to prove:
biggest comp ---------------------------------→ we not stay
out of business in business
What the evidence provided was
biggest comp ----→ some shared SS
out of business go bankrupt
So the missing piece we need to create the logic path in the conclusion is,
some shared SS --→ we not stay
go bankrupt in business
Those two pieces would combine to give us
"if biggest competitor goes out of business, then we go out of business".
- **Goal:** Our answer should be saying, "If some of our shared specialized suppliers go bankrupt, then we still not stay in business",
or its contrapositive, "In order for us to stay in business, our shared specialized suppliers can't go bankrupt".

### Answer choices

**A. The manufacturer's specialized suppliers will not be bankrupted.** — Unrelated to Goal (3% of students)
This would trigger the contrapositive of the first sentence and allow to logically conclude that "our biggest competitors will not go out of business".

But it doesn't help us conclude anything about "what we need in order for us to stay in business".

"In order for us to stay in business" is a New Concept in the Conclusion; it never appears in the evidence, so it definitely needs to be in the correct answer or else we'd never be able to combine answer + evidence and logically derive a conclusion about it.

**B. The manufacturer will stay in business if its biggest competitor stays in business.** — Reversal (12% of students)
This is just an illegal reversal of the conclusion.

CONC:
we stay in business → biggest comp stays in biz

(A)
biggest comp stays in biz → we stay in business

We wouldn't ever expect a correct answer to look like the rewording of an individual claim. We expect it to connect some language from one claim to some language from another claim.

**C. If the manufacturer goes out of business, its biggest competitor will go out of business.** — Negation (2% of students)
This is just an illegal negation of the conclusion.

CONC:
we stay in business → biggest comp stays in biz

(C)
we not stay in business → biggest comp not stay in biz

We wouldn't ever expect a correct answer to look like the rewording of an individual claim. We expect it to connect some language from one claim to some language from another claim.

**D. The manufacturer will stay in business if its specialized suppliers are not bankrupted.** — Negation (26% of students)
This is an illegal negation of what we wanted.

We wanted
if spec supplies go bankrupt → we go out of business

This says
if spec suppliers not go bankrupt → we stay in business

Our premise (contraposed) says
if spec supplies not go bankrupt → biggest comp stay in biz

This answer and the premise would have the same trigger. They can't interact. They can't chain together. They don't go anywhere by combining them.

If we have
A → B
and
A → C
we can't derive
B → C

**E. The manufacturer will stay in business only if its specialized suppliers are not bankrupted.** — Correct (57% of students)
This says (by contrapositive),
spec supp go bankrupt → we not stay in business

The premise said
biggest comp not stay in biz → spec supp go bankrupt

We can connect this answer to that premise to derive this chain:
biggest comp → spec supp → we not stay
not stay in biz go bankrupt in business

We can logically derive that
biggest comp ----------------→ we not stay
not stay in biz in business

Or, by contrapositive,
"For us to stay in business, it's required that our biggest competitor stay in business"

## S3 · Q18 — Parallel

**Correct answer:** C

### Stimulus

Jack's dog howls whenever a train goes by. Occasionally a train goes by while Jack is washing his dog. So Jack's dog must sometimes howl while he is washing it.

### Prompt

The pattern of reasoning in which one of the following arguments is most similar to that in the argument above?

### Argument breakdown

- **Conclusion:** Jack's dog must sometimes howl while he is washing it.
- **Evidence:** Universal premise: the dog howls whenever a train goes by. Particular premise: occasionally a train goes by while Jack is washing the dog.
- **Evaluate:** Structure: All trains-passing → dog howls. Some washing-events overlap with a train passing. Therefore some washing-events feature howling. The pattern is: all A produce B; some C overlaps with A; therefore some C produces B.
- **Goal:** Find an answer with the same structure: a universal "all X have property Y," a particular "some Z is X," and a conclusion "some Z has property Y."

### Answer choices

**A. Every serious jogger can benefit from good running shoes. But there must be a few serious joggers who prefer to run in ordinary sneakers, since some people who can benefit from good running shoes prefer to run in ordinary sneakers.** — Bad Conclusion Match (14% of students)
The conclusion here goes the wrong direction. The original concludes about the second group (washings) — some of them feature the property (howling). This answer concludes about the original group (joggers) — some of them prefer sneakers. The directions of the universal and the conclusion don't line up with the original.

**B. Most serious joggers can benefit from good running shoes. But some serious joggers prefer to run in ordinary sneakers. So some people who prefer to run in ordinary sneakers can benefit from good running shoes.** — Bad Evidence Match (6% of students)
This argument uses "most serious joggers can benefit," not a universal "all/whenever." The original premise is universal: the dog howls whenever a train goes by, with no exceptions. "Most" admits exceptions, which weakens the premise type. The structures don't line up.

**C. Any serious jogger can benefit from good running shoes. But a few serious joggers prefer to run in ordinary sneakers. So some people who can benefit from good running shoes prefer to run in ordinary sneakers.** — Correct (50% of students)
This matches the structure exactly. "Any serious jogger can benefit from good running shoes" is the universal — all joggers benefit. "A few serious joggers prefer to run in ordinary sneakers" is the existential — some joggers prefer sneakers. So those few joggers both benefit (from the universal) and prefer sneakers — meaning some people who can benefit from good shoes prefer sneakers. That parallels: all trains cause howling; some washings overlap trains; therefore some washings have howling. Same shape, valid in both cases.

**D. At least some serious joggers can benefit from good running shoes. But there are serious joggers who prefer to run in ordinary sneakers. So some people who prefer to run in ordinary sneakers can benefit from good running shoes.** — Bad Evidence Match (5% of students)
This uses "at least some serious joggers can benefit" — an existential, not a universal. The original premise is universal: every train going by causes howling. Without a universal premise, the argument cannot guarantee the conclusion in the way the original does.

**E. Any serious jogger can benefit from good running shoes. But some serious joggers occasionally prefer to run in ordinary sneakers. So anyone who can benefit from good running shoes occasionally prefers to run in ordinary sneakers.** — Bad Conclusion Match (24% of students)
The conclusion here is "anyone who can benefit occasionally prefers sneakers" — that is a universal conclusion ("anyone"). The original concludes only "some / sometimes." Promoting an existential premise into a universal conclusion is a different (and invalid) structure.

## S3 · Q19 — Most Supported

**Correct answer:** C

### Stimulus

When plants are growing quickly, they take more carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere than they release into it. But when they are growing very slowly, the reverse is true. Any substantial increase in the amount of carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere causes temperatures to increase in the tropics, where the bulk of Earth's plants are found. And the more temperatures increase in the tropics, the more slowly, on average, the plants there grow.

### Prompt

Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?

### Argument breakdown

- **Given:** When plants grow fast, they take more CO2 out of the atmosphere than they release. When plants grow very slowly, they release more CO2 than they take out. Any substantial increase in CO2 causes temperatures to increase in the tropics. The bulk of Earth's plants are in the tropics. The more temperatures increase in the tropics, the more slowly, on average, plants there grow.
- **Evaluate:** All the Causal statements should prompt us to look for opportunities to chain them together. Only claim 1 mentions fast plant growth and taking more CO2 out, so that one won't chain to anything else. Claim 2 tells us that very slow plant growth causes more CO2 to be released. That doesn't necessarily chain to claim 3, because releasing more CO2 doesn't necessarily create a substantial increase, but in cases where it does, it would increase the temperature in the tropics. That chains to the final claim, resulting in slower average plant growth.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that articulates all or part of our Causal Chain:
Very Slow Plant Growth > Releases CO2
Lots more CO2 > Increase Tropic Temps > Slower Ave. Growth of Tropics Plants
Beware answers that are too strong or that ignore important modifiers (substantial increase, very slow growth, etc).

### Answer choices

**A. The amount of carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere will inevitably increase at a gradually accelerating rate of speed.** — Too Strong: inevitably / Unsupported Quality: gradually accelerating (3% of students)
"Inevitably" is a red flag in Most Supported questions, especially those that are not testing Conditional Logic. We also don't have any information about anything "gradually accelerating."

**B. If the amount of carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere increases substantially, the bulk of Earth's plants will release more carbon dioxide into the atmosphere than they take out of it.** — Unsupported Causal Relationship (26% of students)
A substantial increase in CO2 emissions will increase the temperature in the tropics, and that is where the bulk of Earth's plant's live, so the first half of this answer sounds like it could be correct. However, in order to say that those plants will release more CO2 than they take out, we'd need to know that they are growing very slowly. And while a substantial increase in CO2 will cause higher tropic temperatures which will in turn slow the growth of plants there on average, we can't say that it will slow them to the point where they are growing very slowly. Thus, this answer is tempting, but incorrect.

**C. Plants in the tropics will grow more slowly, on average, if they release enough carbon dioxide to increase substantially the total amount of carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere.** — Correct (48% of students)
This reflects a link in our Causal Chain:
Lots more CO2 > Increase Tropic Temps > Slower Ave. Growth of Tropics Plants

**D. If the plants in the tropics begin to grow more slowly, on average, the temperatures in the tropics will increase substantially as a result.** — Reversal: last claim (12% of students)
This answer is just a reversal of the last claim in the stimulus.

**E. Any increase in temperatures in the tropics will be associated with a substantial increase in the amount of carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere.** — Reversal: second-to-last claim (11% of students)
This answer is just a reversal of the second-to-last claim in the stimulus.

## S3 · Q24 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** D

### Stimulus

Principle: An author of an article that has appeared in a magazine should be allowed to respond in that magazine to harsh criticism of the article if the criticism was published in the same magazine, or, alternatively, if the criticism was published in another magazine and is unfair.

Application: McFinton should be allowed to publish a response in Speculator's Digest to Wallace's most recent article, because Wallace's article is harshly critical of McFinton's essay on self-deception.

### Prompt

Which one of the following, if true, justifies the above application of the principle?

### Argument breakdown

- **Principle:**
criticism of article appears
in mag where orig published author of orig
or → article should be
criticism appears in another able to respond
magazine and is unfair in mag where criticized
- **Application:** McFinton should be allowed to publish a response in Speculator's Digest to Wallace's most recent article, because Wallace's article harshly criticizes McFinton's essay on self-deception.
- **Evaluate:** The way that all these Principle + Application questions work is that the Application concludes the right side of the principle, but it fails to establish the trigger of the principle.
The application is concluding that McF should be able to respond in Spec Digest to criticism of his article.
But we need to know a couple things:
1. Spec Digest is where Wallace's criticism was published
and then either
2a. McF's orig article was in Spec Digest
or
2b. Wallace's criticism is unfair
If the criticism is unfair, it doesn't matter where McF's article is published.
If McF's article was orig published in Spec Digest, it doesn't matter whether Wallace's criticism is fair or unfair.
For the principle to license a response in Speculator's Digest, McFinton's original essay must have appeared in Speculator's Digest (so that "that magazine" = Speculator's Digest). Then either Wallace's criticism must also have appeared in Speculator's Digest (condition a), or it must have appeared elsewhere and be unfair (condition b).
- **Goal:** Find an answer that pins down both: McFinton's essay was in Speculator's Digest, and Wallace's criticism was also in Speculator's Digest (or was elsewhere and unfair).

### Answer choices

**A. McFinton's essay on self-deception appeared in Speculator's Digest as well as in other magazines.** — Half Scope (5% of students)
This tells us McFinton's essay appeared in Speculator's Digest as well as other magazines, but it says nothing about where Wallace's criticism appeared.

Without knowing whether Wallace's article was in Speculator's Digest or was elsewhere and unfair the principle isn't fully triggered.

**B. Wallace's most recent article was published in Speculator's Digest as well as in other magazines.** — Half Scope (6% of students)
This tells us where Wallace's article appeared (Speculator's Digest, among others), but it does not tell us where McFinton's original essay appeared.

The principle only gives McFinton the right to respond in the magazine where McFinton's own essay was published. We don't know whether that magazine was Speculator's Digest.

**C. McFinton's essay on self-deception appeared only in Speculator's Digest, while Wallace's article has been published in a wide array of other magazines.** — Half Scope (5% of students)
This places McFinton's essay only in Speculator's Digest. Yay.

But we still need to know whether Wallace's critique also appeared in Spec Digest or whether it appeared elsewhere and was unfair.

**D. Both McFinton's essay on self-deception and Wallace's most recent article were published in Speculator's Digest.** — Correct (65% of students)
This gives us the two things we need to know.

McFinton's essay was published in Speculator's Digest, so that is where the principle says McF can respond to criticism.

And Wallace's harshly critical article was also published in Speculator's Digest — meaning the criticism appeared in the same magazine as the original article.

(It doesn't matter whether the criticism was unfair, if the criticism appeared in the same magazine as the original article.)

**E. Wallace's most recent article appeared in Speculator's Digest and was unfair in its harsh criticism of McFinton's essay on self-deception.** — Half Scope (20% of students)
This places Wallace's article in Speculator's Digest and adds that it was unfair, but it tells us nothing about where McFinton's original essay appeared.

If McFinton's essay never appeared in Speculator's Digest, the principle gives McFinton no right to respond there. It only allows the author of an article in a magazine to respond in that magazine to harsh criticism of the article.
