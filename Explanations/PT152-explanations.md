# Section 1 (Logical Reasoning)

## S1 · Q1 — Strengthen

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Researcher: During the rainy season, bonobos (an ape species closely related to chimpanzees) frequently swallow whole the rough-surfaced leaves of the shrub *Manniophyton fulvum*. These leaves are likely ingested because of their medicinal properties, since ingestion of these leaves facilitates the elimination of gastrointestinal worms.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the researcher's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** During the rainy season, bonobos swallow leaves of *Manniophyton fulvum*.

  Ingestion of these leaves facilitates the elimination of gastrointestinal worms.
- **Conclusion:** Bonobos likely ingest the leaves because of their medicinal properties.
- **Evaluate:** This argument is attempting to explain what causes bonobos to eat *M. fulvum* leaves. The evidence describes a correlation between two facts: Fact #1 is that the bonobos eat the leaves. Fact #2 is that the leaves help to eliminate worms inside the body.

  The conclusion jumps to a claim that this medicinal property of eliminating worms is what causes the bonobos to eat the leaves. But there are other, equally plausible reasons for the bonobos to eat the leaves.

  Maybe the leaves taste good. Maybe the bonobos like the rough texture of the leaves. The argument does nothing to rule out these alternatives. We haven't heard from a bonobo food critic or one with a medical degree. So we can't conclude with certainty that the bonobos are eating the leaves because of medicinal properties.
- **Goal:** Expect an answer to strengthen the argument by either providing more evidence that medicinal properties are causing the bonobos to eat the leaves, or by ruling out an alternative possibility.

**Answer choices**
**A. Bonobos rarely swallow whole leaves of any plants other than *M. fulvum*.** — No Impact (5% of students)
We have not been told if other plants do or do not have medicinal properties for bonobos. We have not been told about the effect that other leaves might have if bonobos swallowed them whole. Without more information, we can't say that this answer has any real impact on the argument.

**B. Chimpanzees have also been observed to swallow rough-surfaced leaves whole during the rainy season.** — Weaker Impact (1% of students)
If chimpanzees have a similar behavior that could potentially strengthen the argument. We're told that chimpanzees are similar to bonobos. But we haven't been told why chimpanzees swallow the leaves whole. We have to assume that the leaves that chimpanzees swallow have a similar medicinal effect, and assume that the chimpanzees swallow them for that reason. That makes this a pretty weak answer.

**C. Of the rough-leaved plants available to bonobos, *M. fulvum* shrubs are the most common.** — Opposite (if anything) (0% of students)
If this type of rough leaf is the most common one available to bonobos, that potentially weakens the claim that bonobos are eating them for a very specific reason. But we really need more information to know exactly how this answer impacts the argument.

**D. The leaves of *M. fulvum* are easier to swallow whole when they are wet.** — Opposite (if anything) (2% of students)
This gives a potential reason for the bonobos to eat the leaves during the rainy season, and it's a reason that has nothing to do with medicinal properties. If we want to dive deeper, this answer doesn't explain why the bonobos eat the leaves, so it's actual impact on the argument isn't clear.

**E. The rainy season is the time when bonobos are most likely to be infected with gastrointestinal worms.** — Correct (92% of students)
This provides another correlation that strengthens the argument. There's a correlation between the rainy season, when bonobos eat the leaves, and them having gastrointestinal worms. A correlation does not prove causation, but a correlation is potentially evidence of correlation. By providing another relevant correlation, this answer strengthens the argument.

## S1 · Q2 — Main Conclusion

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Policy analyst: Those concerned with safeguarding public health by reducing the risk of traffic fatalities typically focus their efforts on automotive safety measures such as increasing seat belt use, reducing distracted driving, and improving automotive technology. But what would contribute the most to safeguarding public health is a reduction in total miles traveled on our roads. The fact is that traveling by car is itself a major risk factor.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion drawn in the policy analyst's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** What would contribute the most to safeguarding public health is a reduction in total miles traveled on our roads.
- **Evidence:** Traveling by car is itself a major risk factor.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we do Main Conclusion or Role, we specifically listen for upside-down arguments, where the Conclusion appears earlier than the Evidence.

  The two dominant patterns on Main Conclusion are that the conclusion is
  - the First Sentence
  or
  - some Pivot / Rebuttal idea

  The first sentence of this argument isn't the author's opinion, so it won't be the conclusion. Instead, it's an attribution:
  Those concerned with X typically focus their efforts on Y.

  That would lead us to anticipate a Pivot / Rebuttal. Sure enough, we get the But to start the 2nd sentence. Is this claim an opinion from our author?

  Yes! She's saying what she thinks would contribute the most to public health.

  Is this claim Supported (as all conclusions must be)?

  Yes, the last sentence is a "fact" that supports it.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that captures the meaning of the 2nd sentence. It might take on more language that feels like the Rebuttal: "What would contribute the most to public health isn't auto safety measures but simply a reduction in total miles driven."

**Answer choices**
**A. Public health can be safeguarded through reducing traffic fatalities.** — Bad Match (1% of students)
This somewhat resembles the 2nd sentence, because it mentions "public health", but the 2nd sentence is specifically saying "the #1 thing we can do for safeguarding public health is reducing total miles traveled". This answer doesn't capture that part at all.

**B. Those concerned with safeguarding public health should focus their efforts on reducing traffic fatalities.** — Bad Match (1% of students)
This somewhat resembles the 2nd sentence, because it mentions "public health", but the 2nd sentence is specifically saying "the #1 thing we can do for safeguarding public health is reducing total miles traveled". This answer doesn't capture that part at all.

**C. Increasing seat belt use, reducing distracted driving, and improving automotive technology all safeguard public health.** — Opposite (0% of students)
This is more like the claim that the author is rebutting. This doesn't resemble the 2nd sentence at all.

**D. A reduction in total miles traveled would contribute more to safeguarding public health than would any automotive safety measure.** — Correct (92% of students)
This matches the meaning of the 2nd sentence, which was our main conclusion. It was an opinion from the author and it was supported by the 3rd sentence.

**E. Traveling by car is itself a major risk factor for traffic fatalities.** — Premise / Last-Claim Trap (6% of students)
This is the 3rd sentence, which was a premise. We don't expect the last claim to be the conclusion on Main Conclusion or Role. It will be sometimes, but we should always second guess ourselves if we're picking the last claim, because it's a common trap answer that students walk into out of force of habit.

For any other question type besides Main Conclusion / Role, the most likely place for the conclusion is the final claim.

## S1 · Q3 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Letter to the Editor: The arts section of this paper shows a lamentable bias toward movies and against local theatrical productions. Over the last year alone, the paper has published over five times as many movie reviews as reviews of live plays.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (lamentable = opinion):** The arts section of this paper shows a lamentable bias toward movies and against local theatrical productions.
- **Evidence:** Over the last year alone, the paper has published over five times as many movie reviews as reviews of live plays.
- **Evaluate:** The evidence involves a Curious Comparison, and the conclusion is offering an explanation for that asymmetry, so we can use our Explain Curious Fact mindset.

  Curious Fact … How come the paper publishes five times as many movie reviews as reviews of live plays?

  Author's Explanation … The people who run the arts section are biased towards movies. They prefer movies over plays, so they choose to publish more movie reviews because they would rather write about and publish stuff that they like.

  We always ask ourselves

  1. Could there be an Alternate Explanation for the curious fact?

  2. How plausible is the Author's Explanation?
- **Goal:** We can either hurt the argument by suggesting an Alternate Explanation for why there are five times as many reviews of movies as of live plays (maybe there are way more movies than plays / maybe the readers of the newspaper are five times more interested in movies than in plays), or hurt it by making it seem less plausible that the editors of the arts section prefer movies to plays and thus publish far more reviews of movies than of plays.

**Answer choices**
**A. Some newspapers do not publish any reviews of live plays.** — Too Weak / No Impact (0% of students)
This says that "In the world, there exists at least one newspaper that doesn't publish any reviews of live plays". Okay, cool, but that newspaper doesn't have anything to do with the conversation we're having about this newspaper, which does publish some reviews of live plays.

The word "some" is almost always too weak to be a correct answer on Strengthen, Weaken, Paradox, and Sufficient Assumption.

**B. The number of movies released last year was significantly greater than the number of live plays performed locally.** — Correct (91% of students)
This provides an Alternate Explanation for why there are way more reviews of movies than of plays. "It's not because the editors of the arts section have a bias towards movies; it's just because there are way more movies to review than there are plays to review."

On Weaken (and Flaw, Necessary Assumption, Evaluate), the correct answer on an Explain Curious Fact style argument usually provides an alternate explanation for the curious fact.

Meanwhile, on Strengthen, a correct answer is more likely to boost the plausibility of the author's explanation for the curious fact.

**C. The newspaper has five movie critics, but only one theater critic.** — Strengthens, if anything (5% of students)
This feels like an Alternate Explanation for why there are five times as many reviews of movies vs. plays:

**D. The newspaper does not have the space in the arts section to publish a review of every movie that is released or every live play that is locally performed.** — Too Weak (1% of students)
This would only hurt the author if she had been thinking (implausibly, since this is an insane notion) that "the newspaper does have the space to publish a review of every single movie and play released or performed".

She wasn't thinking any crazy thought like that, so this answer can't hurt her. She wasn't arguing that every play / movie should be reviewed. She just seems to expect an equal number of plays / movies would be reviewed, if the arts editors were unbiased.

**E. The newspaper published more reviews of live plays in the last year than it did two years ago.** — No Impact (3% of students)
While this somewhat goes against the plausibility of the Author's Explanation ("It doesn't sound like the arts section has a bias that favors movies over plays; after all, they reviewed more plays last year than they did the year before.")

But it has very little impact, because even if the number of live plays reviewed has increased, it's still only 1/5 the number of movies that get reviewed.

## S1 · Q4 — Main Conclusion

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Archaeologist: Our university museum possesses several ancient artifacts whose ownership is in dispute. Although the museum has documentation showing that the items were obtained legally, there is an overriding principle that any important ancient artifact belongs by rights to the nation on whose territory it was discovered. Given that an institution is obliged to honor those rights, our museum should return the artifacts.

### Prompt
Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion of the archaeologist's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Background:** Our university museum possesses several ancient artifacts whose ownership is in dispute.
- **Opposing Idea (Although):** The museum has documentation showing that the items were obtained legally.
- **Evidence (Given that):** There is an overriding principle that any important ancient artifact belongs by rights to the nation on whose territory it was discovered.

  An institution is obliged to honor those rights.
- **Conclusion:** Our museum should return the artifacts.
- **Evaluate:** A few clues help us identify the parts of this argument. One of the most helpful ones is the phrase "given that" at the beginning of the final sentence. "Given that" is a premise indicator, and this sentence follows a common pattern:

  Premise Indicator — Premise — Comma — Conclusion

  "Given that" is followed by the premise about an institutions obligation. That statement is followed by a comma, which is followed by the argument's conclusion. It also helps to note that this final statement is a recommendation: "the museum should return the artifacts." A recommendation will commonly be the conclusion of an argument.

  It helps to be sure that nothing else in the argument is a conclusion that we somehow overlooked. The premise we just mentioned speaks of an obligation "to honor those rights." What rights? The ones mentioned in the preceding sentence, the rights a nation has to important artifacts discovered on their territory.

  The word "although" at the beginning of the second sentence is a hint that the conclusion will eventually refute or at least pivot away from this statement. That's exactly what happens: although the museum has documentation showing that the items were obtained legally, our museum should return the artifacts.

  The very first sentence seems like a statement of fact, so it's not likely to be a conclusion. After reading the rest of the statements, it appears to be background information. It provides context, but by itself is not the conclusion.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that mirrors the final statement in the argument. Beware of answers that describe other parts of the argument, like premises.

**Answer choices**
**A. The university museum should return the ancient artifacts in dispute.** — Correct (94% of students)
This mirrors the final statement in the argument. It refers back to the first sentence, which states that the museum is a university museum and that the artifacts are ancient ones whose ownership is in dispute.

**B. Any important ancient artifact belongs by rights to the nation on whose territory it was discovered.** — Premise (2% of students)
This is a premise that is used to support the conclusion.

**C. The ancient artifacts whose ownership is in dispute were obtained legally by the university museum.** — Opposing Idea (0% of students)
The fact that the artifacts were obtained legally (or at least the university has documents stating so) is an opposing idea that the argument pivots away from. It's not the conclusion of the argument.

**D. The university museum is in possession of several artifacts whose ownership is in dispute.** — Background (1% of students)
This is background information that provides context for the argument. It's not the conclusion.

**E. There is an overriding principle that any important ancient artifact belongs by rights to the nation on whose territory it was discovered.** — Premise (3% of students)
This is a premise that is used to support the conclusion.

## S1 · Q5 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Many fictional works have characters who are supposedly precognitive—that is, able to accurately perceive future events. But a perception of a future event is accurate only if that event comes to pass. Thus, the plots of these works often show that the characters are not truly precognitive, since some of the future events the characters perceive do not in fact come to pass.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption on which the argument depends?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The plots of these books show that these characters are not precognitive.
- **Evidence:** Precognitive means "able to accurately perceive future events", and accurately perceiving future events means "your prediction comes to pass", but some of the supposed precog's predictions do not come to pass.
- **Evaluate:** Given That some of their predictions don't come to pass,
  How Can We Still Argue they are correctly portrayed as precognitive?

  Maybe we could say that 90% of the time they're right? If someone were able to predict the future accurately 90% of the time, the fact that they were sometimes wrong wouldn't keep me from saying, "That fool has some precognition."
- **Goal:** Look for an answer suggesting, "if you make any mistakes, then you're not to be counted as precognitive", or something like "being correct about predictions the vast majority of the time would not be enough to be appropriately called precognitive".

**Answer choices**
**A. A character is truly precognitive only if all of that character's perceptions of future events are accurate.** — Correct (86% of students)
Since this answer is conditional ("only if" = necessary), we can put it into conditional logic and ask ourselves whether it matches a move the author made.

```
Truly precognitive → all perceptions accurate
```

Since the conclusion was "not truly precognitive", we should contrapose this answer.

```
some perceptions   → not truly
not accurate         precognitive
```

Does that match our author's logic?

Yes, that's exactly the move she made from premise to conclusion. The art / skill in seeing this answer choice for what it is resides in knowing your conditional words (only if), knowing how to contrapose, and knowing that you always want conditional rules to match up Premise → Conclusion.

**B. It is impossible for someone to perceive future events accurately with absolute consistency.** — Too Strong: impossible (1% of students)
The author's argument is only about the characters in these fictional works. She doesn't take any stance on whether precognition is ever possible in real life.

**C. The plots of fictional works that portray characters as precognitive often do not specify whether the future events those characters perceive come to pass.** — Out of Scope: do not specify (8% of students)
The author didn't discuss any cases in which we never find out whether or not someone's predictions came to pass. Since the author never talked about this, she doesn't need to assume anything about it.

Her argument is only concerned with cases in which we do find out whether future events predicted have come to pass (we find out they haven't past, calling into question that character's precognition skills).

**D. When fictional works portray characters as precognitive, those characters' perceptions of future events are generally central to the plots of those works.** — Too Strong/Specific: generally central (3% of students)
It doesn't matter to the author's argument whether these "fake" clairvoyants are central characters or minor characters, whether their predictions are major events or minor events in the plot.

**E. No work of fiction has portrayed a truly precognitive character.** — Too Strong: no work ever (1% of students)
The author's conclusion is merely "the plots of these works often show characters who aren't truly precognitive". She doesn't have to assume 'truly precognitive' never happens.

## S1 · Q6 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Economist: There have been large declines in employment around the globe, so it's not surprising that the number of workers injured on the job has decreased. What is surprising, however, is that the percentage of workers injured on the job has also decreased.

### Prompt
Each of the following, if true, helps to explain the surprising result mentioned by the economist except:

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Background:** Since there have been declines in employment (fewer jobs) around the globe, the number of workers injured has gone down.
- **Surprising Claim:** The percentage of workers injured on the job has gone down.
- **Evaluate:** This isn't quite a paradox in the sense that the background information doesn't really make the surprising claim more or less likely. It should just be an irrelevant factor.

  All other things being equal, whether you have more jobs or fewer jobs, the percent of workers injured would stay the same.
- **Goal:** We just need four answers that explain why a higher proportion of workers are getting injured.

  - perhaps the jobs that remain are the more dangerous ones / the jobs we lost were the safer ones

  - perhaps the employees that remain with jobs are the more injury-prone ones / the employees that got fired were the safer ones

**Answer choices**
**A. Overall, people who are employed are working fewer hours each day.** — Explains Lower Percentage (3% of students)
If people who have jobs are working fewer hours, on average, then that should bring the injury rate down. The less you're at work, the less you're at risk of being injured at work.

**B. A decrease in the demand for products has reduced the pressure on workers to meet production quotas and deadlines.** — Explains Lower Percentage (3% of students)
When there's less pressure to meet production quotas and deadlines, people can work in a calmer, safer fashion.

Injuries often occur when we're tired, distracted, stressed, or hurrying.

**C. Some of the most dangerous industries have had especially big declines in employment.** — Explains Lower Percentage (4% of students)
If a lot of the jobs that were lost were in dangerous industries, then the remaining pool of jobs is "safer" than the old pool of jobs.

**D. There has been a general decline in the resources devoted to workplace safety.** — Deepens Paradox (85% of students)
This doesn't explain why the injury rate went down. This sounds like a reason why the injury rate should be going up!

Less spending on workplace safety would generally mean a less-safe workplace.

**E. Inexperienced workers have lost their jobs at higher rates than experienced ones.** — Explains Lower Percentage (5% of students)
If more of the lost jobs were for inexperienced workers, the remaining pool of workers is more experienced than before.

Is there a common sense connection between experience and avoiding injury?

Yes. If you think about jobs that are likely to have injuries in the first place (manufacturing, construction, building maintenance), it's easier to imagine a "rookie" getting injured than a savvy veteran.

## S1 · Q7 — Sufficient Assumption

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Editorial: Animated films appropriate for children are those that are innocently whimsical, mischievous perhaps, but not threatening. Since new animated films aimed at adults have dark themes such as poverty and despair, such films cannot be considered appropriate for children.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption that would allow the conclusion to be properly drawn?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence (Since):** Animated films appropriate for children are not threatening.

  New animated films aimed at adults have dark themes such as poverty and despair.
- **Conclusion:** Such films cannot be considered appropriate for children.
- **Evaluate:** Huh. I was going to watch an episode of Love, Death & Robots with my niece. I guess we'll watch Shrek instead.

  There isn't a conclusion indicator in this argument, but the final sentence follows a common form:

  Premise Indicator - Premise - Comma - Conclusion

  The premise indicator "since" is followed by a premise. That premise is followed by a comma, which is followed by the argument's conclusion.

  If we just look at this premise and the conclusion, we can see a missing link. The premise describes new animated films aimed at adults, but doesn't make any claims about them being appropriate or inappropriate for anyone. The conclusion jumps to a claim about these films not being appropriate for children. "Not appropriate for children" is a new idea in the conclusion.

  The conclusion would follow logically if we connect the information in the premise to the new idea in the conclusion with an assumption like this:

  "Films with dark themes are not appropriate for children."

  However, there's a second assumption to consider. The first sentence in the stimulus connects directly to the conclusion: it's about films being appropriate for children. Because it connects directly to the conclusion, we can treat it as a premise. This sentence states that animated films appropriate for children are not threatening. That's a conditional statement:

  ```
  appropriate for kids → ~threatening
  ```

  The contrapositive of this is that threatening films are not appropriate for children.

  ```
  threatening → ~appropriate for kids
  ```

  We already know that new animated films aimed at adults have dark themes. If we assume that "dark themes are threatening," and combine this with the two existing premises, that would also allow the conclusion to follow logically:

  New animated films aimed at adults have dark themes such as poverty and despair.
  Dark themes are threatening.
  Threatening films are not appropriate for children.
  Therefore, new animated films aimed at adults are not appropriate for children.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer which states that dark themes are not appropriate for children or that dark themes are threatening. Beware of answers that are illegal reversals or illegal negations of one of these assumptions.

**Answer choices**
**A. Films that are whimsical and mischievous are not threatening.** — Premise Support (3% of students)
The first premise already draws a distinction between whimsical or mischievous films and threatening ones. This answer just supports that distinction. It doesn't directly connect any of this information to the conclusion.

**B. Films that are appropriate for adults are seldom appropriate for children.** — Out of Scope / Too Weak: seldom (4% of students)
The argument mentions films that are "aimed at adults." That doesn't necessarily mean that these films are "appropriate for adults." Some people would argue that certain films aren't appropriate for anyone.

Also, to make the conclusion 100% follow logically, we need an answer to state that something is not appropriate for children. Anything that is "seldom appropriate" won't completely guarantee the conclusion.

**C. Films that have dark themes are threatening.** — Correct (92% of students)
This takes the information in the second premise, which states that new animated films aimed at adults have dark themes, and connects it with the first premise, which states that threatening films are not appropriate for children.

**D. Children enjoy films only if the films include animation.** — Out of Scope: enjoy / Unrelated to Goal (0% of students)
The argument does not include any statements about what children enjoy or don't enjoy. An assumption that focuses on this will not have an impact on the argument. This answer also fails to describe anything as threatening, or as not appropriate for children.

**E. Children do not attend to some details in films aimed at adults.** — Out of Scope: attend / Unrelated to Goal (1% of students)
The argument does not address the idea of what children "attend to." The argument focuses on the types of movies that are appropriate for children, regardless of whether or not the children are paying attention. This answer choice does not have an impact on the argument. This answer also fails to describe anything as threatening, or as not appropriate for children.

## S1 · Q8 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Monarch butterflies must contend with single-celled parasites that can cause deformities that interfere with their flight. In populations of monarch butterflies that have not migrated, as many as 95 percent are heavily infected by the parasites, while less than 15 percent of those in migrating populations are infected. This shows that migrating allows monarch butterflies to avoid these parasites.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the argument is flawed in that the argument overlooks the possibility that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (this shows that):** Migrating allows monarch butterflies to avoid these single-celled parasites that can cause deformities that interfere with their flight.
- **Evidence:** In populations that have not migrated, up to 95% are infected by parasites. In populations that do migrate, less than 15% are infected.
- **Evaluate:** The evidence is look at an asymmetric comparison (i.e. a Curious Fact) and concluding a causal interpretation (migrating causes monarchs to avoid these parasites).

  As always, we're supposed to react to this on Flaw primarily by considering Alternative Explanations for the curious fact.

  Whenever the author presents a correlation, "Those who migrate are less likely to be infected by the parasites" and then concludes that the first things causes the second "migration causes them to avoid parasites", we have two alternate explanations we always consider:

  Reverse Cause? (could it be that avoiding parasites allows them to migrate? That could make sense. If you're infected by the parasite, it causes deformities that interfere with flight, so you wouldn't be able to migrate. That would explain why there's a correlation between infected and non-migrating. The infection comes first and prevents the migration!)

  Third Factor? (could there be some 3rd factor we haven't identified yet that both increases the likelihood of migrating and decreases the likelihood of parasite infection?)
- **Goal:** Because this question stem is prefaced with "fails to consider / overlooks the possibility", we basically want to treat these answers like Weaken.

  The correct answer could attack the author's causal conclusion by suggesting an Alternate Explanation for the correlation between migrating and having low incidence of parasite infection. Or it could attack the plausibility of the author's story that migrating allows monarchs to avoid these parasites.

  But the Reverse Cause alternate explanation seems highly likely, since it would be very logical to interpret these correlations as "the flight-debilitating infection came first, and that caused the inability to migrate".

**Answer choices**
**A. monarch butterflies are unable to detect which areas are free from parasites** — No Impact (6% of students)
This doesn't provide an Alternate Explanation for why there's a correlation between migrating and not-being-infected with parasites.

This also doesn't say anything that hurts the plausibility that migration helps the monarchs to avoid these parasites.

**B. long migrations are no better protection from parasites than are short migrations** — Unclear Impact (1% of students)
Does this hurt the plausibility of the idea that migrations allow monarchs to avoid these parasites?

Not really. Do the migrations need to be of a certain length in order to help protect the butterflies? It's not clear what length that would be or why that would make a difference in terms of parasite-protection.

There might be one month (let's say January) when the parasites thrive. If a butterfly has migrated away during January, it will be protected from the parasites, whether that butterfly is gone for a short 3 month migration or for a long 6 month migration.

**C. populations of monarch butterflies that have not migrated are much larger than migrating populations** — No Impact (9% of students)
The relative size of these populations doesn't matter.

If we find out that 95% of left handed people get a disease X, while less than 15% of right handed people get disease X, we have good reason to suspect that disease X is connected to the genes that result in left-handedness.

It doesn't make any difference that there are 8x as many right handed people in the world.

**D. monarch butterflies infected with parasites are typically unable to migrate** — Correct (83% of students)
This provides the reverse cause Alternate Explanation we anticipated.

It's not that migrating allows monarchs to avoid these parasites. It's that avoiding these parasites allows monarchs to migrate.

We see arguments like, "People who attend exercise classes tend to be in better health than those who don't. Thus, exercise classes help improve your health", and LSAT wants us to consider the alternate, reverse cause interpretation of that correlation: maybe being healthy allows someone to attend an exercise class.

**E. populations of monarch butterflies tend not to migrate if they have stable food sources** — No Impact (1% of students)
Why populations do or don't tend to migrate doesn't matter to us, unless the basis for that decision relates to whether or not a butterfly population would have significant exposure to the parasites.

But there's no common sense connection between whether or not a population has a stable food source and whether or no they're exposed to this parasite.

## S1 · Q9 — Principle-Strengthen

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Legal doctrine: The government cannot appropriate private property without offering fair compensation to the property owner.

Application: If the government institutes a regulation that blocks construction on undeveloped private lots on the shore of Lake Crowell—thereby diminishing their market value—it must offer fair compensation to the owners of that property.

### Prompt
Which one of the following principles, if valid, most justifies the above application of the legal doctrine?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Doctrine:** If the government appropriates private property, they need to offer fair compensation to the owner.

  1. Make the Author's Explanation more plausible
  2. Rule out an Alternate Explanation for the curious fact

  What could be an alternative way to explain why most of the diagnosed strokes were left-side?

  - maybe there were just more strokes on the left-side
  - maybe the doctors were more motivated for some reason to look for strokes on the left-side

### Stimulus

**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** You'll never be able to completely forgive yourself.
- **Evidence:** You'll never be able to completely understand yourself,
  and understanding a person leads to completely forgiving that person.
- **Evaluate:** This got pretty deep. Once you return from staring into the void of your own self-reflection, questioning whether you completely understand yourself and whether you'll ever [gulp] completely forgive yourself, we'll talk about the argument.


  ```
  PREMISE          →  CONCLUSION
  Can't understand    Can't forgive
  yourself fully.     yourself fully.
  ```

  That's an illegal opposite move, which goes by the name of Necessary vs. Sufficient.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer describing the Necessary vs. Sufficient error, or one pointing out that even though we can't understand ourselves fully (which is one way to get complete forgiveness), there might be some other method we can use that also leads one to forgive themselves entirely.

condition that brings about an outcome = SUFFICIENT
the only way to realize the outcome = NECESSARY

Understanding oneself fully would bring about the particular outcome of completely forgiving oneself. But the author treats "understanding oneself fully" like it's the only way to completely forgive oneself, because she thinks "since we can't fully understand ourselves, we'll never fully forgive ourselves".

**B. confuses something that is necessary for an action to occur with something that necessarily results from that action** — Out of Scope: necessary for action to occur (14% of students)

Conditionally,
Admitted to law school → Applied to law school

Conditionally,
Admitted to law school → Homework

Either way you slice it, "Admitted to law school" is on the left. This isn't describing a conditional error where an author is treating a Necessary like a Sufficient or vice versa.

In this argument was there anything "necessary for an action to occur"?

Nope. So since we can't even match up the first concept with the argument, we can eliminate the answer.

**C. takes for granted that something that has merely been said to be true is, in fact, true** — Wrong Flaw (6% of students)
This answer is testing a distinction between Opinion and Fact. There is an opinion given in the first sentence: it has been said that X.

Nothing in the argument discusses whether or not we should try to attain complete understanding. ## S1 · Q16 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
A popular complaint about abstract expressionist paintings—that "a child could paint that"—holds that their stylistic similarities to young children's paintings show that they are no more aesthetically pleasing than those inexpert works. But most participants in a psychological study, when shown pairs of paintings consisting of an abstract expressionist painting and a preschooler's painting, consistently rated the abstract expressionist painting as aesthetically better, refuting this complaint and thereby establishing that abstract expressionist paintings are aesthetically pleasing.

### Prompt
The argument depends on assuming which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Main Conclusion:** Abstract expressionist paintings are aesthetically pleasing.
- **Intermediate Conclusion:** The complaint that "abstract expressionism is no more aesthetically pleasing than young children's paintings" is refuted. (abstract expressionism is more aesthetically pleasing than kids' paintings)
- **Premise:** In a study, most participants consistently rated abstract expressionist stuff more aesthetically pleasing than kids' paintings.
- **Evaluate:** It's not crucial that we see the conclusion as an Intermediate Conclusion and thereby a Main Conclusion. But it is important we think about both of the claims that conclusion is making.

  - Have they actually refuted the complaint? Have they shown that abstract expressionism is more aesthetically pleasing than kids' paintings, given that most people in this study thought so?

  No, we could still fight. We could say something was wrong with the study. The quality of abstract stuff was better than average, and/or the quality of kids' paintings was worse than average. Or the people were unrepresentative. They were full of art history majors who could tell the difference.

  - Have they proven that abstract expressionism is aesthetically pleasing. Wait, didn't we just adjudicate this? How is this different from the previous claim?

  The previous claim was that "A.E. is more aesthetically pleasing than kids' art is."
  This claim is that "A.E. is aesthetically pleasing."

  If you're better than a kid at chess, does that mean you're good at chess?
  If you're stronger than a kid, does that mean you're strong?
  If you're wealthier than a kid, does that mean you're wealthy?

  This is the ol' Relative vs. Absolute distinction that LSAT is constantly testing.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer protecting the integrity of the study (i.e. "the samples of A.E. and kids' paintings were representative quality of those categories), or an answer dealing with the shift from "if it's more aesthetically pleasing than X, then it's aesthetically pleasing".

**Answer choices**
**A. People are better at judging the aesthetic value of a painting when they compare it with another painting.** — Irrelevant Comparison (20% of students)
Nothing in the argument dealt with the distinction of "judging a painting by itself vs. judging a painting by comparing to another".

Would negating this become an objection? No, the author would not be bothered if people were just as good at judging the aesthetic value of a painting when they saw it alone as they were when they saw it compared to another painting.

**B. Most of the preschoolers' paintings used in the study were not aesthetically displeasing.** — Correct (52% of students)
This is a weird correct answer, but it has the attractive feature (on Necessary Assumption) of saying something was not the case. These are the moments when we're most likely to profit from trying the Negation test.

If we said, "Hey, author most of the kids' paintings were aesthetically displeasing", would that be an objection?

Yes, because it addresses the Relative vs. Absolute switch. It's saying, "Sure, most people in the study thought that the abstract expressionism was more pleasing than the kids' paintings, but the kids' paintings were garbage. So the study proves that abstract expressionism is more pleasing than garbage. That doesn't prove that abstract expressionism is pleasing."

There would be very little chance of understanding this answer if we didn't spot the Relative vs. Absolute shift to that final conclusion.

### Stimulus

### Prompt
The reasoning in the researcher's argument is questionable in that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Having a medical self-help book improves family health.
- **Evidence:** In a study where 500 families were given a medical self-help book and 500 weren't, the group that was given a book visited the doctor 20% less than the group that didn't wasn't given a book.

  Improved health leads to fewer doctor visits.
- **Evaluation:** This argument has a little bit of an Illegal Backwards move. We are given a conditional,
  Improved family health → fewer doctor visits

  The author sees that the book-group of families had fewer doctor visits, and then concludes/assumes that the book-group of families had improved family health. That's an illegal backwards move.

  The author also assumes that the improved family health comes from the self-help book (when it could have come from other places).

  This argument also has some Curious Fact / Author's Explanation elements to it.
  Curious Fact: Why did the book-group visit the doctor 20% less than did non-book group?
  Author's Story: The book led to improved family health, which led to fewer doctor visits.

  When we see a causal argument structure on Flaw, we think, "The author might be overconfidently concluding one possible causal explanation, when there could be others".

  How else could you explain why the book-group visited the doctor 20% less?
  - it may have nothing to do with the book. Perhaps more families in that group just happened to have started doing healthier things, like better diet or more exercise.

  - it may have something to do with the book, but not "improved family health". Maybe they used the book to self-diagnose and find treatment options, so their health wasn't any better than the non-book group, but they didn't as frequently feel the need to go see a doctor in order to get diagnosed and prescribed some sort of treatment.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer talking about the illegal backwards move or an answer pointing out that there could be other explanations for why the book-group went to the doctor 20% less.

**Answer choices**
**A. it is possible that the families in the experiment who were not given a medical self-help book acquired medical self-help books on their own** — Not Our Biggest Gripe (9% of students)

Ultimately, this answer is just less direct of a criticism than the correct answer is. The illegal backwards move and the failure to consider alternate explanations for why the book-group had 20% fewer visits are the two big reasoning flaws.

This objection is kind of just nitpicking at a premise, making it seem like there wasn't a difference between the two groups in the first place. That doesn't provide a different way to explain the 20% difference (if both groups had self-help books, then we have no idea why one group is visiting docs 20% less than the other).

If we had an argument that said, "Jumping off a tall building will lead to a broken bone. Jim has a broken bone. Thus he must have jumped off a tall building", our primary objections would be the backwards logic or the failure to consider alternate explanations, not "it is possible that a tall building has a lot of bushes at ground level to cushion one's fall".

**B. the families in the experiment could have gained access to medical self-help information outside of books** — No Impact (12% of students)
This is similar to (A), but it doesn't do a great job telling us which families we're even talking about. In order for this to weaken, we'd need to know that the families in the book-group got medical self-help info from some other source, and that info is why they saw the doc 20% less.

That would undermine the argument that medical self-help books improve family health by making it seem like the medical self-help book didn't have anything to do with the improved family health.

This answer doesn't discriminate between the two groups, so it doesn't give us an alternative way to explain the data. And the author didn't need to assume that people's only access to medical self-help info is books.

**C. a state of affairs could causally contribute to two or more different effects** — No Impact (15% of students)
In order for saying, "Hey, author, X could contribute to Y and Z" to be an objection, our author would have needed to be thinking that a certain causal factor only caused one thing.

Our author does think that having a self-help book caused improved family health, and that improved family health caused 20% fewer trips to the doctor.

So our author actually already accepts that a state of affair (having a self-help book) could causally contribute (directly or indirectly) to two or more effects.

**D. two different states of affairs could each causally contribute to the same effect even though neither causally contributes to the other** — Correct (50% of students)
Welcome to the Hall of Nightmares, kids! This correct answer definitely goes in my top 20 worst all time.

The way LSAT wanted us to match this up was like this:

two diff states of affairs each causally contribute to the same effect
```
improved family health --> fewer doctor visits
medical self-help book --> fewer doctor visits
```

even though neither causally contributes to the other
the medical self-help book did not causally contribute to improved family health.
(nor vice versa, but no one cares about that causal possibility)

This answer is getting at the objection we posed that maybe the medical self-help book really did lead to 20% fewer visits, but not because the book improved the families' health; rather, the book just allowed them to self-diagnose and learn how to treat their conditions when they were sick, obviating the need to go visit the doctor.

**E. certain states of affairs that lead families to visit the doctor less frequently could also make them more likely to have a medical self-help book in the home** — No Impact (15% of students)
If this argument hadn't been in the context of an experiment, and instead had just been a correlation like "People who own medical self-help books are less likely than those who don't to visit the doctor", then this answer could have pointed out the reverse causality possibility that FIRST came the healthy underlying factors, THEN came buying the medical self-help book.

But for this argument, we know that people are given the book as part of the experiment, so it removes this line of objection.

### Stimulus

### Prompt
Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its flawed reasoning to the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** S claims she supported the proposal.
  H also claims he supported the proposal.
  If they both supported it, it would have received approval.
  It didn't receive approval.
- **Conclusion:** H did not support the proposal.
- **Evaluate:** We could see this as providing a conditional premise with an AND in the trigger:
  S supports and H supports → approved

  A premise fact that triggers the contrapositive:
  ~Approved. (Thus, ~S support or ~H support)

  And then a conclusion that arbitrarily picks one of those options, failing to consider the other option:
  Thus, ~H support.

  Our objection would be like, "hey, how do you know it's ~H support? Couldn't it be ~S support?"

**Answer choices**

TV news: accident occurred on A street
Morgan: saw accident from kitchen window

Conditional: y'all can't both be right. If it was on A street, then M couldn't see from window.

Conclusion: I'll just arbitrarily say that the newspaper was wrong.
Us: Couldn't Morgan be the one who's wrong?

We could interpret the original argument in that same style:
S: I supported it
H: I supported it

Conditional: Y'all can't both be right. If you both supported it, it would have gotten approval. But it didn't.

Conclusion: I'll just arbitrarily decide that H is the one who is saying something false.
Us: Couldn't it be S that's saying something false?

This is a really tough problem / correct answer, and a good reminder that the correct answer on Parallel Flaw will sometimes stray pretty far from matching certain structural features of the original argument. What a correct answer must do is be vulnerable to a similar objection.

**B. According to the city government, 15 percent of city residents are behind on their property taxes. This conclusion isn't saying "thus She's right, He's wrong". It's just offering an evaluation. There's no He Said / She Said debate in the evidence. Definitely nothing resembling the "they can't both be right" tension we need to replicate the original flaw.

We don't have any "they can't both be right" tension in the evidence.

## S2 · Q6 — Most Supported

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Researchers studying athletes found that those who played mainly for the love of their sport actually had sharper vision during athletic competitions than those whose main goal was winning a trophy or championship. The vision of the first group of athletes was sharper because the concentration necessary for acute vision during an activity is typically possessed to a greater degree by those whose attention is focused on the activity itself.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the statements above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:**

  *Comparison - a Difference*
  ```
  playing for      sharper       playin' 4
  the love         vision        that chip
  of the game  >                 (for a trophy)
  ```

  *Causal Difference-Maker (because)*
  The vision was sharper, because our sharpness of vision is determined by our ability to focus on the activity itself. So ...
  ```
  CAUSE                  EFFECT(s)
  ability to focus on    increased        sharper
  the activity itself    concentration    vision
  ```
- **Evaluate:** This stimulus is my dream come true, because it speaks to every scrappy blacktop basketball player out there. When you're just playing for the love of the game, rather than peripheral concerns like trophies or championships, you have sharper vision, because you're more focused on the activity itself.

  "Sharper vision" is the overlapping term in these two facts, so we want to think about how these two facts about "sharper vision" combine.

  The second sentence is a monster. It's saying that there's a correlation between "focused on activity itself" and having "more concentration".

  ```
  attention is focused || more concentration
  on activity itself
  ```

  Concentration is necessary for acute (sharper) vision. And they're implying that the vision of the group playing mainly for the love of their sport was sharper because they possessed a greater degree of that vision-concentration.

  Putting those two together, we're getting this rough idea that,

  ```
  the more your attention    the more you      the sharper
  is focused on activity     can concentrate   your vision
  ```

  In the first sentence we were told that people who play for the love have the sharper vision. So we can infer that people who play for the love have more attention focused on the activity itself.
- **Goal:** Look for a safely worded claim, probably dealing with the crossover (basketball pun intended) idea that "people playing for the love have their attention focused more on the activity itself / people playing for trophies or championships have less attention focused on the activity itself"

**Answer choices**
**A. Winning a trophy or championship is not important to athletes who play mainly for the love of their sport.** — Too Strong: not important (0% of students)
People who plain mainly for the love can still find winning a trophy or title important. Just because something isn't your main reason for doing something, doesn't mean it's an unimportant reason for doing something.

Maybe the main reason you'll pick Law School X will be because of school ranking, but that doesn't mean that financial aid considerations will not be important to you.

In general, beware answer choices that feel like they're only pulling language from one sentence. The correct answer is almost always a crossover of ideas from 2 or more claims.

**B. If an athlete's main goal during an athletic competition is winning a trophy or championship, that athlete will lack the concentration necessary for adequate vision during that competition.** — Too Strong: lack adequate vision (7% of students)
This information we got would support the idea that athletes whose main goal is winning a trophy/championship have less sharp vision than people playing mainly for the love.

But we can't say they would lack something required for adequate vision.

If something is required for adequate vision, and you lack it, then you do not have adequate vision. Your vision is inadequate. That's too harsh.

The paragraph is comparing how much of certain traits people have (Relative), not dealing with on / off ideas (Absolute), like "adequate / not adequate".

**C. Athletes who play mainly for the love of their sport concentrate more on the sport itself during athletic competitions than do athletes whose main goal is winning a trophy or championship.** — Correct (89% of students)
We know the athletes playing mainly for the love have sharper vision, and that sharper vision comes from having your attention focused on the activity itself.

So athletes playing mainly for the love have more attention focused on the activity itself.

This answer uses "concentrate on the sport" as a synonym for "attention is focused on the activity".

**D. It is impossible for an athlete to concentrate on more than one thing at a time during an athletic competition.** — Too Strong: impossible (0% of students)
There was no language as extreme as impossible in this paragraph. We were talking about people playing "mainly for the love", which allows for them playing somewhat for the trophy.

It seems like that suggests you can concentrate on more than one thing.

**E. During athletic competitions, an athlete whose attention is focused on the sport itself will perform better than any athlete whose attention is focused elsewhere.** — Too Strong: any (4% of students)
This is also guns blazing:
every single athlete who's focused on the sport itself will perform better than all those who aren't focused on the sport itself.

All we know is that being focused on the sport helps with sharper vision. Sharper vision is a positive thing, but it wouldn't guarantee that you perform better than the people with less sharp vision.

## S2 · Q7 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Economist: ChesChem, a chemical manufacturer located in Chester, uses natural gas for its enormous energy needs. Currently, natural gas costs twice as much in Chester as it does in Tilsen. If the cost of natural gas in Chester becomes more than twice that in Tilsen, ChesChem will move its manufacturing operations to Tilsen. So if the cost of natural gas in Chester increases at all, ChesChem will move its manufacturing operations to Tilsen.

### Prompt
The economist's argument requires assuming that

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If the cost of natural gas in Chester increases at all, CC will move its manufacturing to Tilsen.
- **Evidence:**
  ```
  If the cost of natural gas       CC will move its
  in Chester becomes more      →   manufacturing
  than twice that in Tilsen        to Tilsen
  ```
  The cost of natural gas in Chester is currently twice that in Tilsen.
- **Evaluate:** This argument seems hard to disagree with at first. Isn't the conclusion correct?

  The cost of gas in Chester is currently twice the cost in Tilsen, and we have a rule that says as soon as the cost is more than twice of Tilsen's cost, that CC will move its operations to Tilsen.

  So wouldn't it be true that if the cost of gas in Chester increases at all, we will trigger the rule and move to Tilsen?

  We have to figure out how it could be possible that
  the cost of gas in Chester increases,
  but we don't trigger the rule, i.e. but the cost is not more than twice that of Tilsen.

  The wiggle room comes from the possible objection that the cost of gas might go up simultaneously in both Chester and Tilsen.

  Suppose right now that these are the prices of natural gas per cubic foot in each city:
  Chester - $40 / ft3
  Tilsen - $20 / ft3

  The author is thinking that if Chester goes up at all, say to $41 / ft3, then it will be more than twice of Tilsen.

  But what if they both increased by $1 at the same time?

  Chester - $41 / ft3
  Tilsen - $21 / ft3

  Now, Chester is actually less than twice as much as Tilsen, so the rule would not be triggered.
- **Goal:** Since there's only one mathematical loophole out of this logic, we can be confident that the correct answer will rule out our objection and sound something like, "When the cost of natural gas in Chester goes up, the cost in Tilsen will not also go up."

**Answer choices**
**A. ChesChem spends far more on natural gas than on any other expense** — Too Strong: far more than / Out of Scope: any other expense (1% of students)
Comparing what CC spends on natural gas to what it spends on other stuff it totally outside the conversation.

This conversation is only about whether CC will relocate to Tilsen, based on a rule that is strictly related to comparing the price of natural gas in Chester and Tilsen.

**B. the price of natural gas in Tilsen will not increase** — Correct (81% of students)
If we negate this, it becomes a big weakening idea:
the price of natural gas in Tilsen will increase

That would allow us to argue that even if the cost of gas in Chester goes up, CC won't necessarily relocate its operations to Tilsen.

That doesn't definitively tell us whether it will mean that Chester's price is still twice of Tilsen's, or is more than twice as much, or less than twice as much.

But because it creates doubt, it weakens. The author is thinking that since Chester is currently 2x Tilsen, any change to Chester will necessarily make it greater than 2x Tilsen, but in order for that logic to work, you have to hold Tilsen's number constant (or decrease it).

**C. ChesChem would not be profitable if its energy costs increased** — Out of Scope: profitable (2% of students)
Nothing in this conversation is discussing profits. We're just analyzing a very mathematical cost-of-gas comparison that triggers a relocation.

**D. the only benefit ChesChem would receive by moving its manufacturing operations to Tilsen is lower energy costs** — Too Strong: the only / Only Thing Mentioned ≠ Only Thing (1% of students)
If we negate this, would it weaken?
CC would receive other benefits in addition to cheaper gas costs, by moving to Tilsen.

That would actually strengthen, making it more likely that CC will move to Tilsen.

This answer choice does what so many trap answers on Necessary Assumption do. It tries to pretend like "the only benefit we mentioned (lower gas prices) is therefore the only benefit there is."

**E. ChesChem will not move its manufacturing operations to Tilsen unless the price of natural gas in Chester increases** — Reversed Logic of Conclusion (15% of students)
The conclusion says:

```
price of natural gas    →   CC moves operations
increases in Chester        to Tilsen
```

This answer choice says:

```
CC moves operations     →   price of natural gas
to Tilsen                   increases in Chester
```

### Written (single style)
**Answer choices**
**A. interprets lack of evidence for a claim as support for an opposing claim** — Not Unproven vs. Proven False (2% of students)
This answer refers to one of the 10 Famous Flaws, Unproven vs. Proven False, in which an author treats a failure to prove X true as evidence that X must be false.

This argument has no rebuttal qualities (as Unproven vs. Proven False normally would). The author's evidence never calls out a certain claim that lacks evidence.

**B. relies on an ambiguity in the expression "most polluted"** — Not Equivocation (1% of students)
This answer alludes to one of the 10 Famous Flaws, Equivocation, in which an author uses the same term or concept in two very different ways.

(This answer is almost always wrong.)

The way the author used "most polluted" was clear and consistent: it means "more polluted than any other river in the District".

Answers that ask us for specific numbers/measurements or definitions are basically always wrong.

The specific details behind the premises aren't what's being tested when we're being asked about the problem with reasoning. We're supposed to grant the truth of the premises and think about how they still fail to prove the conclusion.

So we'll accept that Lalolah was ranked 1st and is now ranked 3rd. We aren't supposed to object by saying, "Why should I believe those premises? What was the basis for these rankings?"

This argument didn't do that. That would have felt like, "Each river in the district is polluted. Thus, the district as a whole is polluted."

**E. equates a decrease relative to the other ranked rivers with an absolute decrease** — Correct (91% of students)
For all Flaw answer choices, we can ask ourselves:
1. Did this really happen? Is it descriptively true?
2. Does it matter? Does it point to some problem with the logic?

It's certainly true that the author goes from Evidence about a relative decrease (the Lalolah River went from being more polluted than all other rivers, to being less polluted than two rivers) to a Conclusion that assumes an absolute decrease (she thinks that cleanup measures led to the river having less pollution than it had before).

This answer points to a problem with the logic (very indirectly) by suggesting the Alternate Explanation for the curious fact:

No, author, the river didn't get less polluted, it's just that two other rivers got so much worse that they surpassed Lalolah in the pollution rankings.

If we had read the argument, "Last year Elon Musk was the richest person in the world. This year, he is the 3rd richest. So apparently his decision to buy Twitter has caused him to lose a bunch of money", then this answer choice would apply just the same.

We would be able to object, "Wait a sec — maybe he didn't lose any money over the past year and the real reason that he's now 3rd is simply because two people who used to be under him in terms of wealth have gained enough wealth to surpass him. Just because his wealth changed in relative terms (in terms of how he compares to other) doesn't mean that his wealth changed in absolute terms (it doesn't mean that he now has less money).

- **Shelton:** Recently, the moose population has steeply declined because they picked up a parasite from local white-tailed deer.
- **Russo:** In a nearby region, there's been a big increase in white-tailed deer, but not change in the moose population.
- **Evaluate / Goal:** We need the correct answer to point out some meaningful difference between these two regions / these two situations.

  How come in one situation, the increase in deer is leading to a decrease in moose (via parasite), whereas in the other situation, the increase in deer is not leading to a decrease in moose?

**Answer choices**
**A. The region with the declining moose population is larger than the neighboring region and, even after the decline, has more moose than the neighboring region.** — Irrelevant Comparison (4% of students)
We wouldn't care which of the two regions' moose populations is bigger. We're concerned with relative changes within a region (less moose than before vs. same moose as before).

**B. The region with the declining moose population consists mainly of high-quality moose habitat, but the quality of moose habitat in the neighboring region is marginal.** — Opposite (2% of students)
We're looking for a way to explain why in the neighboring region the moose aren't declining, and this answer tells us "in that region, they have worse habitat". So that's the opposite of what we want.

**C. Wolf packs in the region with the declining moose population generally prey on only moose and deer, but in the neighboring region the wolf packs prey on a wider variety of species.** — Too Weak (18% of students)
We're looking for a way to explain why in the neighboring region the moose aren't declining, and this answer tells us "in that region, the wolves at a wider variety of species". Does that mean that wolves don't eat the moose as much and that's why there's a stable number of moose?

That's a stretch of a story. Not to mention, it doesn't have a good logical connection to what's different when it comes to more deer here vs. more deer there.

**D. There is a large overlap in the ranges of moose and white-tailed deer in the region with the declining moose population, but not in the neighboring region.** — Correct (75% of students)
What an answer for our COVID-19 times. In the neighboring region, the uptick in deer population isn't infecting the local moose with parasites as much because over there the moose and the deer have "socially distanced" habitats.

**E. Moose require a habitat with very little human settlement, whereas white-tailed deer often thrive in and around areas with considerable human settlement.** — Doesn't Provide Difference (1% of students)
This just generalizes about moose and deer in a way that will hold true for both regions, both situations. So it's not going to help us explain why those two regions/situations are different.

### Stimulus

Which one of the following propositions is best illustrated by the statements above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Statements:**
  Used to be ... classroom computers were a luxury.

  Now ... educators think that students without training in computer skills will lack the skills necessary to compete in the global marketplace.

  However ... schools that emphasize computer technology spend more time teaching computer skills but less time developing students' basic math and reading skills.
- **Evaluate:** Questions that ask us to support a Generalization / Proposition usually describe one specific scenario. The correct answer is safely worded takeaway, whereas trap answers are often too strong.

  Something that used to be considered extraneous is now considered vital, and it seems to have resulted in a re-allocation of teaching time.

**A. A knowledge of the latest technologies is no more valuable than a knowledge of the fundamental academic disciplines.** — Out of Scope: no more valuable (4% of students)

"Hmm, she talked about schools caring more about computers, but then she says However they're spending more time on computers and less on basic math and reading. She must be mad about this?"

It's also much broader to say "giving students a knowledge of new technologies" than it is to talk about emphasizing computer technologies. (it's not as if all new technologies are computer technologies)

## S2 · Q16 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Most pet owners who take allergy medication are allergic to pets. Therefore, since Chuck owns a pet dog, it is likely that he will take allergy medication if he develops an allergy to pets.

### Prompt
Which one of the following arguments exhibits flawed reasoning that is most similar to that exhibited by the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** If Chuck (a pet owner) develops an allergy to pets, he'll probably take allergy medication.
- **Evidence:** Most pet owners who take allergy medication are allergic to pets.
- **Evaluate:** Whether or not we understand the flaw in this argument, we can map out the structure, by keeping track of quantifiers and overlapping terms.

  Premise
  Most A's who are B are C.
  Person X is an A.
  Conclusion
  If person X is C, they are likely to be B.

  The flaw with this argument is reversing the logic of a Most statement.

  When we say "Most lawyers are wealthy", then we're allowed to say
  If Lois is a lawyer, then it is likely that she is wealthy.

  But, we're not allowed to say
  If Lois is wealthy, then it's likely that she's a lawyer.
- **Goal:** The key part of the flaw is that there's a Most statement:
  Most X's are Y
  and then the author performs an illegal backwards move, saying
  If Y, then likely X.

**Answer choices**
**A. Most cars taken to Acme Automotive Electronics have electrical problems. Therefore, it is likely that Anastasia's car has electrical problems if she has taken it to Acme Automotive Electronics.** — Reversed Conclusion / Valid (15% of students)
This gives us the

Most A's that are B are C.

But it needs to conclude that,
If this A is a C, then it's likely a B.
or, equivalently,
it it likely that B is true if C is true.

The matching conclusion would have been,
it is likely that A's car was taken to Acme if it has electrical problems.

This conclusion actually is a valid inference from the Most premise.

**B. Most cars taken to Acme Automotive Electronics have electrical problems. Therefore, it is likely that Anastasia will take her car there if it has electrical problems.** — Correct (72% of students)
We have the same
Most A's that are B are C

We want a conclusion that's saying
likely to be B, if C

This conclusion does do that:
likely to be Acme, if it has electrical problems

What this argument and the original have in common that wasn't noted in our original breakdown of the argument is how the conclusion not only performs the reversed Most move, it also switches to a future tense.

The original said,
If he develops a pet allergy, he'll be likely to take allergy meds,
since most people who take allergy meds have a pet allergy.

This answer is saying
If the car has electrical problems, she will likely go to Acme,
since most people who go to Acme have electrical problems.

**C. Most cars that are taken to Acme Automotive Electronics do not have electrical problems. Therefore, it is likely that Anastasia will not take her car to Acme Automotive Electronics if it has electrical problems.** — Bad Conclusion Match (2% of students)
This conclusion involves some truth-value switching that the original argument didn't have.

It goes from "do not have electrical problems" in the premise to "does have electrical problems" in the conclusion.

And it goes from "do take to Acme" in the premise to "won't go to Acme" in the conclusion.

**D. Most cars taken to Acme Automotive Electronics have electrical problems. Therefore, it is unlikely that Anastasia will take her car to Acme Automotive Electronics if it does not have electrical problems.** — Bad Conclusion Match (2% of students)
This conclusion flips a couple things:
the original was saying "it's likely to be B, if C"

This conclusion says
"it's unlikely to be B, if not C"

**E. Most cars taken to Acme Automotive Electronics have electrical problems. Therefore, it is likely that Anastasia will take her car to Acme Automotive Electronics whenever she thinks it has electrical problems.** — Inferior Conclusion Match (8% of students)
This is real close. But the conclusion gets into her beliefs:
"if she thinks the car has electrical problems".

The original didn't say, "if Chuck thinks he has developed an allergy".

## S2 · Q17 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Scientist: A number of errors can plague a data-collection process. Since examining the collected data enables researchers to detect many of these errors, it is standard practice for researchers to correct collected data. However, in my field, there is a striking tendency for such corrections to favor Jones's theory; that is, the majority of corrections result in the corrected data's being closer than the uncorrected data to what Jones's theory predicts.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the tendency of corrections in the scientist's field to favor Jones's theory?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Background:** Data-collection is plagued by erroneous data points and researchers examine collected data for errors so that they can correct them.
- **How Can We Explain:** Most corrections result in the data point becoming closer to what Jones's theory would predict.
- **Evaluate:** Let's say that Jones's theory predicts a value of 10 for each data point.

  We get a bunch of data points:
  (10, 10, 7, 8, 13, 10, 10, 11, 15, 6, 10, 9)

  Some of them turn out to be wrong, and when we fix them, they tend to move closer to Jones's predictions.

  For example, we find out that the 7 is an incorrect data point. In reality it should be 8.
  We find out that the 13 is an incorrect data point. In reality it should be 12.

  How come the corrections are shifting closer to Jones's prediction of 10? Shouldn't the errors be random? The 13 was actually lower than it should have been? Or the 7 was higher that it should have been?

  What accounts for the fact that corrections usually bring us closer to Jones's estimate?
- **Goal:** My first thought was "Maybe Jones's theory is correct! A correct theory makes correct predictions. Data points should align with the predictions. An erroneous data point, when corrected, will move closer to a correct theory, since a correct theory makes correct predictions."

  However, I know not to get too much anchor bias to my anticipated answer on Paradox questions, since the correct answer is frequently surprising.

**Answer choices**
**A. Researchers normally give data that is in line with a theory the same weight as data that conflicts with that theory when they are determining whether to accept that theory.** — No Impact (5% of students)
We don't care about whether a theory is going to get accepted or not. We just care about why corrected data points are generally drifting towards Jones's theory.

**B. Researchers in the scientist's field give data that conflicts with Jones's theory greater scrutiny than they give data that is in line with Jones's theory.** — Correct (67% of students)
This points to a distinction that could be a causal difference-maker. If the people scanning for errors are giving more scrutiny to data points that conflict with Jones's theory, then they won't notice erroneous data points that align with Jones's theory but are actually bad data points. Those data points would have shifted away from Jones's theory.

This is more or less describing confirmation bias skewing the results of a study.

If a high school teacher thought that male students were more likely to plagiarize than were female students, he might give the essays from male students more scrutiny, in terms of checking online to see if they seemed to have plagiarized any of their papers.

Since he doesn't expect the female students to plagiarize, he's not checking their papers as closely.

Through this act of confirmation bias, he will reinforce this stereotype he has. He'll find more examples of males plagiarizing than females plagiarizing, since he's putting more effort into looking for trouble with males' papers.

Similarly, if these researchers already implicitly assume J's theory is right, they'll have some confirmation bias. They won't bother checking the the data points that align with Jones's theory, because they're assuming those data points are probably correct.

Meanwhile, they'll expend effort looking at data points that diverge from Jones's theory, so they'll catch more offenders (erroneous data points).

**C. Researchers in the scientist's field are more likely to pursue lines of research that they expect will favor theories they accept than pursue other lines of research.** — Unclear Impact (18% of students)
Someone might have liked this if they were looking for an answer that said, "the corrected data points shift towards Jones's theory, because Jones's theory is right".

This is more like, "Some lines of research will favor Jones's theory. These researchers accept Jones's theory, so they pursue flattering lines of research. Thus, since Jones's theory is 'right' for these lines of research, corrections will usually bring data points closer to Jones's predictions."

There's a lot of stretching for us to try to turn this answer into that story. The biggest gap is that we have no idea whether any researchers in this field favor Jones's theory, and without knowing that we can't possibly get this answer to go anywhere.

**D. Even if researchers fail to detect errors in a data-collection process when they examine data that they collected, that does not guarantee that no such errors exist.** — Very Weak (5% of students)
"Even if ... that does not guarantee" is language that shows there is a possibility for at least one thing. Even if they fail to detect errors, it is possible that at least one error exists.

"At least one" strength of language (some, sometimes, not all, can, may, might, need not) is almost never correct on Strengthen / Weaken / Paradox.

This answer has no power to make a distinction between data points that do / don't align with Jones's predictions.

**E. Researchers in the scientist's field have formulated several other theories that attempt to explain the same range of phenomena that Jones's theory attempts to explain.** — No Impact (4% of students)
The fact that Jones's theory is not the only theory doesn't change anything, and it doesn't explain why corrected data points are mainly moving in the direction of aligning more closely with Jones's theory.

## S2 · Q18 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** C

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Doctor: Angiotensinogen is a protein in human blood. Typically, the higher a person's angiotensinogen levels are, the higher that person's blood pressure is. Disease X usually causes an increase in angiotensinogen levels. Therefore, disease X must be a cause of high blood pressure.

### Prompt
The doctor's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on which one of the following grounds?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Disease X must be a cause of high blood pressure.
- **Evidence:** Disease X usually causes angiotensinogen levels to go up.

  Typically, the higher a person's angiotensinogen levels, the higher that person's blood pressure
- **Evaluation:** The author's causal story is that:
  ```
  Disease X → Higher          → Higher blood
              angiotensinogen    pressure levels
              levels
  ```

  She has established that Disease X causes higher angiotensinogen levels, but she hasn't established that higher angiotensinogen levels cause higher blood pressure.

  We know that the two things are tightly correlated (typically rising and falling together), but it's possible that higher blood pressure causes higher angiotensinogen levels, or that both higher blood pressure and higher angiotensinogen levels are caused by some underlying third factor.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer calling out the author's move from "higher angiotensinogen is correlated with higher blood pressure" to "higher angiotensinogen causes higher blood pressure".

  Or look for an answer posing the alternate interpretations, that maybe "higher blood pressure causes higher angiotensinogen " or maybe "they are both symptoms of the same underlying cause".

**Answer choices**
**A. It confuses a necessary condition for a sufficient condition.** — No Conditional Logic (7% of students)
In order to commit the famous Necessary vs. Sufficient flaw, there needs to be some conditional logic. Our two premises (typically and usually) are not conditional.

**B. It overlooks the possibility that even if a condition causally contributes to a given effect, other factors may fully counteract that effect in the presence of that condition.** — Bad Conclusion Match (11% of students)
The syntax of "fails to consider that even if X, it may not be Y" implies that the first idea should match the Premise and the second idea should match the opposite of the conclusion. "Even if your Premise is true, your Conclusion may still be wrong" is basically our objection to every argument.

So does the premise have a condition that causally contributes to a given effect?
Yes, Disease X usually causally contributes to higher angiotensinogen levels.

Okay, so the 2nd half is saying "other factors may fully counteract the higher angiotensinogen levels in the presence of Disease X".

This is an objection saying, "Even if Disease X wants to cause your angiotensinogen levels to go up, something else in that situation would be fully counteracting that and keeping your angiotensinogen levels from going up".

That doesn't sound like any sort of objection to this argument. We want to object, "even if Disease X causes your angiotensinogen levels to go up, your blood pressure might not go up".

**C. It illicitly infers, solely on the basis of two phenomena being correlated, that one causally contributes to the other.** — Correct (71% of students)
Does the author conclude/assume that one phenomenon causally contributes to another?
Yes, the conclusion is that Disease X increases blood pressure.
The assumption is that higher angiotensinogen levels increase blood pressure.

Does the evidence say that Disease X and blood pressure, or that higher angiotensinogen and higher blood pressure, are correlated?

Yes, we have a premise that says typically angiotensinogen and blood pressure go hand in hand.

This answer is annoyingly using "infer" to refer to something the author assumed, not something the author concluded explicitly. That does happen in some other correct Flaw answer choices. What makes this doubly annoying though is that there is also a causal conclusion, and the causal conclusion is based on two pieces of evidence (Disease X causes higher angiotensinogen + the correlation between angiotensinogen / blood pressure).

Meanwhile, this answer is saying that an inference is built solely on a correlation. This answer choice wouldn't apply to the author's argument as a whole.

So we have to understand this answer as testing a subsidiary argument happening within the author's broader argument: "the author concluded from the correlation between angiotensinogen and blood pressure, that higher angiotensinogen causes higher blood pressure"

**D. It confuses one phenomenon's causing a second with the second phenomenon's causing the first.** — Never Correct (8% of students)
A flaw answer that says "the author thinks X caused Y, but in reality Y caused X" has never been right (as far I know).

That's because we can't be sure that Y caused X. It's just a possibility. Our answer choice should be saying, "His argument is flawed because it overconfidently arrives at one possible explanation for the data, when other possibilities exist".

It's fine to say "the author thinks X caused Y, but Y may have caused X". But it's not fine for us to be sure that reverse causality is happening.

This answer would have to say something more tentative, like "It interprets one phenomenon's causing a second from evidence consistent with the possibility that the second phenomenon causes the first".

**E. It takes for granted that if one phenomenon often causes a second phenomenon and that second phenomenon often causes a third phenomenon, then the first phenomenon cannot ever be the immediate cause of the third.** — Bad Evidence Match / Bad Conclusion Match (4% of students)
An answer with the syntax "It takes for granted that if X and Y, then Z", is saying, "The author goes from knowing X and Y in the evidence, to concluding Z."

In the evidence do we have one phenomenon that often causes a second?
Yes, Disease X often causes higher angiotensinogen levels.

Do the evidence also say that "higher angiotensinogen levels cause some third phenomenon"?

No, the evidence says that "higher angiotensinogen levels are correlated with some third phenomenon (higher blood pressure)". From this mismatch, we could stop reading and eliminate.

The last part of the answer would be saying that the conclusion is that "Disease X can never be the immediate cause of higher blood pressure", and that would also be a bad match for our conclusion.

## S2 · Q19 — Parallel

**Correct answer:** A

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Some species of tarantula make good pets. However, no creature with poison fangs makes a good pet. Therefore, not all tarantula species have poison fangs.

### Prompt
Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its pattern of reasoning to the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Evidence:** (Because there are quantifiers / conditional indicators / repeating ideas, it makes a lot of sense to turn this argument into Algebra)

  Some tarantula's make good pets. Some A's are B.

  No creature with poison fangs makes a good pet. No C's are B.
- **Conclusion:** Not all tarantula's have poison fangs. Not all A's are C.
- **Evaluate:** We don't really need to judge the validity of the logic here, but it is sound, for what it's worth.

  Premise 2 says that "if poison fangs, then not good pet", which contraposes into "if good pet, then not poison fangs".

  Thus, since Premise 1 says that "Some tarantulas make good pets", we can infer that "Some tarantulas do not have poison fangs".

  Since this argument is using two negative quantifiers (no / not all), it's worth re-capping the negative to positive translations.

  No A's are B = All A's are ~B
  Few A's are B = Most A's are ~B
  Not all A's are B = Some A's are ~B

  At any point on LSAT they might trade one form for the other, and they're perfectly interchangeable.

  We wrote our argument as

  Premise 1: Some A's are B.
  Premise 2: No C's are B.
  Conclusion: Not all A's are C.

  But we could have written it interchangeably as

  Premise 1: Some A's are B.
  Premise 2: All C's are ~B.
  (All B's are ~C).
  Conclusion: Some A's are ~C.
- **Goal:** We know we need two premises and one conclusion.

  The two premises should be a mix:
  1 some / not all claim
  1 no / all claim

  The conclusion is a some / not all claim.

  Assuming an answer has those basic ingredients, we'll make sure that the Some / Not All premise gives us an idea that triggers the No / All premise.

**Answer choices**
**A. None of the poetry written by Strawn has a regular meter. But some of the poems in this collection have a regular meter. Therefore, at least some of the poems in this collection were not written by Strawn.** — Correct (55% of students)
Our two premises are None and Some, and our conclusion is Some, so this is worth reading.

Premise 1: Some A's are B. Some poems in this collection have a regular meter.
Premise 2: No C's are B. No written by Strawn have regular meter.
Conclusion: Not all A's are C. Some poems in this collection are ~written by Strawn.
(Some A's are ~C)

Everything matches up!

**B. Some of the poems in this collection have a regular meter. However, some of the poetry written by Strawn does not have a regular meter. Therefore, at least some of the poetry written by Strawn is not in this collection.** — Bad Premise Match (11% of students)
Our two premises are Some and Some, so there's no need to read this one. We needed a Some/Not All premise and a No/All premise.

Premise 1: Some A's are B.
Premise 2: No C's are B.
Conclusion: Not all A's are C.
(Some A's are ~C)

**C. Some of the poetry written by Strawn has a regular meter. However, this collection contains no poetry written by Strawn. Therefore, not all of the poetry in this collection has a regular meter.** — Invalid Logic / Bad Conclusion Match (28% of students)
Our two premises are Some and No, and our conclusion is Not All, so this is worth reading.

Premise 1: Some A's are B. Some WBS is RM.
Premise 2: No C's are B. No PIC is WBS.
Conclusion: Not all A's are C. Some PIC are ~RM.
(Some A's are ~C)

(PIC = poems in this collection)
(RM = regular meter)
(WBS = written by Strawn)

The logic on this one doesn't work. The 2nd premise is a conditional that says,
PIC → ~WBS
WBS → ~PIC

Does the 1st premise talk about PIC or WBS?
Yes, it says, "Some RM is WBS",
which allows us to conclude
"Some RM is ~PIC".

So the correct conclusion that should have been drawn is,
"Some poetry with regular meter is not in this collection"
also known as
"Not all poetry with regular meter is in this collection".

Instead, we got a conclusion saying
"Not all poetry in this collection has regular meter"
also known as
"Some poetry in this collection does not have regular meter".

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Opposing Idea:** It is widely believed that, given its northerly latitude, England's mild winters must be due to the Gulf Stream, which brings warm water flowing northeastward across the Atlantic Ocean.
- **Conclusion:** This belief is mistaken [in other words, England's mild winters are not due to the Gulf Stream].

Everything that follows this second sentence provides support for the conclusion.

  The pivot indicator "but" at the beginning of the second sentence helps us see that we're pivoting from the belief described in the first sentence to something different. The first sentence states that this idea about England's winter is "widely believed," which is presented as a statement of fact.

**Answer choices**
**A. It is widely believed that England's mild winters must be due to the Gulf Stream.** — Opposing Idea (0% of students)
This describes the opposing idea presented in the first sentence. The conclusion of the argument refutes this belief.

**B. The belief that England's mild winters must be due to the Gulf Stream is mistaken.** — Correct (98% of students)
This mirrors the conclusion of the argument, "This belief is mistaken." It's worded in a way that describes exactly what "this belief" refers to.

**C. It is true that the Gulf Stream brings tropical water to England.** — Opposing Idea (0% of students)
This is presented as a fact in the argument. It's a fact which supports the widely held belief that England's mild winters must be due to the Gulf Stream, a belief that the conclusion refutes.

**D. In the Pacific Ocean, the Kuroshio Current brings tropical water only as far north as Oregon.** — Premise (1% of students)
This is evidence used to support the conclusion.

**E. North America's west coast has mild winters well north of Oregon.** — Premise / Last Claim Bait (1% of students)
This answer might be trying to trick people who are used to the final statement in an argument being the conclusion. But this isn't the conclusion. It's evidence used to support the conclusion.

## S4 · Q15 — Parallel Flaw

**Correct answer:** B

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Commentator: The reported epidemic of childhood obesity in our country is a myth. Over the last 8 years, there was only a 1 pound (0.45 kilogram) increase in children's average weight. This is not a substantial increase, so the proportion of children who are obese cannot have increased substantially.

### Prompt
Which one of the following contains flawed reasoning most similar to the flawed reasoning contained in the argument above?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The supposed epidemic of childhood obesity is a myth.
- **Intermediate Conclusion:** The proportion of children who are obese cannot have gone up much over the last 8 years.
- **Evidence:** There was only a 1 pound increase in children's average weight over the last 8 years.
- **Evaluate:** If we aren't mathematically inclined, then the structural features to go off of are these:
  Premise: talks about average going up a little
  Conclusion: says that proportion couldn't have gone up much

  The mathematical flaw here is that the author is treating "average" too literally, and thinking that "if the average only went up 1 pound, then the kids' weights only went up 1 pound."

  But average is the net effect of everyone together. You can have a lot of different up/down movements that balance out to be neutral (or almost neutral, at +1 pound).

  Pretend that 100 pounds is considered obese for a 3rd grader.

  8 years ago, here are the weights of our ten 3rd grade students:
  50, 70, 70, 80, 80, 80, 90, 90, 110

  Average weight = 80,
  proportion who are obese = 10%

  Here are the weights of ten 3rd grade students now:
  50, 50, 50, 80, 80, 80, 110, 110, 110

  Average weight = 80,
  proportion who are obese = 30%

  For simplicity's sake, I didn't even both to change the average. This shows how an average might move very little, if at all, even though the proportion of kids who are obese is now three times as much as it was before.

  There could be kids putting on a lot more weight (who go from non-obese to obese) while those weight gains are offset by other kids losing weight, which is why the average stays stable.
- **Goal:** Find an argument saying "because the average hasn't moved much, a proportion of some subset hasn't changed much".

**Answer choices**
**A. The average summer temperature over the past five years must have increased, since most people believe that summers are getting hotter.** — Bad Premise / Conclusion Match (1% of students)
Here, the conclusion is about average (average should be the premise), and the premise is about what people believe, when the other half of the argument is supposed to be a claim about proportion / ratio / fraction / percentage of a subset.

**B. The proportion of employees who earn very high salaries must not have increased more than slightly over the last year, since the average salary has increased only slightly over that time.** — Correct (90% of students)
The conclusion is about proportion and the evidence is about average. Huzzah!

Our same objection would apply here: it's possible that a much higher percentage of people now make very high salaries. If, simultaneously, a lot of people now make much lower salaries, those two effects will balance out and cause the average to increase only slightly.

**C. The proportion of apartment buildings in the downtown area must have increased substantially in recent years, since a substantial number of office buildings have been converted to apartment buildings during that time.** — Bad Premise Match (3% of students)
The conclusion is correctly about proportion, but the premise is supposed to be about average. Instead, it's about number.

Also, the original argument was saying "since average barely moved, proportion barely moved". This one is saying "since there's a substantial number of new apartment building, there must be a substantial increase to the proportion".

**D. The average weight of adults must have increased in the last few years, since restaurants have increased the proportion of high-calorie dishes on their menus in recent years.** — Reversed Premise / Conclusion (2% of students)
Here, the conclusion is about average and the support is about proportion. The original argument had those two ingredients reversed.

**E. The average price of a house must be increasing, since the proportion of household income spent on housing has increased over the last eight years.** — Reversed Premise / Conclusion (4% of students)
Again, the conclusion is about average and the support is about proportion. The original argument had those two ingredients reversed.

## S4 · Q16 — Flaw

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Editorial: The main contention of Kramer's book is that coal companies are to blame for our region's economic difficulties. Kramer bases this contention primarily on allegations made by disgruntled coal company employees that the companies made no significant investments in other industries in our region. Yet the companies invested heavily—albeit sometimes indirectly—in road building and manufacturing in the region. Thus, the book's main contention is simply false.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the editorial's argument is flawed in that this argument

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (thus):** The book's main contention is simply false.
  rephrase: Coal companies are not to blame for our region's economic difficulties.
- **Evidence:** Kramer based this contention on allegations that the companies made no significant investments in other industries in our region, but the companies did make significant investments (albeit sometimes indirectly) in road building and manufacturing in the region.
- **Evaluate:** Whenever we're doing Flaw and the argument is a Rebuttal we want to immediately look out for the two rebuttal flaws from the 10 famous flaws:

  1. Ad Hominem, in which the author dismisses someone's point of view because the person has a biased interest in the matter or because they have conflicting past behavior.

  2. Unproven vs. Proven False, in which the author says that "Since your attempt to prove X failed, that must mean that X is false."

  This argument seems to be committing #2, since the conclusion is saying "this view is false". Kramer based his conclusion on idea Y, and the author tells us that idea Y is false.

  Just because we base a conclusion on a wrong idea doesn't mean that the conclusion is wrong. In the old days, people concluded that humans were special based on the belief that Earth was the center of the universe. Now that we know that Earth is not the center of the universe, does that prove that humans aren't special? Of course not. Maybe we are special, but just for a different reason (like maybe we're the most intelligent species so far).
- **Goal:** Let's look for an answer calling out the Unproven vs. Proven False flaw. Just because the evidence of Kramer's argument turns out to be false doesn't prove that Kramer's conclusion is false.

**Answer choices**
**A. concludes that one party is not to blame for a particular outcome merely on the grounds that another party is to blame for that outcome** — Bad Evidence Match (1% of students)
Since this answer describes a 2-part move, concludes X on the grounds that Y, we should try to match X up with the conclusion and Y up with the evidence.

The conclusion is indeed saying that one party (coal companies) are not to blame for a particular outcome (region's economic difficulties). But the evidence didn't say that some other party is to blame for the region's economic difficulties. The evidence just said that Kramer's premise was false.

**B. concludes that a person's statement is false merely on the grounds that, if accepted as true, it would impugn the reputation of an important industry** — Bad Evidence Match (2% of students)
Since this answer describes a 2-part move, concludes that X merely on the grounds that Y, we should try to match X up with the conclusion and Y up with the evidence.

The conclusion is indeed saying that a statement is false (Kramer's main contention). But the evidence didn't say that "If Kramer were right, this would impugn the reputation of the coal industry, which is an important industry". The evidence said, "Kramer's premise was false".

**C. rejects an argument merely on the grounds that the person offering the argument has an ulterior motive for doing so** — Not Ad Hominem (10% of students)
This answer describes the famous Ad Hominem flaw. The author does reject an argument, but doesn't do so because she thinks that Kramer has an ulterior motive for making it. She rejects his conclusion because the premise on which he based it is false.

**D. takes a sufficient condition for the coal companies' having made significant investments in other industries in the region to be a necessary condition for their having done so** — Not Necessary vs. Sufficient (18% of students)
This answer describes the #1 famous flaw Necessary vs. Sufficient, in which the argument presents a conditional rule as a premise and then tries to apply that rule in an illegal backwards or negated fashion. This argument did not have a conditional logic premise, so there's no way this could be right.

**E. concludes that a person's statement is false merely on the grounds that an inadequate argument has been given for it** — Correct (70% of students)
This describes the famous Unproven vs. Proven False flaw. The author did indeed conclude that someone's statement is false (Thus, the main contention is simply false), and the evidence was indeed a description of the inadequate argument Kramer made to support his contention.

He based his contention primarily on allegations that our author tells us are not true. Thus, Kramer's support was inadequate to prove his conclusion. But of course that doesn't prove that the conclusion itself is false.

## S4 · Q17 — Necessary Assumption

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Health-care facilities have a duty to protect their patients from unnecessary harm. So, since influenza viruses pose substantial risks to patients, and since vaccines can significantly reduce the spread of these viruses, health-care facilities must institute policies that make influenza vaccinations mandatory for all employees.

### Prompt
Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion (So):** Health care facilities must institute policies that make influenza vaccination mandatory for all employees.
- **Evidence (since / since):** Health-care facilities have a duty to protect their patients from unnecessary harm.

  Influenza viruses pose substantial risks to patients.

  Vaccines can significantly reduce the spread of these viruses.
- **Evaluate:** If we were trying to prephrase a Linking assumption here, it would sound something like, "If action X would help a health care facility achieve one of its duties, then health care facilities must mandate action X".

  But since this argument has a Plan / Recommendation feel to it, we're more likely to see an answer dealing with ruling out a possible objection. So let's try to think of a possible objection.

  Given that ... vaccines can reduce the spread of the flu, which poses a big risk to patients.
  and given that ... health-care facilities have a duty to protect their patients from unnecessary harm,

  How could we argue that ... health-care facilities don't have to institute policies that make flu vaccinations mandatory for all employees?

  When an argument identifies a problematic situation and concludes that "we must do X", they are assuming two automatic things:
  1. X would help solve the problem
  2. there aren't any other solutions that could also solve the problem

  So this author is assuming that mandatory vaccinations would achieve the goal of making the facility's patients be less exposed to unnecessary harm.

  And she's assuming that health care facilities don't have any other options (maybe instead of making the vaccines mandatory, they could just incentive them by saying "everyone who gets vaccinated gets an extra day of vacation for the year").

  Maybe we don't need all employees to be vaccinated. Maybe the employees that work away from the patients (like in the kitchen or laundry room) don't need to be vaccinated.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer Linking "mandatory vaccines" to "protecting patients from unnecessary harm".

  Or look for an answer ruling out an objection, probably ruling out an alternative plan for how the facility could protect patients from harm without making vaccinations mandatory for all employees.

**Answer choices**
**A. Health-care facility employees do not regard mandatory vaccination policies as violating their rights.** — Out of Scope: how they regard it (4% of students)
We never talked about how employees would regard a mandatory vaccination policy.

But we should negate this and consider if it would be an objection to the conclusion.

If we say, "Some employees do consider mandatory vaccination as violating their rights", does that help us argue that these facilities do not need to institute mandatory vaccine policies?

Not really. The argument is worried about protecting patient health. It doesn't really care if employees feel their rights are being violated, unless that manifests in some way that endangers patient health.

If employees are mad but get vaccinated anyway, or they're mad and they quit out of refusal to get vaccinated, neither of those hurts the argument.

The outrage of some employees would only be relevant to this argument if the angry employees "faked" being vaccinated but then still spread the flu to patients or if the employees took out their anger about the vaccine policy in ways that increased danger of harm to patients.

**B. Influenza viruses are the most harmful airborne pathogens to which patients risk exposure when entering a health-care facility.** — Too Strong: the most harmful (4% of students)
This argument is definitely assuming that flu viruses pose some risk of unnecessary harm to patients, but it doesn't need to assume that flu viruses are the #1 most harmful airborne pathogen patients risk exposure to.

If the flu viruses are the #2 most harmful airborne, the argument still makes just as much sense.

**C. Most patients in health-care facilities are not vaccinated against influenza.** — Too Strong: most (11% of students)
The word "most" is wrong 99% of the time it shows up on Necessary Assumption because it's so specifically about this 50% threshold.

Does the author need there to be at least 51% of patients unvaxxed for his argument to make sense? Or would the argument still make sense if only 49% of patients are unvaxxed?

The argument would work either way, so while this answer would strengthen the argument, it isn't needed for the argument to work.

**D. Voluntary vaccination policies at health-care facilities would not adequately protect patients from the risks posed by influenza viruses.** — Correct (80% of students)
If "most" is the least lovable word in Necessary Assumption, then "not" is the most lovable word. So many correct answers play a Defender role; they rule out a potential objection using the word "not/no". These are ripe for negating.

Does it hurt the argument if we say, "Voluntary vaccination policies would adequately protect patients from the risks of flu viruses"?

Yes! That basically contradicts the conclusion, which was saying "these facilities need to make the vaccinations mandatory". It points to an alternative solution. They don't need to do mandatory vaccinations if voluntary vaccinations would provide adequate protection.

**E. Society has already accepted the idea of mandatory vaccination in other contexts.** — Out of Scope: society / other contexts (1% of students)
(Man, it's fun to read these answers in a post-Covid era.)

This argument wasn't talking at all about whether society has / hasn't accepted mandatory vaccinations in other contexts.

If we negate this and say, "society hasn't already accepted this idea in other contexts", that doesn't inherently weaken the argument.

The author can just say, "Okay, that's fine. I guess this will be the first context in which society accepts the idea. Or society can fail to accept it. Who cares. We only care about the facility upholding its duty to protect patients from unnecessary harm".

### Stimulus
Etiquette helps people to get along with each other. For example, it prevents people from inadvertently offending one another. While many people criticize etiquette because they believe it has no beneficial effects for society, these same people think that kindness and social harmony are good.

### Prompt
The statements above, if true, most strongly support which one of the following?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Facts:**
  causal: Etiquette helps people to get along, by preventing people from accidentally offending one another.

  opinion: Many people think etiquette has no beneficial effects, while also thinking that kindness and social harmony are good.
- **Evaluate:** Inference questions are all about synthesizing ideas. The first two ideas could be combined to yield, "Avoiding accidentally offending one another helps people to get along", since we know that "E helps to do X, by doing Y". That signifies that "Y and X have some causal connection".

  The second sentence has some overlapping concepts with the first sentence, because they both bring up etiquette, and they both bring up people getting along / social harmony.

**Answer choices**
**A. Many people who criticize etiquette have contradictory views about etiquette.** — Only One View (34% of students)
This is a super tempting answer choice and there is no accident that it's (A).

(Beware on Inference questions the traps they often lay with choice A. If it sounds like what you were thinking we were going for, make sure you read all five answers before picking it. When they know we have a guess already brewing in our heads, they like to write a slightly broken version of that and put it in the first answer spot).

If all the statements in the paragraph were true, some of them would contradict. But ... the people who criticize etiquette don't have contradictory views about etiquette.

An example of contradictory views about etiquette would be:
"Etiquette was not taught until the 1500s" paired with
"Etiquette has been taught for the last 1000 years".

These people actually only have one view about etiquette:
Etiquette has no beneficial effects for society

In order for them to have contradictory views about etiquette, they would need to ALSO be saying that "etiquette is sometimes useful / helpful for society."

But we only know about one view they have about etiquette, so there's no way for them to have contradictory views.

We know, in a sense, "these people have an INCORRECT VIEW about etiquette", given what we know about etiquette in the first sentence. However, that's different from saying "these people have CONTRADICTORY VIEWS about etiquette".

**B. Many people have respect for etiquette even though they criticize it.** — Unsupported: respect (7% of students)
The only thing we know about "many people" is criticize etiquette because they think it has no upside. We were never told that they still have respect for etiquette, so we have no support for this answer.

**C. Many people who criticize etiquette are mistaken about its beneficial effects for society.** — Correct (50% of students)
This is a softer, more accurate version of (A). They say etiquette has no beneficial effects for society, but we are told that etiquette helps people get along with each other (beneficial effect for society) and prevents people from inadvertently offending each other (beneficial effect).

If we were nervous about picking this because we feel like we're "adding too much of our outside thinking" to say that "helping people get along with each other" is "beneficial to society", then we've been doing LSAT too long. :)

I definitely had that qualm, but then reminded myself that this is Most Supported (not Must Be True) and that "helps = benefits" every day of the week. "Getting along with each other" certainly seems relevant to "society". Put those together and it basically is a Must Be True inference.

If we were told that "Because people aren't as considerate as they could be, etiquette is useful", then we can flip that into an inference like "If people were more considerate, then there would be less need for etiquette".

But we didn't talk about being inconsiderate at all. (Inadvertently offending someone is not being inconsiderate.) And making a counterfactual in which "there is NO need for etiquette" is a strong idea we don't have support for.

**E. Kindness and social harmony are highly beneficial to society** — Opinion vs. Fact / Too Strong: highly beneficial (9% of students)
Inference questions expect us to distinguish between opinions and facts. "Many people" think that kindness and social harmony are good.

We can't go from that to the more loaded factual claim that "kindness and social harmony are highly beneficial to society".

If you use a wood stove to heat your home, you should use a wood-pellet stove rather than a regular wood stove. Because wood pellets are made from by-products of manufacturing processes that would otherwise go to landfills, heating a home with a wood-pellet stove will not cause more trees to be felled. Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion drawn in the argument?

  why? because ...
- **Intermediate Conclusion (So):** Wood-pellet stoves are better for the environment than are regular wood stoves.

- **Intermediate Conclusion:** Heating a home with a wood-pellet stove will not cause more trees to be felled, whereas heating with a regular wood stove would cause more trees to be felled.

  We know the two most common places to find the Conclusion on a Main Conclusion question are
  - first sentence
  - after a pivot word (like but / yet / however)

  By priming our brain for the possibility of a First Sentence Conclusion, we'll pick up on the opinion indicator "should".   If there's no explicit supporting answer, then the claim isn't a conclusion.

  The second sentence had a "Because claim 1, claim 2" structure, meaning that claim 2 is a conclusion supported by claim 1.

  And the last sentence begins with "So", indicating that it is a conclusion supported by the previous sentence.
- **Goal:** Finding the conclusion is the hard part. Now we just have to pick an answer that matches the meaning of the first sentence.

**Answer choices**
**A. Wood pellets are made from waste products of manufacturing processes that would otherwise not be recycled.** — Premise (1% of students)
This is at the bottom of our hierarchy. No support was provided for this claim. This claim was used as support for one of the Intermediate Conclusions, that heating a home with wood-pellet won't necessitate cutting down more trees.

**B. Heating a home with a wood-pellet stove is better for the environment than is heating a home with a regular wood stove.** — Intermediate Conclusion (25% of students)
This is the reason (i.e. the support) for the first sentence, so the first sentence is the Main Conclusion.

Why should we use wood-pellets rather than regular?
because, wood-pellet is better for the environment.

**C. Using a wood-pellet stove to heat one's home does not cause trees to be felled.** — Intermediate Conclusion (1% of students)
This idea is support for the notion that wood-pellet stoves are better for the environment than are regular wood stoves, which in turn is support for the Main Conclusion in the first sentence.

**D. Using a regular wood stove to heat one's home causes trees to be felled.** — Premise (1% of students)
This is the second to last sentence. Given that the last sentence draws a conclusion based on this sentence (indicated by "So"), there's no way this second to last sentence could be the Main Conclusion.

**E. People who use wood stoves to heat their homes should use wood-pellet stoves instead of regular wood stoves.** — Correct (73% of students)
Here's an answer choice that replicates the meaning of the first sentence. This is an opinion from the author, and it's supported by the rest of the paragraph.

We should use wood-pellet, because they're more environmentally friendly, because they don't force us to cut down more trees, because wood-pellets are made from by-products of manufacturing that would otherwise end up in landfills.

## S4 · Q22 — Weaken

**Correct answer:** D

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
Economist: Gifts of cash or gift cards, which allow the recipient to choose the actual gift, are more highly valued by recipients than are gifts chosen for them by others. In a study, when people were asked how much they would have been willing to pay for gifts chosen for them by others, they responded by citing amounts that were on average only about two-thirds of the actual price of the gifts.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the economist's argument?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Cash / gift cards are more highly valued by recipients than are gifts chosen for them by others.
- **Evidence:** In a study, people were asked how much they'd pay for a series of gifts that people had bought them, and the average price they said was only about 2/3 the cash price of the gift.
- **Evaluate:** Given That
  people only said they'd spend 2/3 what their gifts were worth

  How Can We Argue That
  people value gifts given to them by others more than they would the cash equivalent?

  We could say that monetary value is only part of "value". What about sentimental value? My daughter might only pay $10 for the $15 penguin stuffed animal that I got her when I was in Patagonia, but that doesn't mean that the penguin I gave my daughter is only worth $10 to her.

  As a commodity on the shelf, she thinks it's worth about $10. As a totem of my love for her, she wants to keep it forever.

  Similarly, we often buy gifts for people that are more indulgent than what they would spend on themselves. I will only pay about $35 for a bottle of scotch, as will many of my scotch-drinking friends. But if I'm buying a gift for one of them, I'll buy the $50 bottle for them.

  Would they pay $50 for that bottle of scotch? No. But would they rather have $50 or the bottle of scotch?

  Possibly the bottle of scotch. If they just had the $50, they would spend it frugally. That's not as much fun as someone else spending $50 indulgently in some "Treat yo'self" type way.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer that talks about the more complex ways we evaluate the value of gifts (more than just what we would pay for it). Maybe an answer will provide a story where we would see more value in the overpriced but thoughtful gesture than we would in an equivalent cash gift.

**Answer choices**
**A. The rate at which gifts are returned to retailers has been steadily increasing since the rate was first measured.** — Strengthens (1% of students)
This reinforces the idea that people aren't buying us great gifts (we're returning the gifts more and more), and so it argues in favor of just giving us cash or a gift card instead so that we can' choose our own gift.

**B. Gifts of cash and gift cards currently represent only about 14 percent of all gift giving.** — Irrelevant (5% of students)
It doesn't make any difference what % of gifts are currently cash/gift cards vs. items.

If I'm trying to argue that "solar panels are more valuable than wind turbines", it doesn't affect my argument one way or the other which one is currently more prevalent.

**C. People in the study would have been willing to pay more for gifts chosen for them by close friends and relatives than for gifts chosen for them by others.** — Unclear Impact (34% of students)
At first this seems kind of tempting. Granted, the conclusion is just comparing "cash/gift cards vs. things chosen by others". The "others" isn't segregated, so do we really care about gifts from friends/relatives vs. gifts from other people?

No, but we kind of like this answer because it seems like the "what about sentimental value" objection we were thinking about.

The problem is that this answer choice doesn't control for the fact that friends/relatives are usually going to spend more on our gift than other people would. I might buy a $10 candle for someone I don't really know, whereas I'll buy that $50 bottle of scotch for my brother.

The fact that the person I gave the candle to would only pay about $6.70 for it, while my brother would only pay about $35 for the scotch might be all that this answer choice is telling us.

In both cases, the author's argument still holds. Since the recipient was only willing to pay 2/3 of what I spent, then wouldn't they have rather just had the cash I spent?

If this answer had controlled for the price of the gift, it would have been better:
"People in the study would have been willing to pay more for the same gift if it had been chosen by a friend/relative than if had been chosen by others"

This would show that there's more to our sense of value than just the object itself. The giver matters, and thus maybe sentimental value is why?

But even then it would be a weak objection, because we'd be qualifying the conclusion, "In cases where the gift comes from a friend/relative, we value the gift more than the equivalent cash value."

The conclusion is averaging all gifts together in a big lump sum comparison.

**D. People are unwilling to sell gifts chosen for them by others unless offered about one and a half times the gift's actual price.** — Correct (58% of students)
This helps us argue that "people value gifts chosen by others more than the cash/card equivalent". After all, doesn't the price they would be willing to sell something for indicate how much value it has to them?

If the selling price is 1.5 times the cash equivalent, it sounds like they value it more than if someone were to just offer them the cash.

Let's say I buy my cousin a $30 piece of art. In the study, she would have said, "I'd probably only pay $20 for that". But once it's in her possession, she says, "I wouldn't sell that for less than $45."

This answer creates a lot of doubt about the completeness of the evidence: apparently how much we would pay for something is not a sufficient metric for how much we value that item. Given that what we would pay for something is less than the actual price, but what we would need to sell it is more than the actual price, it calls the author's conclusion highly into doubt.

**E. Most retailers require receipts before people can return gifts for refund or exchange.** — Irrelevant: receipts (1% of students)
If anything this strengthens by making it seem like getting a gift that you don't want and then returning it for its cash value or exchanging it for something of equivalent value would be a headache, since we need to have the receipt the gift giver might not have given us.

## S4 · Q23 — Paradox

**Correct answer:** E

> Note: LSAT Lab offers only a single authored written explanation for this question (no Standard / Brief / Another Way selector).

### Stimulus
An antitheft device involving an electronic homing beacon has been developed for use in tracking stolen automobiles. Although its presence is undetectable to a car thief and so does not directly deter theft, its use greatly increases the odds of apprehending even the most experienced car thieves. The device is not yet used by a large percentage of car owners, but in cities where only a small percentage of car owners have the device installed, auto thefts have dropped dramatically.

### Prompt
Which one of the following, if true, would most help to explain the dramatic impact of the antitheft device?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Background:** New tracking device installed on cars is invisible to car thieves, so it doesn't directly deter theft, but once they steal a car it makes it way easier to apprehend even the best thieves.

  It's not used by many car owners yet.
- **Surprising Claim:** But in cities where even a small percentage of drivers have the tracking device installed, auto thefts have dropped dramatically.
- **Evaluate / Goal:** Given That
  in certain cities, only a small percentage of car owners have these devices installed

  How Can it Be That
  in these cities, auto thefts have dropped dramatically?

  We need some way that a small number of cars with these devices could have a big effect on car theft.

**Answer choices**
**A. Car thieves will tend to be less cautious if they are unaware that a car they have stolen contains a homing beacon.** — No Impact (11% of students)
This explains why a car thief is likely to get apprehended when they steal a car with this tracking device, but it doesn't help explain why a small % of cars having it could lead to a huge drop in car thefts.

In particular, these cars were still stolen! The fact that the thief would get sloppy and get caught doesn't remove the car theft from the police record. We need an answer where some of these apprehensions are somehow deterring lots of future car thefts.

**B. Typically, the number of cars stolen in cities where the homing beacons are in use was below average before the device was used.** — No Impact (8% of students)
It doesn't matter how the cities we're talking about compare to other cities.

The fact set we're trying to explain is about these cities NOW vs. these cities PREVIOUSLY. This answer is about "these cities PREVIOUSLY vs. other cities PREVIOUSLY)

**C. Before the invention of the homing beacon, automobile thieves who stole cars containing antitheft devices were rarely apprehended.** — No Impact (29% of students)
This answer would allow us to say that even though only a small % of cars have the device, the % of car thefts that are apprehended has gone up dramatically.

If you go from "5 car thefts were apprehended (rarely) to 25 car thefts were apprehended", that's a 400% increase.

But the problem is we don't care about the rate of apprehension. We care about the rate of car thefts. This has nothing to do with that. Whether we apprehend the thief or not, the car theft has already occurred. We're looking for a causal story where a small group of people having these tracking devices leads to way fewer cars being stolen in the first place.

**D. A large proportion of stolen cars are stolen from people who do not live in the cities where they are stolen.** — Unclear Impact (5% of students)
Are the out-of-towners people who have the tracking device? Do a higher percentage of them than the car owners of the city that they're visiting have the tracking device?

If an out-of-towner does have the device, has their car stolen, and uses the tracking device to apprehend the thief, how does that lead to a dramatic drop in car thefts in that city they were visiting?

**E. In most cities the majority of car thefts are committed by a few very experienced car thieves.** — Correct (47% of students)
This allows us to solve the paradox of "how could a small number of cars have a big effect on the city's car theft numbers"?

In most cities, most thefts are committed by a few very experienced thieves. These thieves ain't used to being apprehended. They steal a car, sell it, steal another.

However, once a small % of cars start to have these tracking devices, some of these super thieves are going to unknowingly steal one of these, get apprehended (because remember it doesn't matter how good/experienced you are), get arrested and jailed, and thus they're behind bars, instead of stealing another 100 cars.

If there are like 20 thieves responsible for most of the 1000 car thefts each year, then if we snag 10 of them from that small % of cars with the tracking device, we'll put them in jail and prevent the 500 other car thefts they would have done this year had we not busted them and thrown them in the clink.

### Stimulus
Taken as a whole, the computers that constitute the Internet form a complex, densely interconnected collection that transmits information like the neurons that form the human brain. And like a developing human brain, the Internet is growing at millions of points. So we can expect that the Internet itself will someday gain a humanlike intelligence.

### Prompt
The reasoning in the argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** The Internet will one day gain a humanlike intelligence.
- **Evidence:** The network of computers that constitute the Internet form a complex, densely interconnected assemblage that transmits information like the neurons in a human brain.

  And like a developing human brain, the Internet is growing at millions of points.
- **Evaluate:** "That door has a mail slot, which is an opening with a lid, just like a toilet has an opening with a lid. And both the door and a toilet have a handle you pull down on. Thus, we can expect that door will one day be used like a toilet."

  Just because things share a couple features doesn't mean we get to automatically assume they share some other feature.

  Maybe the Internet bears some similarities to to a human brain, but there might be certain necessary ingredients for humanlike intelligence that the Internet does not and will never have.
- **Goal:** Look for an answer objecting that the analogy might break down once we get to the issue of possessing intelligence, or an answer describing the author's illicit move, "because Internet has thing X and thing Y in common with a human brain, it will one day also have thing Z in common with a human brain".

**Answer choices**
**A. equates the complexity of an entity with the intelligence of that entity** — Too Strong (29% of students)
The author does not say "since X and Y are equally complex, X and Y should be equally intelligent". That's what this answer choice accuses the author of doing.

The author is doing something softer and subtler — "because X is a complex information transmitting collection, just as the neurons in the human brain are, and because X is growing at a million points just as a developing brain does, then one day X will have something resembling human intelligence."

**B. fails to consider the possibility that other technologies may simulate human intelligence before the Internet does so** — Not an Objection (1% of students)
This answer could only hurt the author if she had promised that the Internet will be the first thing to someday gain a humanlike intelligence.

She didn't. She doesn't have a position on whether it'll be the next thing that does, the 3rd thing that does, the 20th thing that does, etc.

**C. draws a dubious analogy between the information that is processed by the human brain and the information that is transmitted on the Internet** — Out of Scope: analogous information (19% of students)
This answer is meant to attract us since the argument was definitely an argument by analogy. But the author never discusses the type of information processed by the brain / by the Internet. She only discusses the method by which information is transmitted by each.

The analogy was saying "since both things transmit information through a dense interconnected network and since both things developed by growing at millions of points, they will both end up with the property of intelligence".

We could pretty much fix this answer by saying, "draws a dubious analogy between the 'intelligence' that the Internet might possess and the intelligence that the human brain possesses".

**D. fails to give an indication of why the characteristics it focuses on are sufficient for the eventual development of humanlike intelligence** — Correct (50% of students)
Does the author indicate why "transmitting info through dense interconnection + growing at a million different nodes" proves that the Internet will one day be intelligent?

No. Is that a logical problem? Yes. This is just saying that the author's evidence does not appear to prove the conclusion, and the author gives us no reason to think we should consider the evidence to be dispositive of the conclusion.

We might be almost disappointed by an answer that makes a humdrum complaint like, "fails to produce evidence that is sufficient to prove the conclusion" because that generic complaint is true of every flawed argument, but that doesn't make it incorrect to point that out.

If we read it less formally for matching and more conversationally, it's supposed to just sound like the objection of, "Why should we believe that dense network of info transmission + growing at million points is enough to produce intelligence? What if other stuff is needed too?"

**E. presumes, without providing justification, that the people administering the Internet are interested in developing a system with humanlike intelligence** — Out of Scope: interested (1% of students)
The author never discusses the people administering the Internet, so we can't accuse her of assuming anything about them.

If we negated this supposed assumption would it weaken?

The people administering the Internet are not interested in developing a system with humanlike intelligence.

That doesn't weaken. The people who developed nuclear fission weren't interested in developing an atomic bomb that could be dropped on Japan, but the latter still tragically happened.

Which one of the following, if true, would most strengthen the reasoning in the editorial?

### Written (single style)
**Argument breakdown**
- **Conclusion:** Democratic societies should adopt policies that ensure constant economic expansion.
- **Evidence:** Economic expansion gives people more opportunities to improve their economic standing.

  When democratic societies segment into different income classes with little economic mobility (i.e. bad income inequality), that society is endangered, since class divisions strengthen divisive political factions that impede good governance.
- **Evaluate:** In a general sense, this argument is saying
  When Y occurs, democracies face problem X.
  Thus, democracies should do thing Z.

  What is an argument like that assuming?
  It assumes that thing Z would be good at preventing Y.

  Our conclusion is recommending "policies that ensure constant economic expansion", but the evidence never talked about constant economic expansion. What is the author assuming you get from constant economic expansion?

  We would assume she's saying
  "constant economic expansion" helps us to avoid the problem of "income inequality / little mobility / divisive political factions".

**Answer choices**
**A. Discord within a society tends to increase inequities in the distribution of wealth.** — Unrelated to Goal (7% of students)
This has nothing to do with "economic expansion". It's lost in the description of the problem, telling us that one aspect (divisive discord) creates a positive feedback loop with the cause of the problem (income inequality)

The word "sometimes" is so weak (at least once) that it will almost always be wrong on Strengthen / Weaken / Paradox.

**C. Economic expansion results in a proportionally greater increase in earnings for people at low income levels than for people at other income levels.** — Correct (65% of students)
This connects our New Term in the conclusion "economic expansion" to the pressure points of the problem we're trying to avoid (income inequality with little economic mobility).

Economic expansion will cause upward mobility for the poorer segments, at the same time reducing income inequality (by narrowing the margin between poor and rich).

**D. Economic expansion cannot occur unless there is significant financial investment in the economy by people at the highest income levels.** — Irrelevant: can't occur vs. should occur / Weakens, if Anything (3% of students)
This isn't really relevant because whether it can / can't occur is a separate discussion from whether it should / shouldn't occur.

But additionally this answer seems to give us some pessimism about achieving economic expansion, so it sounds like it would move away from, not towards, the author's conclusion.

**E. The presence of divisive political factions can be an obstacle to economic expansion.** — Irrelevant: can't occur vs. should occur / Weakens, if Anything (14% of students)
