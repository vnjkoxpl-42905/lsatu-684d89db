## Q1 — Agree/Disagree

**Stimulus**

Rhett: Otto gives me a ride to work every morning and expects me to help pay for fuel. But I shouldn't have to pay. Giving me a ride doesn't increase the amount he spends on fuel, since he has to go right by my house anyway.

Barbara: By that flawed logic, you would be entitled to the warm air that heats the unused spare room of my house, if you could divert it to your house without increasing my expenses.

**Written Explanation**

*Rhett's Claims*
1. Otto gives me a ride to work every morning.
2. Otto expects me to help pay for fuel.
3. I shouldn't have to pay.
4. Giving me a ride doesn't increase the amount he spends on fuel.
5. He has to go right by my house anyway.

*Barbara's Responses*
1. no comment
2. no comment
3. Disagrees
4. no comment (possibly agrees)
5. no comment

*Evaluate:* Barbara doesn't dispute any of the facts that Rhett presents as evidence, such as Rhett's claim #1, #2, or #5. She might even agree with claim #4, because she mentions diverting warm air from her house "without increasing my expenses." So, when Barbara states that Rhett's logic is "flawed," she seems to be disagreeing with his conclusion that he should not have to pay Otto.

*Goal:* We'll look for an answer that addresses claim #3.

**Question:** The dialogue provides the most support for the claim that Rhett and Barbara disagree over whether

- **(A)** Otto requires Rhett to help pay for fuel for the ride to work — *Too Strong.* Rhett never even says that Otto "requires" Rhett to pay, only that Otto expects him to pay. Otto might expect Rhett to pay, but might still reluctantly give Rhett a ride without getting any money for fuel. (3% of students)
- **(B)** Rhett should have to help pay Otto's fuel expenses — **Correct.** This is claim #3. Rhett concludes that he should not have to pay Otto. Barbara indicates that this is a flawed conclusion. (89% of students)
- **(C)** giving Rhett a ride to work increases Otto's fuel expenses — *Unsupported Agree Position.* Rhett states the opposite of this, and there is no indication at all that Barbara disagrees with Rhett. There is actually some indication that she might agree with Rhett. (7% of students)
- **(D)** Rhett is entitled to the warm air that heats Barbara's unused spare room — *Unsupported Agree Position.* The way that Barbara uses the warm air in her analogy indicates that she would not agree with this statement: she seems to think that this would be flawed logic. If anything, the fact that Barbara is using this analogy in her reply to Rhett indicates she thinks Rhett might agree with her view on taking warm air from her spare room. But we don't actually know what Rhett thinks. (1% of students)
- **(E)** Rhett could divert to his house the warm air that heats Barbara's unused spare room without increasing her expenses — *Unsupported Agree Position.* Barbara gives this as a hypothetical situation ("if you could divert it..."). So we don't even know if Barbara thinks this is actually possible. And we have no idea what Rhett thinks about this. (0% of students)

## Q2 — Sufficient Assumption

**Stimulus**

Classicist: In the ancient Athenian democracy, unlike in any other political system in world history, the legislature consisted of all eligible voters. Athenian voters, not elected representatives, made all political decisions by direct vote after public debate in the Assembly. Thus, if the world has ever known true democracy, it was only in ancient Athens.

**Written Explanation**

*Conclusion (Thus):* If the world has ever known true democracy, it was only in ancient Athens.

*Evidence:* It was only in ancient Athens that the legislature consisted of all eligible voters. Athenian voters made all political decisions by direct vote after public debate in the Assembly.

*Evaluate:* The conclusion we're trying to prove is a weird type of claim. Suppose I said, "Thus, if Shelly has any flowers in her kitchen right now, she has daisies". I'm not actually trying to prove she has daisies. I'm saying daisies are the only possible type of flower in her kitchen, but it wouldn't make me wrong if there weren't daisies. I would only be wrong if there were some other type of flower in her kitchen. So, similarly, we don't need to prove that ancient Athens was a true democracy. We just need to know that every other political system has not been a true democracy. Since the argument didn't give us a proving mechanism for "If xyz, then not true democracy", the correct answer will have to give us such a rule. What do we know about all other political systems that would allow us to prove they aren't true democracies? We know that only ancient Athens had a legislature consisting of all eligible voters. So in every other political system, we know that wasn't the case.

*Goal:* So, we're probably going to see an answer like, "If a political system didn't have a legislature consisting of all eligible voters, then it wasn't a true democracy".

**Question:** The conclusion of the classicist's argument follows logically if which one of the following is assumed?

- **(A)** Most political systems in world history have not been democracies. — *Unrelated to Goal.* Since this doesn't provide a rule that says, "If xyz, then not true democracy", it won't help us to prove the conclusion. (1% of students)
- **(B)** Public debate is an essential part of any democratic decision-making process. — *Unrelated to Goal.* (1% of students)
- **(C)** Athens was the only city in ancient Greece that had a democratic political system. — *Unrelated to Goal.* (2% of students)
- **(D)** A political system is not a true democracy unless the eligible voters themselves vote directly on all political decisions. — **Correct.** This is one of those fun Sufficient Assumption examples where the "New Concept in Conclusion" shortcut takes us all the way to the correct answer choice. We're trying to prove that all other political systems besides ancient Athens were not true democracies. This is the only answer that provides a rule that would allow us to prove "not true democracy": Eligible voters don't themselves vote directly on all political decisions → not a true democracy. We were told that ancient Athens was the only political system in world history in which the legislature consisted of all eligible voters, who made all political decisions by direct vote. So we know that this rule applies to every other political system, proving that none of them were true democracies. (94% of students)
- **(E)** Most Athenians who were eligible to vote attended the Assembly whenever it was debating important political decisions. — *Unrelated to Goal.* (2% of students)

## Q3 — Role of a Claim

**Stimulus**

Near-Earth objects (NEOs) such as asteroids threaten the Earth because they have the potential to collide with it. The goal of NEO research is to develop measures to counteract a possible hit by a sizable NEO. Government funding of this research is not a waste of money. Buying home insurance makes good fiscal sense, and governments fund NEO research for the same reason that people insure their homes.

**Written Explanation**

*Conclusion (not a waste = opinion):* Government funding of NEO research is not a waste of money.

*Evidence:* Buying home insurance makes good fiscal sense, and governments fund NEO research for the same reason that people insure their homes (it's designed to develop measures to counteract a possible hit by a sizable NEO).

*Evaluate:* Whenever we're trying to find the conclusion on Role and Main Conclusion, we remind ourselves that 90% of the arguments on those two question types are "upside-down" arguments: we hear the Conclusion before we hear the Evidence. So we have to listen keenly for Opinion indicators or Rebuttals. The first two sentences provide background context so that we know what NEO research is all about. Then the 3rd sentence gives us the author's opinion: government funding of NEO research is "worth it / not a waste".

*Goal:* We're being asked about the 2nd to last claim, which was one of the two premises. (99% of the time that two claims are joined by "and", they are both premises.) It also happens to be invoking an analogy between buying home insurance and funding NEO research. So let's look for something like "an analogy / premise that supports the 3rd sentence".

**Question:** The statement that buying home insurance makes good fiscal sense plays which one of the following roles in the argument?

- **(A)** It connects an analogy made in the argument to the argument's conclusion. — **Correct.** This wording is not instantly lovable, but it's not broken, so it ultimately wins the prize. The author is arguing that it's good fiscal sense to invest in NEO research just as it's good fiscal sense to buy home insurance (better to spend money now to avert having to spend even more money later). The idea that this "connects" the analogy back to the argument's conclusion actually seems more appropriate as a description for the final claim, not the 2nd to last claim. But this seems to be the best available answer. (88% of students)
- **(B)** It is the overall conclusion of the argument. — *Wrong Role.* The conclusion is the 3rd sentence. We're looking for something like "premise". (1% of students)
- **(C)** It defines a key term used in a premise of the argument. — *Wrong Role.* The only key term in the argument is NEO, and that is defined in the first sentence. (1% of students)
- **(D)** It provides a contrast to the situation that is the main focus of the argument. — *Opposite.* Bringing up home insurance is not meant to provide a contrast; it's meant to provide an analogous similarity. (4% of students)
- **(E)** It is a general principle for which the argument attempts to provide support. — *Out of Scope: support provided.* We could probably live with calling "it makes good fiscal sense to buy home insurance" a general principle. But the argument never tries to provide support for this. (7% of students)

## Q4 — Agree/Disagree

**Stimulus**

Oscar: Clearly, student evaluations provide the best assessment of teacher performance. Who is in a better position to judge a teacher than that teacher's students?

Bettina: Student evaluations of teachers are usually conducted at the end of a class. However, students generally fail to appreciate the impact of a teacher until many years later. So, peer evaluations of teachers might be a good supplement or a better alternative.

**Written Explanation**

*Given Oscar's Claims*
1. Student evaluations provide the best assessment of teacher performance.
2. Who is in a better position to judge a teacher than that teacher's students? [Rhetorical question — Oscar believes students are in the best position to judge a teacher.]

*Evaluate Bettina's Responses*
1. Disagrees
2. no comment

It's interesting to note that Bettina doesn't necessarily disagree that students are in the best position to judge a teacher. What Bettina is saying is that students' impressions at the end of a class might not accurately reflect the impact that the teacher has. Students' judgements might be valid and accurate, but possibly not until many years later. Bettina's statement about peer evaluations being a better alternative directly refutes Oscar's claim about student evaluations being best.

*Goal:* We'll look for an answer that addresses claim #1. There were more details in Bettina's statements than in Oscar's, so beware of answers which focus on details about which Oscar never expressed an opinion.

**Question:** The dialogue provides the most support for the claim that Oscar and Bettina disagree over whether

- **(A)** students are ever capable of adequately judging a teacher's performance — *Both Agree.* The words "ever" and "adequately" are important. Oscar believes that students can adequately judge a teacher's performance right away. Bettina indicates that students might eventually be able to do this, but it will take many years. (6% of students)
- **(B)** students' opinions may change over a period of many years — *Half Scope.* Bettina agrees with this. None of Oscar's statements indicate agreement or disagreement. (1% of students)
- **(C)** student evaluations furnish the optimal assessment of teacher performance — **Correct.** Oscar agrees with this. Bettina believes that peer evaluations are a better alternative. (91% of students)
- **(D)** teacher performance should be assessed without conducting any student evaluations — *Half Scope.* Oscar disagrees with this. Bettina claims that peer evaluations "might be a good supplement," which means student evaluations could still be used, but should not be relied on as the best way. (2% of students)
- **(E)** student evaluations are usually conducted at the end of a class — *Half Scope.* Bettina states this. Nothing in Oscar's statements indicates disagreement. (0% of students)

## Q5 — Flaw

**Stimulus**

Taste buds were the primary tool early humans used for testing foods. Sour taste warns of possible spoilage of food while bitterness is a warning of many poisons. Early humans also recognized sweet foods and salty foods as meeting nutritional needs. So the fact that people can now clearly distinguish these four tastes—sour, bitter, sweet, and salty—is completely explained by people's use of taste to test for the healthfulness of foods.

**Written Explanation**

*Evidence:* Taste buds were the primary tool early humans used for testing foods. Sour taste warns of possible spoilage of food while bitterness is a warning of many poisons. Early humans also recognized sweet foods and salty foods as meeting nutritional needs.

*Conclusion:* The fact that people can now clearly distinguish these four tastes—sour, bitter, sweet, and salty—is completely explained by people's use of taste to test for the healthfulness of foods.

*Evaluate:* Wow! Completely explained? That's a mighty strong conclusion. The evidence describes details about food that would have been important for early humans to know, like possible spoilage, and describes a correlation between these details and certain tastes like sourness and bitterness. This correlation doesn't guarantee that needing to know about spoilage caused our ability to detect a sour taste. It doesn't guarantee that the need for nutrients caused our ability to detect sweetness or saltiness. The evidence doesn't guarantee that this one use for our sense of taste completely explains our ability to distinguish these four tastes. The ability to distinguish these four tastes could have developed for more than one reason.

*Goal:* Since this is such a strong conclusion, a correct answer only needs to point out that there could be more than one reason for our ability to distinguish these four tastes.

**Question:** The reasoning in the argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument

- **(A)** takes a necessary condition for the truth of its conclusion to be sufficient to justify that conclusion — *Necessary vs. Sufficient.* This describes a conditional logic flaw (Evidence: A → B, B, Conclusion: A). This argument doesn't use a conditional statement as evidence and then draw a conclusion based on an illegal reversal. (5% of students)
- **(B)** fails to consider that many people associate foods more with their smells than with their tastes — *Irrelevant Comparison.* "Fails to consider" means this should weaken the argument. Some people associating foods with smell more than taste doesn't prove or disprove the claim about why we can distinguish a certain four tastes. (2% of students)
- **(C)** fails to consider that some nutritious foods are bitter when raw but not after being cooked — *Irrelevant Comparison.* Without more information, this doesn't really impact the argument. (2% of students)
- **(D)** fails to consider that most early humans ate a much more limited range of foodstuffs than do contemporary people — *Irrelevant Comparison.* It's not clear how this comparison impacts the argument. (4% of students)
- **(E)** takes what might be only a partial explanation of a phenomenon to be the complete explanation — **Correct.** The need to test food could certainly help explain why people can now clearly distinguish these four tastes. It might easily be a partial explanation. But the evidence doesn't support a claim that the need to test food completely explains our ability to distinguish these tastes. (87% of students)

## Q6 — Paradox

**Stimulus**

To discourage congestion, a city raised on-street parking rates in a downtown business district. For restaurants in the district, whose customers require short-term parking, the surprising result was a small increase in sales.

**Written Explanation**

*Given that:* The city raised on-street parking rates in a downtown business district in order to discourage congestion.

*Why is it that:* Restaurants in the district that require short-term parking ended up having a small increase in sales.

*Evaluate:* Raising the price of parking meters is supposed to discourage people from street parking; the fewer cars driving around searching for an open meter, the less congestion. But after this city raised the price of parking meters, restaurants whose customers have to find short-term parking did even a little better than before. We would expect these restaurants to struggle now that customers are thinking, "Isn't that the place that requires street parking? Didn't they just raise the meter rates? Let's go somewhere else."

*Goal:* On Paradox, predicting is borderline risky since the correct answer might surprise us. Let's just look for a way to explain something that changed since the meters got more expensive that could somehow slightly benefit restaurants in the area.

**Question:** Which one of the following, if true, contributes to an explanation of the increase in sales at restaurants in the district?

- **(A)** Customers of the district's businesses can also use private parking lots, which are generally more expensive than on-street parking. — *No Impact / Distinction.* This could work if the private lots were cheaper, but if they're more expensive, then parking is still more expensive overall, which should discourage people. The lots were presumably always more expensive, so nothing has changed. (4% of students)
- **(B)** Owners of the district's businesses, for the most part, initially opposed the raising of the on-street parking rates. — *No Impact / Distinction.* It doesn't matter whether owners supported or opposed the measure. It passed. Learning how owners felt before doesn't give us anything that changed. (0% of students)
- **(C)** Even before the change in parking rates, the staffs of the businesses in the district used only off-street parking. — *No Distinction.* "Even before the change" flags this as wrong; it describes something equally true before and after. (1% of students)
- **(D)** More expensive parking leads to greater turnover in cars parked in front of the businesses in the district. — **Correct.** This is the only answer that speaks to something that changed. Two possible angles: (1) People who wanted Restaurant X but were often stymied by lack of parking would give up; with greater turnover there are more opportunities to park in front, so more customers can dine. (2) With expensive meters, diners hurry their meals (pay for 1 hour and finish swiftly vs. a leisurely 90-minute meal), so restaurants can serve more customers per day and make slightly more money. (93% of students)
- **(E)** The business district is in competition with malls that offer free parking to their customers. — *Deepens Paradox.* If parking fees go up and the alternative is a free-parking mall, that should push people to malls and hurt district restaurants. (2% of students)

## Q7 — Agree/Disagree

**Stimulus**

Mark: Advances in technology have caused the decline of newspaper sales. News can be reported in articles on the Internet as it happens and at any time of day. No print newspaper can possibly keep up with that.

Fatuma: Newspaper writing is hampered by conventions that do not add to readers' understanding of the news. It is not the fault of technology that newspapers are losing their audience to the Internet. Newspaper articles are just too long. On the Internet, news articles get to the point.

**Written Explanation**

*Given Mark's Claims*
1. Advances in technology have caused the decline of newspaper sales.
2. News can be reported in articles on the Internet as it happens and at any time of day.
3. No print newspaper can possibly keep up with that.

*Evaluate Fatuma's Responses*
1. Disagrees
2. no comment
3. no comment

Fatuma and Mark disagree about the cause of declining newspaper sales. Fatuma's second sentence reveals the point of disagreement: Mark believes technology caused the decline, while Fatuma states "it is not the fault of technology that newspapers are losing their audience to the Internet."

*Goal:* We'll look for an answer that addresses claim #1. Beware of answers focusing on details only one person commented on.

**Question:** Mark and Fatuma disagree over the truth of which one of the following?

- **(A)** Readers are abandoning newspapers in favor of news articles on the Internet because of the speed with which they appear on the Internet. — **Correct.** This summarizes Mark's claim about technology causing the decline. Fatuma claims readers are abandoning newspapers due to how newspaper articles are written, not how quickly news appears online. (92% of students)
- **(B)** Newspaper articles are too long. — *Half Scope.* Fatuma states this. Mark doesn't comment. (1% of students)
- **(C)** News articles on the Internet get to the point. — *Half Scope.* Fatuma states this. Mark doesn't comment. (0% of students)
- **(D)** Newspaper writing is hampered by conventions that do not add to the reader's understanding of the news. — *Half Scope.* Fatuma states this. Mark doesn't comment. (5% of students)
- **(E)** No print newspaper can possibly publish news as fast as it can be published on the Internet. — *Half Scope.* Mark states this. Fatuma doesn't comment. (2% of students)

## Q8 — Complete the Argument (Analogy)

**Stimulus**

Statistics teachers often debate whether to teach the mathematical theories underlying statistics or to focus on the use of statistical methods to solve real problems. This is like the difference between learning to build a car and learning to drive one. Almost all statistics courses teach "building," and if there is extra time, they may cover a tip or two on "driving." But, just as ordinary drivers do not need to know how to build a car in order to drive one, _______.

**Written Explanation**

*Setup Claims:* There's a debate among stats teachers whether to teach the math theories underlying statistics or to just focus on using statistics to solve real problems. This is like the difference between learning to build a car and learning to drive one. Currently, almost all stats courses teach "building" and, time permitting, also teach "driving".

*The Blank:* Just as ordinary drivers don't need to know how to build a car in order to drive one, ______.

*Evaluate:* Because of the "Just as" start, our job is to Complete the Analogy. In this analogy, "building a car" = teaching the math theories that underlie statistics; "driving a car" = focusing on the use of statistical methods to solve real problems.

*Goal:* Finishing the sentence match-for-match: "Ordinary users of statistics do not need to know the math theories that underlie statistics in order to use statistical methods to solve real problems." In a gist-y sense, this reflects the author's judgment that we should teach less of the underlying math theories and more of the real-world use-cases.

**Question:** Which one of the following most logically completes the argument?

- **(A)** students should not be presumed by their teachers to have a particular goal or purpose in taking a class — *Out of Scope: goal of taking class.* No part of the analogy involves the goal/purpose of taking the class or of learning to drive. (0% of students)
- **(B)** statistics should be considered as a discipline that is distinct from mathematics — *Unrelated to Analogy.* It's already implied stats and math are distinct. This wouldn't complete the analogy. (0% of students)
- **(C)** statistics teachers should focus on providing their students with the skills that they are most likely to use — *Weak Match / Out of Scope: most likely.* Somewhat tempting since it goes in the right direction, but it doesn't talk about "less teaching of underlying math" or "how to solve problems." Our job is to carry over the language from the first half of the sentence. (9% of students)
- **(D)** users of statistics do not need to understand the underlying theory in order to solve real problems — **Correct.** This carries over the language from the first half of the sentence to the other half of the analogy: "similarly, ordinary stats users do not need to know the underlying math theories in order to use stats to solve real problems." (90% of students)
- **(E)** statisticians do not need to understand the needs of their students in order to become teachers — *Out of Scope: to become teachers.* Nothing in the paragraph was about hiring requirements for stats teachers. (0% of students)

## Q9 — Flaw

**Stimulus**

Gecko lizards are found in any environment where there is an abundant population of gnats. Gnats can survive only in wet climates. Because there are no gecko lizards living here, there must not be an abundant population of gnats here. Consequently, the climate here must not be wet.

**Written Explanation**

*Conclusion (consequently):* The climate here must not be wet.

*Intermediate Conclusion (must):* There aren't lots of gnats here.

*Evidence (because):* There are no geckos here. Anywhere you find lots of gnats, you'll find Geckos (Lots of gnats → Geckos). Gnats can survive only in wet climates (Gnats → wet climate).

*Evaluate:* Flaw question with conditional logic indicators (any, only) — deeply suspect the #1 Famous Flaw, Necessary vs. Sufficient. The author draws two conclusions. The first (intermediate) conclusion is that there must not be abundant gnats, because there are no geckos, and geckos are found in every environment with abundant gnats (~Geckos → ~Lots of Gnats; Here = ~Geckos; thus Here = ~Lots of Gnats). This mini-argument is airtight (it applies the contrapositive). The main conclusion is that the climate is not wet. We were told gnats survive only in a wet climate (Gnats → Wet climate; ~Wet climate → ~Gnats). The author's move to the final claim is "If there is not an abundant population of gnats, then it must not be a wet climate" (~Gnats → ~Wet climate). That's an illegal negation.

*Goal:* Look for an answer describing the Necessary vs. Sufficient error on the 2nd conditional (gnats/wet climate), or the conversational objection: "Just because there are no gnats doesn't mean it's not a wet climate."

**Question:** The reasoning in the argument is flawed in that it

- **(A)** presumes, without providing justification, that gecko lizards can survive only in environments with abundant gnat populations — *Not Assumed / Too Strong: only.* This answer is itself making a Necessary vs. Sufficient error. We were told abundant gnats are sufficient to indicate geckos (lots of gnats → geckos); this says the author assumed abundant gnats are necessary for geckos (geckos → lots of gnats). This would work only if the author had messed up the first sentence's conditional; he botched the second. (24% of students)
- **(B)** presumes, without providing justification, that all wet environments contain abundant populations of gnats — **Correct.** Did the author assume Wet climate → abundant gnats (contrapositive: ~abundant gnats → ~Wet climate)? Yes — the author's final move was "There is no abundant population of gnats here. Consequently, the climate must not be wet" (~abundant gnats → ~Wet climate). Correct answers are usually written in a contraposed form. (68% of students)
- **(C)** does not consider whether small populations of gnats can survive in climates that are not wet — *Not an Objection.* Can we hurt the argument by saying "small populations of gnats can survive in non-wet climates"? No — the author can allow for a small population of gnats and still think the climate isn't wet. A correct version would be "does not consider whether small or no populations of gnats are sometimes found in climates that are wet." (4% of students)
- **(D)** does not mention whether gecko lizards eat anything besides gnats — *Irrelevant.* What geckos eat has nothing to do with the logical flaw. (2% of students)
- **(E)** fails to establish that some gecko lizards could not survive in a dry climate containing only a small population of gnats — *Not Assumed.* Negate it: "all geckos could survive in a dry climate with few gnats." That wouldn't hurt the argument. (2% of students)

## Q10 — Parallel Reasoning

**Stimulus**

If Thompson appeals to moderates, his most ardent supporters will desert him and he will not have enough votes to win the election. However, if he does not appeal to moderates, these moderates will vote for his opponent and he will not have enough votes to win the election. Either way, Thompson is bound to lose the election.

**Written Explanation**

*Evidence:* If Thompson appeals to moderates, he will not have enough votes to win (TAM → ~WE). If he does not appeal to moderates, he will not have enough votes to win (~TAM → ~WE).

*Conclusion:* Either way, Thompson is bound to lose (~WE).

*Evaluate:* No clear premise/conclusion indicators, but two statements are conditional and the final sentence is a prediction based on them. Predictions tend to be conclusions. The conditionals describe a binary choice (appeal or not appeal), and either choice results in Thompson not winning. The conclusion confirms that outcome.

*Goal:* Look for an answer with a binary situation where either option results in the same outcome, and a conclusion confirming that outcome. Beware of answers whose evidence isn't actually binary (more than two possibilities) or whose conclusion doesn't simply confirm a necessary outcome.

**Question:** Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its reasoning to the argument above?

- **(A)** If Chen supports building a new art center, the center will be built. If Chen opposes it, the center will not be built. So whether or not a new art center will be built depends on Chen's decision. — *Bad Evidence Match / Bad Conclusion Match.* Two different options produce two different outcomes (original: same outcome). And the conclusion doesn't assert one outcome will occur. (0% of students)
- **(B)** If the company decides to relocate, it will lose a number of employees, since many are unwilling to move. But if it stays, it will lose a number of employees, since many are unhappy in this location. So the company will lose employees no matter what it decides. — **Correct.** relocate → lose employees; ~relocate → lose employees; therefore lose employees. Matches the structure and pattern. (96% of students)
- **(C)** If money is not appropriated to fix the city roads, the pothole problem will get out of control. And if the pothole problem gets out of control, the city councilors will all be replaced. So the city council has no choice but to appropriate money. — *Bad Evidence Match / Bad Validity Match.* Two conditionals that chain together (not two separate conditionals leading to the same outcome). Conclusion rests on an assumption that the council must prevent its members from being replaced. (1% of students)
- **(D)** If the city council approves a small property-tax increase, there will be enough money to restore the swimming pools. If it approves a large increase, there will be enough to restore pools and build a rec center. Either way, the city will make progress in improving Rice Park. — *Bad Evidence Match.* Not a binary situation (could keep taxes the same or reduce them), and it describes more than one possible outcome. (2% of students)
- **(E)** If Madsen supports the proposed shopping mall, she will make an effective spokesperson. But if she opposes it, there will be no difficulty finding another effective spokesperson. So the project's success does not depend on Madsen's decision. — *Bad Evidence Match / Bad Conclusion Match.* More than one possible outcome, and the conclusion says success does not depend on Madsen (not that a certain outcome will occur). (0% of students)

## Q11 — Sufficient Assumption

**Stimulus**

It is morally praiseworthy to be honest only if one is honest out of respect for morality. Strictly speaking, therefore, Downing did not act in a manner worthy of such praise when he told the judge the truth about his business partner's fraudulence. Downing was motivated by concern for his own well-being—even though what he did was certainly honest.

**Written Explanation**

*Conclusion (therefore):* Telling the judge the truth about his business partner's fraudulence was not worthy of praise.

*Evidence:* If you're not being honest out of respect for morality, then it's not worthy of praise. He was honest, but he was motivated by concern for his own well-being.

*Evaluate:* We're trying to prove that a certain action was "not worthy of moral praise". Did the argument provide a proving-mechanism like "If xyz, then not worthy of moral praise"? It did — a conditional premise: as long as you can establish that X was not being honest out of respect for morality, then X's being honest is not worthy of moral praise. So we need to establish that Downing's honesty was not out of respect for morality. Did the argument give a proving-mechanism for "not out of respect for morality"? No — we only know Downing was motivated by concern for his own well-being.

*Goal:* We need to hear "If you were motivated by concern for your own well-being, then you were not motivated by a respect for morality."

**Question:** The conclusion drawn above follows logically if which one of the following is assumed?

- **(A)** An action motivated by concern for oneself cannot be deserving of moral condemnation. — *Out of Scope: moral condemnation.* We need "an action motivated by concern for oneself cannot also be done out of respect for morality". This won't prove Downing's honesty wasn't out of respect for morality. (5% of students)
- **(B)** Some actions that are essentially honest are not morally praiseworthy. — *Too Weak: some / Unrelated to Goal.* One-data-point answers are almost always wrong on Sufficient Assumption; "Some" is an immediate red flag. Won't prove the needed link. (10% of students)
- **(C)** An action performed out of respect for morality cannot also be an action motivated by concern for oneself. — **Correct.** Like most correct Sufficient Assumption answers, it's given in contrapositive form: Performed out of respect for morality → not motivated by concern for self; contrapositive: Motivated by concern for self → not performed out of respect for morality. This provides the missing link (evidence connects Downing's action → motivated by concern for self, and "not out of respect for morality" → not worthy of praise; this answer bridges the two). (82% of students)
- **(D)** The moral praiseworthiness of an action can be judged only according to standards that refer to the circumstances of the person acting. — *Unrelated to Goal.* Won't prove Downing's honesty wasn't out of respect for morality. (1% of students)
- **(E)** Morality demands that one be honest, even in cases where this could be detrimental to one's own well-being. — *Unrelated to Goal.* (1% of students)

## Q12 — Flaw (Ad Hominem)

**Stimulus**

Art history professor: Costa criticizes my theories about the distinction between baroque and neoclassical Austrian painting. He argues that since there are no features possessed by all and only the works from a given historical period, assigning works of art to period styles is intellectually bankrupt. His reasoning can be discounted, however, since his own current theories on the transition from classical to romantic French opera also presuppose such an assignment.

**Written Explanation**

*Conclusion:* Costa's reasoning can be discounted.

*Evidence (since):* His own current theories presuppose the same assignment he's criticizing me for.

*Evaluate:* The details of what Costa said are immaterial to recognizing the Famous Flaw. On Flaw with a Rebuttal-style argument, look for two of the 10 Famous Flaws: (1) Ad Hominem — dismissing person X's view because X has a biased interest or conflicting past behavior; (2) Unproven vs. Proven False — concluding X is false due to lack of evidence. The conclusion doesn't say Costa is wrong, so it's not #2; it says Costa's view can be discounted/dismissed, which is #1. Why discount Costa? Because "Costa does the same thing" — like getting busted for playing with matches and arguing "Timmy did the same thing!" Mom will say both did something wrong. Someone who commits tax fraud should still be listened to when accusing X of tax fraud.

*Goal:* Look for "Just because Costa is guilty of X doesn't mean we should discount Costa when he argues that we are guilty of X." Or that the author fails to address Costa's substantive point, distracting with irrelevant details about what Costa does.

**Question:** Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the art history professor's argument?

- **(A)** The argument confuses a necessary condition for discounting a person's reasoning with a sufficient condition for discounting a person's reasoning. — *Not Necessary vs. Sufficient.* There's no conditional logic premise here. The only premise is "Costa's own theories presuppose such an assignment". (2% of students)
- **(B)** The argument overlooks the possibility that theoreticians can hold radically different theories at different times. — *Not an Objection.* Doesn't weaken; the premise is about Costa's current theories. (2% of students)
- **(C)** The argument rejects the reasoning on which a criticism is based merely on the grounds that that very criticism could be applied to theories of the person who offered it. — **Correct.** A "conflicting behavior" version of Ad Hominem ("My dad tells me not to smoke, but he smokes, so his reasoning can be discounted" — he could be right about the advice, wrong in his behavior). Structurally, "does X merely on the grounds that Y": X matches the conclusion (rejecting the reasoning behind a criticism — Costa's reasoning should be discounted), Y matches the evidence (the same criticism applies to the critic's own theories — Costa's own theories presuppose the assignment he calls intellectually bankrupt). (89% of students)
- **(D)** The argument presumes, without providing justification, that what is true of art in general must also be true of every particular type of art. — *Not Part vs. Whole.* The premise isn't about "art in general", and the conclusion isn't about "every particular style of art". (4% of students)
- **(E)** The argument presumes, without providing justification, that theories about one type of art cannot be compared to theories about another. — *Not an Assumption.* The author isn't assuming that extreme claim; she only talks about Costa's theories, and seems to think at least some theories can be compared. (4% of students)

## Q13 — Necessary Assumption

**Stimulus**

Columnist: Computer voice-recognition technology currently cannot distinguish between homophones such as "their" and "there." As a consequence, until voice-recognition technology is improved to recognize and utilize grammatical and semantic relations among words, voice-recognition programs will not accurately translate a computer user's spoken words into written text.

**Written Explanation**

*Conclusion:* Until voice recognition technology can recognize and utilize grammatical and semantic clues, voice recognition will not accurately translate text.

*Evidence:* Voice recognition cannot currently accurately tell the difference between homophones.

*Evaluate:* General form: "Problem X currently prevents Outcome Y. Thus, until we do Action Z, we won't have Outcome Y." This assumes Action Z is necessary to solve Problem X. But what if other actions could also solve Problem X? Then we needn't say "If we don't take Action Z, we'll never get Outcome Y."

*Goal:* The author assumes "if we don't take advantage of grammatical and semantic clues, we'll never solve the homophones problem" — i.e., "solving the homophones problem requires using grammatical and semantic clues."

**Question:** Which one of the following is an assumption required by the columnist's argument?

- **(A)** In order for computer voice-recognition technology to distinguish between homophones, it must be able to recognize grammatical and semantic relations among words. — **Correct.** Negation: "there is a way for voice-recognition to distinguish homophones that doesn't involve recognizing grammatical/semantic relations." Because the conclusion is so strongly worded, any room for doubt is a big objection. This provides the link: ~recognizing grammatical/semantic relations → can't distinguish homophones (→ won't accurately translate). (53% of students)
- **(B)** If voice-recognition technology is improved to recognize and utilize grammatical and semantic relations, voice-recognition programs will accurately translate spoken words into text. — *Opposite Logic of Conclusion.* The author said "until we have X, we won't have Y"; this accuses him of thinking "once we have X, we will have Y." Those aren't equivalent (X is necessary, not sufficient — like strawberry seeds). (19% of students)
- **(C)** Humans can distinguish between homophones without using information about the grammatical and semantic relations among words. — *Out of Scope: how humans distinguish.* No reason the computer's method must match humans'; and the author likely thinks humans DO use grammatical/semantic relations, so this is closer to the opposite. (1% of students)
- **(D)** Unless voice-recognition technology can distinguish between words that are homophones, it cannot recognize grammatical and semantic relations among words. — *Reversed Logic.* The author assumes "if it can't recognize grammatical/semantic stuff, it can't distinguish homophones"; this reverses it ("if it can't distinguish homophones, it can't recognize grammatical/semantic stuff"). (27% of students)
- **(E)** Computer programs that check the spelling and grammar of written text are currently able to distinguish between homophones. — *Out of Scope: what spell check can do.* The argument is only about what voice recognition needs; what other tools can do is irrelevant. (1% of students)
