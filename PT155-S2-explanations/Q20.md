# PrepTest 155 · Section 2 · Question 20

**Type:** Sufficient Assumption
**Correct answer:** E

## Stimulus

Social observer: Advertising agencies are willfully neglecting the most profitable segment of the market: older adults. Older adults control more of this nation's personal disposable income than does the rest of the population combined. Therefore, advertising agencies can maximize their clients' profits if they gear their advertisements mainly to older adults.

## Question stem

Which one of the following, if assumed, would enable the conclusion of the social observer's argument to be properly inferred?

## Answer choices

**A.** Older people generally have larger incomes and have had longer to accumulate resources than younger people.

**B.** No company can maximize its profits unless it markets its products primarily to a population segment that controls most of this nation's personal disposable income.

**C.** Advertising that is directed toward the wealthiest people is the most effective means for a business to improve the reputation of its products.

**D.** No advertising agency that tailors its advertisements mainly to an audience that does not control much of this nation's personal disposable income will maximize its clients' profits.

**E.** Any advertising agency that gears its advertisements mainly to a population segment that controls 50 percent or more of this nation's personal disposable income will maximize its clients' profits.

## Written explanation

**Conclusion**

If ad agencies gear their ads mainly to older adults, they can maximize clients' profits.

**Evidence**

Older adults control more of this nation's personal disposable income than does the rest of the population combined.

**Evaluate**

If this were anything other than Sufficient Assumption, I would be tempted to start marshaling counterarguments, like "yes, but ... older people's consumer habits are already stuck, so ads don't make as much different", or "most of the money they spend is on behalf of other people, so ads should target those people".

But Sufficient Assumption just needs a math-y connector that adds on to what we're told in the premise and allows us to derive the conclusion.

Since there is a New Idea in the Conclusion, (maximizing clients' profits), that idea must be in the correct answer.

The one thing we know from the Evidence that we can swap out in the conclusion is the detail we're told about "older adults" (they're the overlapping idea).

**Goal**

Maybe something like, "If you gear your ads mainly to [the most profitable segment of the market] / [the people who control more personal disposable income than the rest of the population combined], then you'll maximize your clients' profits".

Naturally, they might disguise the correct answer in contrapositive form, and we should wary of illegal reversals / opposites.

## Answer choice explanations

**A — Unrelated to Goal (5% of students)**

This doesn't have any language about "maximizing clients' profits", so it is functionally useless to us.

**B — Illegal Reversal / Opposite (21% of students)**

This says: max out profits → market mainly to [older people]

But our conclusion is saying: market mainly to [older people] → max out profits

Unless = if it is not the case that. So one way to diagram this is: not marketing to population segment that controls most disposable income → won't maximize profits

Our conclusion is the Opposite of that: do market to population segment that controls most disposable income (i.e. older people) → will maximize profits

**C — Unrelated to Goal / Out of Scope = reputation (2% of students)**

This doesn't have any language about "maximizing clients' profits", so it is functionally useless to us. Also, the correct answer to Sufficient Assumption will not bring up any new ideas, such as "enhancing the reputation of products".

**D — Illegal Opposite (17% of students)**

This, like (B), is giving us an opposite/reversed version of the conclusion. It's saying: tailor ads mainly to segment of pop that doesn't control much disposable income (i.e. not older adults) → won't maximize clients' profits

Meanwhile, this argument is saying: tailor ads mainly to segment of pop that does control much disposable income (i.e. older adults) → will maximize clients' profits

**E — Correct (56% of students)**

We know that "older people" are a population segment that controls more than 50% of the nation's personal disposable income.

There is another modern question (a Principle question about completing the space agency's current project, since the money we'd still have to spend on it is more than the money we've already spent) that uses this same mathematical rephrasing: if one chunk is bigger than the rest combined, then that first chunk is more than 1/2.

Since older people control more than everyone else combined, they have more than half the disposable income. Pick any number for older people, make sure it's more than the combined total for non-older people, and you'll see why it's always more than half.

Older people: 10 billion
not older people: 9 billion
nation's total: 19 billion (older people > 1/2)

So since we know that this language of "pop segment that controls 50% or more" applies to "older people", this rule allows us to say that,

If an ad agency gears its ads mainly to [older people], then it will maximize its clients' profits.

Thus, we've derived the conclusion.
