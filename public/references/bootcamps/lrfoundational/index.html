Primary Directive
How to Read an LR Stimulus
The core job: When you read an LR stimulus, do not read passively. Find the conclusion, find the premises, and ask whether the premises really prove the conclusion.
Assessment Metrics
What Logical Reasoning Tests

1. Read Intently: You must pay attention to what each sentence is doing. If you do not really understand what you are reading, you will not answer the question correctly. Do not skim. Do not assume. Do not paraphrase too loosely. Track the role of each statement.


2. Question the Author: Many students read an LR stimulus and think, "That sounds fine." That is the wrong mindset. The author's conclusion is often the weak point. Ask whether the evidence actually proves the claim, whether the author is going too far, and whether there is a gap between the premises and the conclusion.

Structural Triad
The Three Parts of an LR Question
Every Logical Reasoning question has three parts: the stimulus, the question stem, and the answer choices. Of those three, the stimulus matters most. The answer is not sitting in the answer choices. The answer choices test whether you understood the stimulus correctly.
1. Stimulus
The short passage you read first. This is where the reasoning lives. Your job is to figure out what facts or evidence the author is giving you and what claim the author is trying to prove. That means your real task is to separate premises from conclusions.
2. Question Stem
The question stem tells you what job you need to do with the argument: find the flaw, strengthen the argument, weaken the argument, identify the main conclusion, or find an assumption. First understand the argument. Then use the question stem to know what to do with it.
3. Answer Choices
The answer choices do not create the reasoning. They test whether you understood it correctly. Do not start by comparing answer choices before you understand the stimulus. The stimulus comes first.
Execution Example
The Stock Analysts
"Humans are no better than apes at investing. We gave five stock analysts and one chimpanzee $1,350 each to invest. After one month, the chimp won, increasing its net worth by $210. The next best analyst increased by only $140."
Question Stem: Each of the following describes a flaw in the game show host's reasoning EXCEPT:

Simulated Answer Choices:
(A)
It draws a sweeping conclusion about two entire species based on an exceptionally small sample size.
(B)
It takes for granted that a single one-month trial is a reliable indicator of long-term investing skill.
(C)
It fails to consider the possibility that the chimpanzee's success was merely the result of random chance.
(D)
It inappropriately relies on the emotional appeal of an animal succeeding in a human profession. ✓ CORRECT
(E)
It presumes, without providing justification, that the five chosen analysts are representative of human investors generally.
*Because this is an EXCEPT question, (A), (B), (C), and (E) all describe actual logical flaws in the stimulus. (D) is the correct answer because the argument does not rely on an emotional appeal, making it the only option that DOES NOT describe a flaw.
Valid Argument: Premises support conclusions. Conclusions rely on premises.
Invalid Argument (Gap): When evidence is removed or insufficient, the support fails, and the conclusion collapses.
The Building Blocks
Premises: Statements of Fact
Premises are the facts or evidence in the argument. They tell you what the author is giving you to work with. On the LSAT, you accept the premises as true. Do not push back on the premises. Push back on the conclusion.

Key rule: The question is not whether the premises are true. The question is whether the premises actually support the conclusion.
ARE: Facts, Evidence, Support, Building blocks.
ARE NOT: Main judgments, The part you attack, Final claims.
Premise Indicators: FABS
FOR
AFTER ALL
BECAUSE
SINCE
The Main Point
Conclusions: The Author's Claim
Conclusions are the judgments the author makes. They are the main point the author wants you to accept. This is the part of the argument you should examine most carefully.

Key rule: The conclusion is the part of the argument you should be most suspicious of. Ask whether it is too strong, whether it goes beyond the evidence, whether the author assumed something missing, and whether the premises really get us there.
ARE: Author's claim, The point argued for, Part you question.
ARE NOT: Automatically proven, Self-supporting, Always clear.
Conclusion Indicator Words:
THEREFORE
THUS
HENCE
SO
CONSEQUENTLY
AS A RESULT
IT FOLLOWS THAT
THIS SHOWS THAT
Indicator Drill
Indicators in Action
"Monica claimed to have caused the extinction of the dinosaurs, and everything Monica claims is true. Therefore, Monica caused the extinction of the dinosaurs."
Premises: Monica claimed it; everything Monica claims is true.
Conclusion: Monica caused the extinction.
Sequence
How to Read a Stimulus Step by Step
1
Ask whether it is an argument: Not every stimulus is an argument. If there is no claim being supported, it may just be a set of facts. If there is a claim supported by evidence, it is an argument.
2
Find the conclusion: Ask: what is the author trying to prove? That is the conclusion.
3
Find the premises: Ask: what evidence is being used to support that conclusion? Those are the premises.
4
Analyze the gap: Ask: do these premises really justify that conclusion? That is where most LR questions live.
Verification
Main Conclusion: 2-Point Check
Important warning about indicator words: Indicator words are helpful, but they are not enough by themselves. Sometimes they appear. Sometimes they do not. So do not rely on indicator words alone. Instead, ask whether a sentence is giving you evidence or giving you the author's claim.

The 2-Point Check: The main conclusion usually...
1. Sounds like the author's Opinion.
2. Is Supported by at least one other explicit claim.

Not every claim in a stimulus is the main conclusion. Some claims are intermediate conclusions. So ask: which statement sounds most like the author's actual point, and which statement is being supported by other statements?
Contextual Layer
Peripheral Information
When we dissect arguments, we want to focus on the relationship between the conclusion and the premises. But arguments can also include peripheral information. These elements do not form the core structure, but they add context and complexity.
1. Background Info
Sets the stage and provides context but does not directly support the conclusion. Like describing the room before making a recommendation.

Ex: "The average American consumes 222 pounds of meat each year."
2. Opposing Views
Ideas that challenge the main conclusion. Authors often bring them up to show they are considering other perspectives before reinforcing their own point.

Ex: "Many people argue that eating meat is necessary for a balanced diet."
3. Concessions
A concession happens when the author acknowledges a point from the opposing side but then immediately answers it in order to strengthen the original position. Be ready for the author's real point right after it.
"Yes, it is true that a vegetarian diet can lack some nutrients, but supplements can easily make up for it."
Standard of Truth
Valid vs. Invalid
Valid Conclusion = Proven
A valid conclusion is one that must be true if the premises are true. That means there is no gap, no wiggle room, and no missing assumption. If the premises are true, the conclusion has to be true.
Invalid Conclusion = Not Fully Proven
An invalid conclusion is not guaranteed by the premises. The premises may make it sound plausible, tempting, or even likely. But if the conclusion does not have to follow, then it is not valid. That is where flaws, assumptions, weakeners, and strengtheners come in.
Executive Summary
The Student Takeaway
When you read an LR stimulus, always ask three questions:
1. What is the conclusion?

2. What are the premises?

3. Do the premises actually prove the conclusion?

If you get good at those three questions, LR becomes much more manageable.
