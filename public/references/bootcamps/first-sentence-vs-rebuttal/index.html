Argument Basics

Most of the paragraphs we read in Logical Reasoning are labeled arguments or reasoning.

Core Idea

An argument is a claim supported by some other claim(s).

To analyze an argument (i.e. to solve most LR questions), our first step is to find the Conclusion and its Supporting idea(s).

In most cases there is more than one Supporting idea. In a small number of cases, there is more than one Conclusion (a Main Conclusion supported by an Intermediate Conclusion).

⚡ Mini Concept Check
Identify the Conclusion: "It is raining heavily, so we should cancel the picnic."
A) It is raining heavily.
B) We should cancel the picnic.
Core Strategy

The 2-Step Process

Main Conclusion questions are less common, but they are our most straightforward task. Because the task is simple, they complicate it by "hiding" the conclusion.

The 2-Step Check

Step 1: Find the author’s opinion, not merely a factual statement.
Step 2: Check whether that opinion is explicitly supported by at least one other statement.
💡

Coach's Tip: Upside Down Arguments
They very rarely put the conclusion as the final claim. Instead, we usually hear the Conclusion before we hear its Support. If the first sentence sounds like an Author Opinion, verify it gets supported.

⚡ Pop Quiz
Which of the following sounds like an Author's Opinion (Conclusion) rather than a mere fact?
A) The city council voted 5-4 to approve the new zoning laws.
B) The city council made a mistake by approving the new zoning laws.
Structure

Statement Role Framework

For Role questions, you need to identify what role a specific claim played in the argument. We use a four-bucket system.

Main Conclusion

The claim the author is ultimately trying to prove. A generalization supported by evidence.

Support

A claim used in favor of the conclusion. Includes Premises (direct support) and Intermediate Conclusions (supported by other statements, but used to support the main conclusion).

Opposing Viewpoint

A claim the author is responding to, pushing back against (rebuttal target), or conceding before replying.

Neutral Info

Background information, context, or setup that frames the argument without functioning as the conclusion, support, or opposing view.

⚡ Quick Check
Is the word "because" typically a Premise indicator or a Conclusion indicator?
A) Premise Indicator
B) Conclusion Indicator
Translation Skill

Referential Language in Conclusions

Many conclusions act as rebuttals or summaries that rely on referential shorthand words like this, that, they, such, or it.

The Translation Rule

Never leave a conclusion in vague shorthand. Always trace the referential word back to its exact target, and replace the pronoun with the specific noun.

Case Study
"Some people propose laws that would require mandatory background checks. However, such laws won't affect the homicide rate."
1
Identify the shorthand The author uses the phrase "such laws" in the conclusion.
2
Locate the exact target The previous sentence defines them as "laws that would require mandatory background checks."
3
Restate explicitly "Laws requiring mandatory background checks won't affect the homicide rate."

When dissecting arguments in the drills ahead, your job is to translate every vague conclusion into a precise, standalone claim.
