### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

This set has 7 **Principle-Strengthen** questions from tests 50-54, levels 3 - 5

All of these examples are the much-more common style of Principle-Strengthen in which the _answers_ are principles. They often feel more like **Sufficient Assumption** than Strengthen, because the correct answer is usually a conditional rule that is triggered by the Evidence and results in the Conclusion.

But the question stem is technically a Strengthen task (which answer _most justifies_), so we're not supposed to ask ourselves, "Does this answer prove the conclusion", even though in many cases it will. In some rare cases, though, the correct answer could be as weakly worded as a Necessary Assumption. Several questions in this set have correct answers that will feel like necessary assumptions.

Nevertheless, our primary approach to these is to expect a conditional logic answer, so we pre-phrase by articulating an "IF premise, THEN conclusion" reasoning move.

Most of the answers will be conditionals. If so, we want to be very **stubborn with the outcome**. The right side of the rule should match the conclusion (or at least sound like wording that would strongly support the conclusion). We want to be **flexible with the trigger**.

Other answers feel more like comparative ideas that help _Weigh Tradeoffs_ or establish priorities. When arguments seem to be balancing competing ideas (a pro and a con / this obligation with that obligation), then when the author issues her judgment in the conclusion, she's revealing whether she cares more about the good thing or the bad thing, whether she cares more about this obligation or that obligation.

Given this argument, we'd anticipate a conditional answer  
_Billy lied to our customers. Thus he shouldn't be on our staff anymore._

(A) A person can be on the staff only if they are consistently truthful with customers

Given this argument, we might see a weighing-tradeoffs answer  
_Billy is extremely dependable, although she lied to our customers. Clearly, she shouldn't be on our staff._

(A) Dependability is a less important trait in a staff member than is honesty.

#### Right Column

# Try these 7 problems, untimed

- Once you find the Conclusion and identify the main Supporting Idea(s), you will generally want to articulate an, "If [premise], then [conclusion]" linking idea.

- If, otherwise, you felt like the argument was grappling with competing interests, then articulate how the author is weighing tradeoffs: "she cares more about X than about Y".

- If the author arguing that "because of a dissimilarity two things should be treated dissimilarly", or "because of a similarity two things should be treated similarly", then the principle should connect the (dis)similarity in the evidence to (dis)similar treatment in the conclusion.

- Try to get rid of a lot answers quickly because they don't have any good matching language for the conclusion, or because the matching language for the conclusion is being put on the **left** of the conditional arrow.

- Correct answers will often, if not usually, be written in contrapositive form, so be willing to try that move. If you read "should be on staff only if consistently truthful", then you have to make your brain say, "Okay, this is saying _if you're not consistently truthful, should not on staff_".

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Back again for more fun with **Explain a Curious Fact**, in which the evidence will provide some _Curious Fact_ (almost always an asymmetric comparison).

The author is implicitly asking herself, "Wussup with this Curious Fact?", and her conclusion either states or implies some causal explanation.

We evaluate these arguments by thinking about:

- **Alternate Explanations** for this curious fact
- the **plausibility** of the Author's Explanation

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 problems, in 13 minutes

**(1) Locate the Curious Fact in the evidence, and _ask_ the curious question.**

**(2) Spell out the author's explanation for the curious fact, because the author is often conveying a lot of implicit ideas within their conclusion:**

**(3) Think about _Alternate Explanations_ for the Curious Fact.**  
Consider _Reverse Causality, Third Factor, Awareness vs. Incidence, Return to Normalcy_

**(4) Think about what could help us assess the _Plausibility_ of the Author's Explanation.**  
Consider _Covariation_ (No Cause, No Effect / Effect without Cause / Cause without Effect), or _Presence / Motive / Method_ ideas (suspect was at the scene of the crime / suspect had incentive to do it / here's how the suspect could have done it).

**Flaw, Weaken, Evaluate**, more likely to be supplying an Alternate Explanation.  
**Sufficient Assumption**, more likely to rule out any hope of Alternate Explanation.  
**Strengthen, Necessary Assumption**, more likely to be addressing Plausibility of the Author's Explanation.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

# The three main Reasoning Structures

**Conditional Logic** - Correct answers address the author's internal logic.

- assumed links between ideas
- if/then rules
- a missing fact needed to trigger a rule
- valid and invalid conditional inferences

**Comparisons** - Correct answers address assumed similarities or differences.

- part vs. whole
- sample vs. general population
- then vs. now
- this situation vs. that situation
- this group vs. that group

Or they address the net gain / loss of a comparison:

- doing this thing vs. not doing this thing
- this thing vs. that thing
- weighing the positives vs. the negatives

**Causation** - Correct answers address one of these two pressure points.

- alternate explanations for some Curious Comparison
- the plausibility of the author's explanation for it.

#### Right Column

# Try these 7 questions, untimed.

When we're using **Conditional Logic** to "prove an argument", the **Left** side should be triggered by facts we know from the Evidence (or Answer Choice), and the **Right** side of the rule should match the Conclusion's language. If we want to weaken an argument's internal logic, then we're showing how the Left side is compatible with the **opposite** of the Right side (the Premise is compatible with the Anti-Conclusion).

If it feels like you're dealing with a **Comparison**, think about weakening with potential differences or strengthening by affirming some similarity. If you're dealing with a Net Gain / Loss type of Comparison, think about weakening with potential downsides or strengthening with "equal or better" ideas.

If the author is interpreting the **Causal** significance of some Curious Comparison (a weird asymmetry exists, or a study that has some interesting data), take time to understand the Author's Explanation, but then consider Alternate Explanations for the same curious fact (is the author confusing cause and effect? is the author failing to recognize that two things are linked to some third factor?)

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**LENGTH** -- 15 Q's, 15 minutes (1.5x = 22, 2x = 30)  
**TYPES** -- Paradox, Flaw, Role, Main Conclusion, Necessary Assumption, Sufficient Assumption, Strengthen, Weaken, Method, and Must Be True.  
**LEVEL** -- 1

Wait a sec -- these are all level 1? I thought this activity was listed as level 5?

Yes, because the goal is something a level 5 student needs to work on: be able to quickly take care of easier questions, so that you'll have more time and brainpower in reserves for the harder, more flexible thinking questions in the section.

Given how many subtle reading traps there are on LSAT, it's sometimes difficult to ever let yourself believe that an answer you find appealing is correct. "It's never this easy", we might think. But, sure it is! Sometimes it really is that easy. We need to improve our ability to _let the easy be easy._

And we need to develop enough self-trust while we're doing easier problems that we can pick up our pace when possible.

#### Right Column

Try to get through these 15 questions in 15 mins. Keep yourself going at a brisk pace. When you have a strong idea of what you're looking for (like on Main Conclusion, Role, Sufficient Assumption, Flaw), try to be proactive about scanning for and finding your answer, rather than reading all five choices start to finish.

However, on tasks where we compare the impact of new information (like Paradox, Strengthen, Weaken), we may still need to use more time to consider all five answers.

If your thinking feels unfinished on any of them, flag them as you go (so that you can maintain your brisk pace), and then take a second look at them after the timer goes off before you check your answer / the explanation.
