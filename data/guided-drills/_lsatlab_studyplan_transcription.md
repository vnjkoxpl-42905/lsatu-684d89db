## Abstract + Flaw (set 1)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 35 mins | Weeks: Week 2

### Left Column

Sometimes **Flaw** answer choices sound like specific objections to the argument. Other times, they are phrased with very nonspecific, abstract vocabulary.

When abstract language appears within Flaw answers, **many** , if not most, of them allude to the same 10-15 famous flaws.

The other ones feel more like answer choices on Method questions. We need to pay attention to language indicators in each answer choice signifying which half of the answer is meant to describe the Conclusion and which half is meant to describe the Evidence.

Once we've determined that, we can start asking ourselves questions like, "Can I match up this (abstract answer choice) wording with the Conclusion?" If not, eliminate. If so, then we would try to match up the other half, making sure that we're consistent in terms of matching the same nouns/traits to the same abstract terms in each answer choice.

### Right Column

Try ten level **Flaw** problems, whose answer choices will feature some challenging abstract descriptions.

Do them untimed, as this activity is mainly about increasing our understanding of the abstract terminology in the answers.

In a timed setting, you would just find the answer you like the most. But your job is to think about the wrong answers, too. What are _they_ describing? If it's a famous flaw, name it. If not (or if so), try to think of your own sample argument that would be committing that flaw.

In order to judge abstract language quickly, it's powerful to have your own mental example of what that move _would_ sound like if it really _were_ happening in the stimulus.

## Analogies (set 1)Patterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 20 mins | Weeks: Week 5

### Left Column

**Analogies** are two _specific_ cases that share some _general_ trait or relationship.

**TRY THIS**
"Kittens are to cats, what puppies are to ______"

You know the answer is _dogs_ because puppies and dogs have the same general relationship that kittens and cats have (youth vs. adult). They are analogous on at least one level.

Because we, as law students, will be tasked with reading judges' opinions and digesting their full meaning, LSAC wants to test us on how well we pick up on implied ideas. We most commonly talk about implied ideas with the word Assumption (unstated, but implied ideas in an argument). But we can also talk about implied ideas in the sense of Inference (Must Be True / Most Supported), because inferences are _implications_ / _consequences / conclusions_ that can be drawn from the information.

And we can talk about implied ideas when it comes to **Completing Analogies** **.** The structural signpost of an analogy instructs the reader to **carry through** a trait or relationship that was described in the 1st case over to the 2nd case.

### Right Column

## **Try these 5 questions untimed** ,
thinking through the analogies.
- What are the two separate cases?

- What is the common trait?

- What language cues exist to telegraph that the author is making a Comparison / Analogy?

- Do the two cases seem "fair to compare" or do you think there could be an important difference between the two cases that calls into question the implied similarity?

**EXAMPLE**
An NFL quarterback is the leader of his team, responsible for organizing and inspiring his teammates, all within a very high pressured environment. Similarly, the President is the leader of the Executive Branch and all its staff, and so she too ________.

The test would be expecting us to **carry through** the traits we heard about quarterbacks.

_so she too ...._
(A) must provide oversight to her staff and motivate them, while managing very stressful situations.

## Answer a Question (passage pair)Patterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 20 mins | Weeks: Week 3

### Left Column

These two passages from test 3 and 7 both lend themselves to an _Answer a Question_ framework.

Sometimes in such passages, the author poses an actual question. The unusual sight of a question mark in an RC passage is a big wake up call that allows us to think, "Oh, snap! If the author is asking a question, then trying to answer that question is probably her main purpose."

Other times, it's a little more subtle. We pick up on clues like
- scientists are perplexed
- what's puzzling / what's surprising
- still don't have a satisfactory explanation for

There we should exert a little more effort into pausing and making our brain explicitly name what question we're trying to answer.

We should then _Read with an Appetite_ for that answer. It's not always obvious when it occurs, but if we're listening for it, we should see it.

### Right Column

## Try these two passages, **untimed**
working on reading through the _Answer Question_ framework

When you encounter the spot where the passage seems to be setting up a question, pause and say the question back to your brain.

Continue to read the passage yearning for an answer to that question.

When you're done reading the passage, you should be able to articulate the Question and the Answer.

We've removed a couple questions from each set that test minor details, so the questions that remain are mainly focused on our understanding of the Question and the Answer.

## Answer a Question + Must Be True (set 1)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 10 mins | Weeks: Week 14

### Left Column

This set contains two _Answer a Question_ passages from test 69, as well as three questions with a **Must Be True** degree.

Usually, Must Be True is the exclusive province of **Locate Detail** and **Five Questions** . It's signified by keywords like _according to the passage, the passage indicates, the author states / says / mentions_

But occasionally we see an **Inference** question have a Must Be True degree. Instead of asking what is "most supported / suggested / most reasonably inferred", it asks w_hat can be inferred_ from the passage. This doesn't seem to really make a difference in our approach, but our correct answer should feel like something can pretty much prove using ideas from the passage, even though it won't sound like a reiteration of what was said in the passage.

### Right Column

## Attempt the passages and questions **untimed** .

Read the entire passage if you want to reinforce the _Answer a Question_ framework, or just go immediately to whichever part of the passage the question stem keywords are referring you to.

Practice the skill of finding keywords in the question stem, or in an answer choice, and doing a quick visual scan or Ctrl / Command + F search in order to locate where those words appear in the passage.

## Answer a Question + What's Implied (set 1)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 13 mins | Weeks: Week 3

### Left Column

This set contains two _Answer a Question_ passages from tests 61 and 69, as well as six accompanying **What's Implied** question tasks.

These tend to be harder, because the correct answer is usually a gisty re-wording or a technical implication of one or more sentences from the passage.

Most Supported means that question stem is expressed in "squishy" terms, indicating that our answer might not be fully derivable from the passage, but it's more derivable than any other answer:
- most supported
- most reasonably inferred
- most likely to agree
- suggests

While we typically only think of _Implied_ question types (i.e. Inference, Opinion, Attitude) as having a most supported vibe, sometimes we see a Five Questions with a Most Supported degree, even though Five Questions is within the _Stated_ family of question types.

### Right Column

## Attempt the passages and questions **untimed** .

You've read them before, so you might be able to just dive in where needed, but since Most Supported questions often pull support from unexpected sentences or feel like a holistic implication of someone's view, it's safer to have just read the whole passage.

- When looking for an answer, be very cautious about overly Strong / Specific / Comparative wording in the answer choices.

- Gravitate towards stuff that sounds more weakly worded, where the burden of proof is one example, or where you feel like you don't need support beyond the passage to verify an answer is true.

- Don't settle on an answer until you've matched it up with some Support Sentence(s) in the passage that you think you could use, if you had to defend your answer in court.

## Anti-Causal - Set 1Patterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 20 mins | Weeks: Week 5

### Left Column

**Anti-Causal** **** arguments attempt to argue that "X makes no difference / X doesn't help."

The evidence is that things seem to be the same, or worse, without X, or that X wouldn't seem to have anything to do with what really matters.

**EXAMPLES**
Some people add a mindfulness regimen to their LSAT studying. But since mindfulness is primarily used to reduce anxiety and Ann suffered from no anxiety, the mindfulness regimen probably wouldn't do anything for Ann.

The companies that are guilty of the most egregious environmental violations are also the companies that are most scrutinized by environmental regulators. This just goes to show the futility of trying to stop companies from damaging the environment with regulatory oversight.

**OBJECTION - would have been even worse without**
if the environmental regulators _weren't_ on top of these offending companies, the companies' behavior would be even worse. The fact that these companies have the most oversight doesn't mean that oversight does nothing to help; we will always direct more of our Solution Efforts to the Biggest Source of a problem. That will always leave someone able to disingenuously argue, "See? The solution efforts don't work. In the places where we have most solution efforts, we have biggest incidence of problem."

**OBJECTION - overlooked consideration**
even though it looks like mindfulness wouldn't help Ann because she doesn't suffer from anxiety, a mindfulness regimen may still help her, since regular mindfulness breathing changes the gray matter in our brains in ways that boost retention while learning --- _this is true_ _, by the way_

### Right Column

## **Try these five questions, untimed** .

Think about what wording in the Conclusion makes it feel like the author is _denying_ a causal connection between two things.

Think about possible objections just as
- would have been even worse without
- biggest source of problem usually gets most solution-effort
- different initial reference points (not fair to compare)
- overlooked consideration that shows how there _could_ be a causal relationship

### Right Column

## Try these 8 anti-causal questions, untimed.
Keep an eye out for some of these common anti-causal objections:

**Would have been even worse without**
If the environmental regulators _weren't_ on top of these offending companies, the companies' behavior would be even worse. If it weren't for that stimulus, the unemployment rate would be 12%.

**More frequent source of problem gets more attention**
The fact that these bad-acting companies have the most oversight doesn't mean that oversight does nothing to help; we will always direct more of our Solution Efforts to the Biggest Source of a problem. That will always leave someone able to disingenuously argue, "See? The solution efforts don't work. In the places where we have most solution efforts, we have biggest incidence of problem."

The mindfulness _did_ help Ann, because she went from a 140 to a 160, where as Jerry just went from a 150 to 160, or because she tends to have test anxiety that lowers her score on test day whereas he doesn't.

Even though Alicia's best friend Trixie is running against Lucas, Alicia doesn't want Trixie to win and thus be less available for chillaxing, so Alicia has motive to want Trixie to lose.

Note: when a conclusion is saying "X wasn't caused by Y, it was caused by Z", there are two equally valid ways to affect that conclusion:
- information helping us to assess whether it _wasn't_ Y
- information helping us to assess whether it _was_ Z

## Anti-Causal - Set 3Process

- Type: Process | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 17

### Left Column

In many **Anti-Causal** **** arguments, the author doesn't believe that "X causes / explains Y", because the author's premise supposedly makes that causal relationship seem implausible or wrong.

_for example --_ Timmy says that his love of sushi was caused by his upbringing. However, this must be false, since he did not grow up in a Japanese household.

In objecting to these arguments, we're essentially trying to restore the idea that, "yes, X _does_ cause Y" (Timmy's upbringing _is_ what explains his love of sushi) while reconciling that with the author's concern.

Even though your premise is true,
it still _is_ the case that X caused Y,
because ... _?_

Even though Timmy didn't grow up in a Japanese household,
it still _is_ the case that his upbringing caused his love of sushi,
because ... _?_

### Right Column

## **Try these 6 problems untimed.**
## Focus on finding the anti-causal features.

The authors will be thinking, "How can this Cause/Effect relationship be true if fact X seems to go against it?"  (fact X will normally feel like something showing Cause w/o Effect or Effect w/o Cause)

So our job in thinking about these problems is to figure out we can reconcile fact X with the Cause/Effect relationship.

Set up your thinking this way to respond to their evidence:

- A _does_ cause B (or A _did_ cause B)
- X is just true because ...  ?

Correct weakening ideas on Anti-Causal arguments will usually feel like they're "**making an excuse** " for the premise, helping us to see it in a new light that allows us to still believe in the Cause/Effect relationship being disputed.

### Left Column

**Anti-Causal** **** arguments attempt to argue that "X makes no difference", "X didn't help", or "it's not resulting from X, but Y" . Their evidence is usually that things seem to be the same, or worse, without X.

the stimulus bill _did_ help, because the economy was in such a free-fall that if it hadn't been for the stimulus bill, the unemployment would have been at like 12%

- **overlooked consideration**
even though it looks like the stimulus bill did nothing because unemployment continued to rise in its immediate aftermath, that's because it takes several months before stimulus can manifest any changes in employment figures.

Try these three questions that have an Anti-Causal vibe to them.

The conclusion will sound like it's saying,
_"So, X doesn't cause Y"_

If we're objecting to these arguments, we need to find a way to accept the evidence but still find a way to argue that X _could_ cause Y.

## Assumption Family (Easier) Set 1Process

- Type: Process | Time: 35 mins | Weeks: Week 2

### Left Column

In the **Assumption Family** ,
Step 1, find the author's Conclusion and Supporting Idea(s).
Step 2, depends ... which type of question are you doing?

**Sufficient / Necessary Assumption** **,** or**** **Principle-Strengthen** ?****
Start by thinking about the **Internal Logic** of the argument.
- "If Premise, Then Conclusion" linking ideas.
- If there's a _New Concept in Conclusion_ , make the author define that term. What idea from the evidence is she connecting it to?
- Is there an _Untriggered Conditional_ idea in the evidence?
- Do two premises have an overlapping concept? Can we synthesize them?
- "in-the-box" correct answers, re-using language from argument

**Strengthen, Weaken, Flaw** or**Evaluate** ?
Be the Opposing Lawyer, and engage in **Courtroom Debate.**
- Look for hints at Comparative or Causal reasoning.
- If the author is arguing by Comparison, we have to try to weaken the comparison by making two things seem unfair to compare, or by adding a new consideration to the pros and cons balance sheet.
- If the author is presenting a Curious Fact and then concluding something that implies a causal explanation, think about Alternate Explanations, or ways to better assess the Author's Explanation.
- Is the author engaging in any illegal argument moves (famous flaws)?

Remember, these are just **tendencies** . This is like reading the scouting report on an athlete, and knowing they _prefer_ to go to their right. We hedge that way, but we're still willing to use either style of thinking on any question type.

### Right Column

## **Try these 9 questions, untimed**
Think about how our thinking shifts from
_Internal Logic_ to _Courtroom Debate_ , based on the question type.

When you're trying **Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption,** or**Principle- Strengthen** questions, think to yourself that when you combine the correct answer with the existing premises you can derive, or come much closer to deriving, the conclusion.

If the conclusion is trying to prove that "A is Z", then we need two basic things:
- a rule that has an outcome of Z, like "If X, then Z".
- facts telling us that A triggers this rule, like "A is X ".

When you're trying **Strengthen, Weaken,** or**Evaluate** , try to respond to the argument as though you're Opposing Counsel. Even though you accept that the Prosecution's evidence is true, how could you make it seem irrelevant, less trustworthy, or less important than some other consideration?

Think about the argument in one (or more) of these ways.
- **Given** [this evidence], **How can I still argue** [the anti-conclusion] ?
- **Even though** [evidence] is true, [conclusion] **is wrong because ...**
- **Given that I want to argue** [anti-conclusion], **how can I respond to** [author's evidence / logic]?

When you're doing **Flaw,** add onto that list, "Is this author committing any of the **famous flaws** ?"

## Assumption Family (Easier) Set 2Process

- Type: Process | Time: 25 mins | Weeks: Week 3

### Left Column

## The three main **Reasoning Types**

## **Conditional Logic**
Correct answers address the **internal logic:**
- assumed links between ideas
- if/then rules
- a missing fact needed to trigger a rule
- valid and invalid conditional inferences

## **Comparisons**
Correct answers address assumed similarities or differences between two things:
- part vs. whole
- sample vs. general population
- then vs. now
- this situation vs. that situation
- this group vs. that group

Or they address the net gain / loss of a comparison:
- doing this thing vs. not doing this thing
- this thing vs. that thing

## **Causation**
Correct answers relate to one of these two pressure points:
- alternate explanations for the curious background fact
- the plausibility of the author's explanation for it

### Right Column

## Try these 6 questions, untimed.

If it feels like you're dealing with Rules, or **if / then logic** ,
Focus on understanding the If / Then rule.
What idea goes on the **Left** of the conditional arrow?
What idea goes on the **Right** ?
When we're using **Conditional Logic** to "prove an argument", the **Left** side should be triggered by facts we know from the Evidence (or Answer Choice), and the **Right** side of the rule should match the Conclusion's language.
If it feels like you're dealing with a **Comparison** ,
Think about weakening with potential differences or strengthening by affirming some similarity.
If you're dealing with a Net Gain / Loss type of Comparison, think about weakening  with potential downsides or strengthening with "equal or better" ideas.
If the author is interpreting the **Causal** significance of some Curious Fact (a weird asymmetry exists, or a study that has some interesting data),
Take time to understand the Author's Explanation.
Think about what kind evidence would help make that Explanation seem more/less plausible.
Be sure to consider Alternate Explanations for that same Curious Fact.

### Right Column

## Try these four questions **untimed** .

Local Purpose and Meaning in Context usually highlight a specific sentence for us, but the correct answer is really reinforcing one of the adjacent "bookend" sentences.

Trap answers will be about A and B.

## Causal Chains - Set 2Process

- Type: Process | Time: 35 mins | Weeks: Week 8

### Left Column

## **Causal Chains**
popular on **Most Supported, Must Be True,** and**Necessary Assumption**

A causal chain is three or more things causally linked:
_Reading gives Jorge a headache, which makes him take a nap._
The test wants to make sure we see the implicit connection that
"Reading leads to taking a nap".
We might note causal chains on our paper like
__ **_Reading ⇢ Headache ⇢ Take Nap_**

This isn't the same as conditional logic, so we need to understand that these are "causal" arrows we're showing ourselves. With both causal chains and conditional chains, it's illegal to go backwards.

You wouldn't say that "if **Nap** , then **Headache** , then **Reading** "

But here's the big difference: with causal chains it's plausible to say "if the first thing doesn't happen, the last thing won't happen".

On a Most Supported task, we can feel pretty safe about ideas like
(A) If Jorge did less reading during the day, he would take fewer naps.

**VOCAB**
given a causal chain, like **X ⇢ Y ⇢ Z**
we can say that
X **directly** causes Y.
X **indirectly** causes Z.

### Right Column

## **Try these 9 questions untimed.**
Work on building these causal chains,
before you look at answers.

Causal chains within **Necessary Assumption** can be hard to understand because you're missing parts of the chain (the author is assuming them).

EXAMPLE    _B causes C.  So, I guess A causes D._
That's very confusing, because there are no repeating ideas!
EXAMPLE  _Waking up earlier makes people be more productive at work in the morning. So, I guess getting a new dog might lead to a promotion._
Be patient trying to find the right causal sequence. Put **any** cause / effect relationship you can find on the page, with space on your page to both the left and the right of that idea.

earlier wake  ⇢ more prod morn

Now, go seek out something that has to do with causing someone to "wake up earlier", or find something that could be caused by "having a more productive morning".
In the author's mind, we have this causal sequence

_get dog_ **_⇢_** _wake up earlier_ **_⇢_** _more productive morn_ **_⇢_** _promotion_
__ **_ASSUMED_** __ **_PREMISE_** __ **_ASSUMED_**
The middle idea was stated. The other two are assumed. If you go from beginning of the chain to the end, you get the author's conclusion, "get dog ⇢ promotion".
Remember, on **Most Supported** , if you know that A leads to B leads to C, it's pretty fair to say "if you cut back on A, you'll get less C".

## Causal Chains - Set 3Patterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 16

### Left Column

## **Causal Chains** **** show up mostly in
## **Assumption** and **Inference** questions

given this:
_Reading for a long time gives Jorge a headache._
_Headaches make Jorge lie down._

we might notate it like this:
**_Read long time ⇢ Headache ⇢ Lie Down_**

As with conditional logic, we're not allowed to say, "I just saw Jorge laying down. He must have been reading a bunch earlier today". That's inferring that one possible cause of Jorge's laying down is the only one we need to consider.
But we can say stuff like, "This afternoon, Jorge needs to be alert and active. So he shouldn't read a long time after lunch." This is sort of like a contrapositive: _if you don't want the Effect, don't do that Cause_
An argument can use that structure to create flaws or gaps:
_Reading for a long time gives Jorge a headache. Headaches make Jorge lie down. Thus if Jorge needs to stay alert and active all day, he should not read anything._
Whoa, whoa, there author! I understand you're saying, "if he wants to avoid the Effect of laying down, he better not do the Cause (i.e. reading for a long time)". But you said, "he better not read _anything_ ".

_Reading for a long time gives Jorge a headache. Headaches make Jorge lie down. Thus if Jorge needs to stay alert and active all day, he should not Pam's essay._
Whoa, whoa, there author! I understand you're saying, "if he wants to avoid the Effect of laying down, he better not do the Cause (i.e. reading for a long time)". But who said that reading Pam's essay means that he'd be reading for a long time? You must be assuming that, "It would take a long time to read Pam's essay".

### Right Column

## **Try these 8 questions untimed.**
## Work on understanding the causal chain,
## before you look at answers

On Assumption questions, causal chain arguments might create a **missing link** .

EXAMPLE: _Stress causes Anton to sweat  So, Anton probably gets sweaty when he plays chess._

This argument is assuming a missing link between playing chess and being stressed.

**_CONCLUSION_** _play chess   ---------------->   sweat_

**_SUPPORT_** _play chess_ **_⇢_** _stress_ **_⇢_** _sweat_
__ **_ASSUMED_** __ **_PREMISE_** __

Causal chain arguments might create a **missing fact** .

EXAMPLE: _Playing chess gives Anton stress, and he tends to get sweaty when he's stressed. Thus, he will probably be sweating tonight._

This argument is assuming that Anton will be playing chess tonight. It set up a causal chain and seems to be expecting the effect we get at the end of the chain, but it never triggered the causal chain.
When you're trying to organize a causal chain on your paper, be patient trying to find the right causal sequence. Put **any** cause / effect relationship you can find on the page, with space on your page to both the left and the right of that idea. Then, go seek out something that has to do with causing the left side, or something that could be caused by the right side.
Remember, on **Most Supported** , if you know that A leads to B, which leads to C, it's pretty fair to say "if you cut back on A, you'll get less C".

Yes, basically! It feels _naughty_ because that would be an illegal negation in the world of conditional logic, but in the world of Causality, you'll find LSAT being cool with the common sense plausibility of "if cause didn't happen, effect wouldn't have happened".

If I say, "**Because** I took Franklin St to the party and there ended up being an accident there, I was late to the party, which **led** to my missing the opening of gifts." Then it's plausible to say, "if had **not taken** Franklin St., I probably would **not** have missed the opening of gifts".

## Complete the Analogy (set 1)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 20 mins | Weeks: Week 7

### Left Column

**Analogies** are two _specific_ cases that share some _general_ trait or relationship.

**TRY THIS** :
"Kittens are to cats, what puppies are to ______"

You know the answer is _dogs_ because puppies and dogs have the same general relationship (adult vs. youth) that kittens and cats have. They are analogous on at least one level.

Because we, as law students, will be tasked with reading judges' opinions and digesting their full meaning, LSAC wants to test us on how well we pick up on implied ideas. We most commonly talk about implied ideas with the word Assumption (unstated, but implied ideas in an argument). But we can also talk about implied ideas in the sense of Inference (Must Be True / Most Supported), because inferences are _implications_ / _consequences / conclusions_ that can be drawn from the information. ## Complete the Analogy (set 2)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 7

### Left Column

**Analogies** are two separate cases that share some trait or relationship.

**Fill in the blank**
_"Baseball is to the World Series,_
_what (American) football is to ______"_

The answer is _Superbowl_ because World Series has the same relationship to baseball (season-ending championship) as Superbowl has to football. They're at least partly analogous.
Arguments by analogy tend to think, "Because X and Y are similar when it comes to Thing A, they're probably similar when it comes to Thing B".
_Thelma and Rodney both like rap from the 90s. I just made this Spotify playlist of 90s rap. Thelma really likes it. Thus, Rodney will really like it too._
The author has established one similarity "both like 90s rap" and is assuming that Thelma and Rodney will also be similar in terms of a second trait, "liking my Spotify playlist".
Necessary Assumption / Strengthen / Principle / Most Supported questions will want us to **Complete the Analogy** **.**

Flaw and Weaken will want us to challenge this analogy by pointing to a potentially **crucial difference** (like maybe Thelma only likes east coast rap, Rodney's only likes west coast, and my playlist totally leaves out west coast rap like Dre, Snoop, Pac, NWA, etc.)

## Complete the Analogy (set 3)Patterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 14

### Left Column

**Analogies** are two separate cases that share some trait or relationship.
**f.e. -** _"a peak is to a mountain, what a scalp is to a _____."_

One answer would be _a human_ , peak is the top of a mountain and scalp is the top of a human. These two separate cases share some relationship, so they are analogous in at least one respect.

Analogies show up a lot on **Method** of reasoning questions, which ask us to characterize the type of evidence or type of move the author used.

When we see a **fill-in-the-blank** question (aka Logical Completion), the paragraph is leading us to some final idea, and they frequently use a **Complete the Analogy** type structure, as I did at the top of this page.

When we see analogies show up in the **Assumption Family** , they tend to go like this: "Because X and Y are similar when it comes to Thing A, they're probably also similar when it comes to Thing B".

_Five years ago, my family went to Lake Crocus and I didn't have any fun. Thus, I will not have any fun when my family goes to visit Lake Crocus this summer._

The author has established one similarity "both vacations are at the same location" and is assuming a second similarity, "both vacations will be not-fun".

Flaw and Weaken will want us to challenge this analogy by pointing to a potentially **meaningful difference.** Necessary Assumption will _rule out_ salient differences, and Strengthen will add more similarity, make them seem more Fair to Compare.

The first sentence is a correlation, a Curious Comparison (ppl who are X are more likely than those who aren't to be Y). The author is _asking_ herself:  _Hmm -_ **_why is is that_** _there's this correlation between marathon running and excellent health_ ?

**(2) Spell out the author's explanation for the curious fact, because the author is often conveying a lot of implicit ideas within their conclusion:**

_People who run marathons have good mental discipline. This mental discipline helps them not over-eat, not over-indulge in alcohol or smoking. By not doing those things, they end up in excellent health._

**(3) Think about _Alternate Explanations_ for the Curious Fact.**

Consider _Reverse Causality_ (maybe being in excellent health lets you run a marathon) or _Third Factor_ (maybe being wealthy both enables you to have time to practice/run a marathon and also enables you to get better nutrition, exercise, and medical care)

**(4) Think about what could help us assess the _Plausibility_ of the Author's Explanation.**

Covariation answers (No Cause, No Effect / Effect without Cause / Cause without Effect),  or Presence / Motive / Method ideas (Suspect was at the scene of the crime / suspect had incentive to do it / here's how the suspect could have done it).

## First Sentence ConclusionsPatterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 15 mins | Weeks: Week 2

### Left Column

These six questions all have a **Conclusion** in the **first sentence** **,** which of course is not the most obvious place to find a conclusion.

But **that's the point** !

When we're doing Main Conclusion and Role questions, the test writers are intentionally trying to hide the conclusion, so that this otherwise-easy task can still be challenging.

The most common place to find the conclusion is after a Pivot, expressing some Rebuttal sentiment (65% of Main Conclusion questions).

The second most common place they'll stash it is in the first sentence (25% of Main Conclusion questions).

Only about 10% of the time on Main Conclusion does the paragraph end with the conclusion. That's why picking an answer that matches the final claim will usually be wrong (but definitely not always!)

We want to practice reading Main Conclusion questions with a special ear for "upside-down" arguments, where we hear the conclusion and _then_ hear the supporting ideas.

### Right Column

## **Do these six questions,**
thinking about how we would have figured out that
the first sentence was the conclusion.

This exercise is about getting our brains used to the feel of a pattern. Focus on noticing how the first claim is an **opinion** , and then **feeling** how the subsequent claims go on to defend that initial opinion.

Are there any **Support Indicator** words that follow it?
(f.e. **FABS** = for, after all, because, since)

Are there any **Opinion Indicator** words in it?
(f.e. should, ought, probably, better)

If we look at the claim in the first sentence and ask ourselves, "**Why should we believe _that_** ?", does the rest of an argument seem like an answer to that question? (It should)

### Left Column

We often speak of **Highlight Noteworthy** as the "worst framework / our last resort", because it's the least helpful when it comes to telling our brain what 2 or 3 big ideas we need to come away from the passage with.

When we see Highlight Noteworthy passages from _Society_ , we are often chronicling an event or a time period or a social group, and the main point of the passage revolves around how that group/event/period differs from what came before or how it impacted what came later.

## Intermediate Conclusion - (Set 1)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 25 mins | Weeks: Week 10

### Left Column

Try these five questions that test us on the presence (or absence) of _Intermediate Conclusions_ .

**Role** and **Main Conclusion** questions usually "hide" the conclusion by making it the **very first claim** , or by making it some **rebuttal claim** .

They will also sometimes complicate our assessment of the argument's structure by throwing in multiples conclusions.

Arguments need a claim we call the Conclusion, and some Support for that claim, which we call the evidence or the premise(s).

Some of those supporting premises can _themselves_ also be considered conclusions, if _they_ are supported by at least one claim. The more common name for a _supported premise_ is an Intermediate Conclusion, or a Subsidiary Conclusion.

Any time you're asking yourself, "_Is_ this claim a Conclusion?", you should ask yourself "_why should I believe this claim? did the author provide a supporting reason?_ " If it's really a Conclusion, then you'll be able to point to at least one claim that sounds like a reason offered for why we should believe that claim.

If a claim **has** support, we can call it a Conclusion.
If it **provides** support, we can call it a Premise.
If it **has** support and also **provides** support, an Intermediate Conclusion.

In the Assumption / Objection family, when an argument has this three part structure,
_Premise → Intermediate Conclusion → Main Conclusion_
then the correct answer might deal with the connection between Premise and Intermediate Conclusion, or it might deal with the connection between the Intermediate and the Main Conclusion. Hence, we sort of have twice as much to worry about. We need to think about what assumptions are being made with each move, or what objections we might make.

### Right Column

## Try these problems, **untimed**
think through the following:

How can we tell whether there's an Intermediate Conclusion?

Are there Support / Conclusion / Opinion indicators that help us show us when there is or isn't an Intermediate Conclusion?

Practice testing whether a given claim X is or isn't a conclusion by asking yourself, "_Why should I believe X_ ?" If the author offers a reason, then we can call it a conclusion. If she doesn't, then it's just an unsupported claim.

## Missing Links (color-coded) Set 2Process

- Type: Process | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 9

A lot of missing links and inferences rely on **common terms** .

Consider this argument:
_Rex's painting_ __ _uses bold colors_ . _Artists are_ _usually_ _hiding the fact that their painting expresses no deep ideas if they_ _use bold colors_ _. Thus,_ _Rex_ _is_ _probably_ _ashamed of his_ _painting_ _._

Several ideas were mentioned twice:
- Rex's painting
- bold colors
There was also a frequency modifier mentioned twice:
- usually / probably

A couple ideas were mentioned only once:

## Missing Links: First TastePatterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 3

### Left Column

## These 6 problems test our ability to Link language from the evidence to language from the conclusion.

To get better at spotting these missing links, we focus on structure and common terms vs. new terms. Terms that get mentioned twice are usually not going to be in the correct answer. (A terrible mnemonic for that is, _Mentioned Twice? That'll Suffice._ ) Meanwhile, any _New Concept_ that appears in the conclusion is practically guaranteed to be in the correct answer.

Every claim has a a Naming Part and a Telling Part. Suppose we have this conclusion: "Clearly, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior".

The Naming Part is **monkeys that can dance ballet** and the Telling Part says that this thing we named **makes humans feel inferior** .

What did the Evidence tell us about "monkeys that can dance ballet"?
What did the Evidence tell us "makes us feel inferior"?

If both questions can be answered, then there's a missing link between those answers.

If one or both of the questions can't be answered, then there's a missing link between that concept and some concept in the evidence. Here's an example of each.

**_Naming Part Not Mentioned_**
The human ego is certainly frail and volatile, but we know that when we don't feel special, we feel inferior. Thus, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior.

_missing link:_ monkeys that can dance ballet make us feel not-special

**_Telling Part Not Mentioned_**
The human ego is certainly frail and volatile, but when we see monkeys that can dance ballet, we know that we are not categorically different from other higher primates. Thus, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior.

_missing link:_ When we don't feel categorically different from other higher primates, we feel inferior.

**_Naming and Telling Parts Are Both Mentioned_**
The human ego is certainly frail and volatile, but when we see monkeys that can dance ballet, we know that we are not categorically different from other higher primates. And since we feel inferior when we don't feel special, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior.

_missing link:_ When we don't feel categorically different from other higher primates, we don't feel special.

**_Naming and Telling Parts Are Both Unmentioned_**
Watching higher primates do a sophisticated task makes us feel not-special. Thus, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior.

_missing links_ : Monkeys are higher primates / doing ballet is a sophisticated task / feeling not-special makes us feel inferior

### Right Column

Try to predict what's missing. Sometimes, your intuition will clearly hear something missing. Other times, you'll need to ask yourself questions in order to find it.

- Which claim is the Conclusion?
- Is there any wording appearing in the conclusion that never shows up in the evidence?
- If so, what concept in the evidence is that _New Wording in the Conclusion_ supposed to match up with?
- If not, then what ideas from the Evidence are associated with each half of the conclusion? How do those ideas need to be linked?

As you review these problems,
**correct answers -**
does it supply a Missing Link? Was it the contrapositive of your prediction?
is it connecting Conclusion language to Premise language or connecting Premise language to Premise language?

**incorrect answers -**
does it have the **Wrong Strength** ?
does it lack some **Needed Idea** (such as a New Idea in the conclusion)
is it **Irrelevant** for some reason?
it is an illegal **Reversal / Negation** of the connection we wanted?

## Most Supported + Causal Difference-Maker Patterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 23 mins | Weeks: Week 13

### Left Column

## **Causal Difference-Maker** is a fuzzy term
## we use primarily for Most Supported
The most common type of reasoning tested on Most Supported questions is Causality, so whenever we read the stimulus on a Most Supported question, we listen closely for any explicit or implicit cause/effect relationships.

These tend to be expressed in three ways:

- **Causal verbs** _(leads to, results in, contributes to, increases, limits, etc.)_ When they use causal verbs, they often use more than one, setting up a causal chain.

- **Causal connectors** between adjacent claims _(Because of this, this allows, this makes possible, accordingly, thereby, etc.)_ This is a sneaky way of conveying causality. We might hear something like, "Ants shed their hind legs and grow new ones every two years. This cycle allows the ant to avoid suffering from arthritis." This sort of causal connection reveals that an ant's hind legs influence whether or not they suffer from arthritis.

- Presenting two differences, with the suggestion that **one difference explains the other** . (for example: _Tim and Jill were both struggling with reading comp. Jill, unlike Tim, decided to try meditating shortly after practicing each RC passage. Jill no longer struggles with RC, though Tim still does.)_ This seems to be suggesting that meditation was a Causal Difference-Maker when it comes to Jill getting better at RC. We certainly can't**** _prove_ that, but if you've been told about Difference X (Jill does meditation, Tim doesn't) then it's supportable to say it might be the reason for Difference Y (Jill no longer struggles with RC).

### Right Column

## **Try these 4 questions untimed**

Look for causal verbs, like "promotes, inhibits, increases, lowers, contributes to, influences". Look for ", -ing" constructions that convey causation, such as "Sarah yelled at the top of her lungs, shaking the vase on the shelf".

Look for causal connector language that tells you that the previous sentence has a causal relationship to the present sentence, like "because of this, due to this, this allows, this makes possible, accordingly, that is why".

Look for one Difference that could potentially explain another Difference.

## Necessary Assumption - First TasteProcess

- Type: Process | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 3

### Left Column

**Necessary Assumption** is the second most common question type in Logical Reasoning, and it's pretty difficult for a number of reasons. Assumptions perform one of two functions:

- they supply a missing idea the author was clearly thinking
- they rule out an objection that would hurt the author's argument

When they are supplying a **missing idea** , Necessary Assumption questions feel a lot like Sufficient Assumption or Principle-Strengthen. Sometimes they even feel like Must Be True, which makes sense because we're being asked what Must Be Assumed. These examples are heavy on conditional logic, and the correct answer seems to be regurgitating concepts that were mentioned in the stimulus.  ("in-the-box" answers)

But when they are **ruling out an objection** , Necessary Assumption questions feel more like Weaken or Flaw. These examples are more about causation and comparisons. The correct answer will often feel like it's bringing in new, outside considerations that haven't been talked about yet. ("out-of-the-box" answers).

How do we know which sort of Necessary Assumption question we're doing?

We don't, but generally speaking if we see conditional logic or see certain buzzwords / phrases being repeated twice within the paragraph, then we're being tested on a missing idea.

If we otherwise think the author is making a recommendation, making a prediction, or offering a hypothesis to explain some curious background data, then we're probably being tested on potential objections.

### Right Column

## Try these 10 questions, **untimed**

- Ask yourself if there's a New Concept in the conclusion that was never defined in the evidence. If so, then what idea from the evidence is the author connecting with this New Concept in the conclusion? (that's the most common way we spot missing links)

- Ask yourself if the author is assuming a Similarity between two things, or if the author is assuming a Difference between two things. (don't be afraid to spell out the 'obvious' -- in many cases that's all correct answers are doing)

- If neither of those methods worked, then you should try to argue the Anti-Conclusion. If the author is concluding "Thus, the sky is blue", then you're arguing "the sky is NOT blue". How can we accept the author's evidence but still argue our anti-conclusion. Or, alternatively, given the anti-conclusion we're arguing for, how can we respond to the author's evidence?

Can you think of an alternate explanation for it?
Can you argue it's not truly relevant to what's being debated in the conclusion?
Can you outweigh its effect by introducing new considerations?

If you're able to articulate an Objection, then the author is assuming that your objection is **not**  the case.

## **ANSWERS**

be wary of stuff that's **Too Strong**
be wary of **Comparisons** never made or implied
be wary of **Unmentioned** concepts

if an answer is ruling out an idea with the word "**not** ", check that one out! negate it by removing the "not" and see if it turns into an Objection. if so, pick it.

if an answer is **conditional** , diagram it. see whether that conditional (or more likely its contrapositive) matches a reasoning move the author made. be wary of illegal reversals or negations of the author's thinking.

## Principle-Conform - (First Taste)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 12 mins | Weeks: Week 4

### Left Column

There are three varieties of **Principle-Conform**
- The stimulus is a principle (or pair of principles)
- The answers are principles
- There are no explicit principles, just implied/illustrated ones

In this set, we have
- 3 Principles in Stimulus
- 2 Principles in Answers
- 1 Implicit Principle

**Principles in Stimulus**
When the stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.

To find an answer that Conforms to this principle, we want an answer where the situation described matches the Trigger of the principle, and the judgment concluded matches the Outcome of the principle. (Even when the principles aren't expressed as conditionals, there will still usually be two things we need to make sure match up).

**Principles in Answers**
It's essentially the same mindset as a Principle-Strengthen question. We read the argument and then anticipate the principle underlying the argument by pre-phrasing "IF evidence, THEN conclusion". We expect the correct answer to _Link_ language from the Evidence to the Conclusion's language. All that's different is that because the question stem says _conform_ to the author's reasoning, we can't pick an answer that's overly strong. (That usually doesn't matter, but occasionally it does)

**Implicit Principles**
These feel similar to Parallel reasoning questions, because we're given an argument in the stimulus, and then each answer choice is its own argument on its own unique topic. We're looking for an underlying similarity to connect the correct answer to the original argument.

On Parallel questions, those similarities come more in the form of technical / structural similarities (i.e. "Since the original argument had a _Most_ premise and a _Some_ premise, the correct answer's argument will also have those features").

But with Principle-Conform it's more about the underlying gist or rule-of-thumb. We don't try to diagram the arguments. We just want to articulate a general moral to the story or operating principle that's guiding the author's thinking, and then we try to find an answer that matches that.

### Right Column

## Try these six questions, **untimed**

Principle questions are all about matching language in the answer to language in the original.

When we're dealing with Conditionals, then the trigger of the conditional should match the Evidence and the outcome of the conditional should match the Conclusion.

If we're dealing with an implicit principle or a non-binary principle (i.e. "it's important to balance work time and family time"), then it's not quite as cut and dry. We can get a little more gist-y and holistic with our thinking.

## Principle-Conform + Principle in Stimulus (set 1)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 30 mins | Weeks: Week 18

### Left Column

When a **Principle-Conform** stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.

If the rule(s) is conditional, then we need to be attentive to conditional logic words, conjunctions such as "and / or", and sometimes even little qualifying adjectives, as we diagram how the rule works.

_a brutally hard example_
A resignation offered under duress should be accepted only if the person resigning still endorses it one week later or they spoke to HR about it before submitting the resignation.

**only if** = put it on the right

offered under duress          still endorsed 1 week later
and                  →        or
should be accepted            spoke to HR before

The way these principles get used, you want the normative, "Judgy" term to be on the right of the arrow (in this case, whether it _should / shouldn't_ be accepted sounds like the judgment this rule would render). So an effective rephrase would be something like this:

resignation offered under duress
and                                      should
didn't speak to HR before        →      not be
and                                      accepted
not endorsed 1 week later

This is not the only form a correct version could take. We could consider leaving off the context-setting idea that we're talking about resignations offered under duress and just focus on whether we should accept it based on whether the person spoke to HR and whether they endorsed it 1 week later.

Often, simplifying down to the core ideas is beneficial. But if we end up with two answers that both seem to work (they're both seeming to let us conclude that we shouldn't accept a resignation), then we need to re-read the principle because we might be missing some of the context-setting qualifiers like "under duress" that we selectively ignored while diagramming.

Perhaps only one of those two remaining answers actually establishes that this resignation was under duress.

### Right Column

## **Try these 8 problems, untimed** .

- 5 of them want us to apply 1 or 2 conditional rules.
- 2 of them want us to conform to a fuzzier, non-conditional rule or pattern.
- 1 of them wants us to actually find a situation that **violates** the rule.

Given a principle: _has cinnamon in it  → delicious_
We can only use that rule to conclude that something **is** delicious. Rules can only prove what's on their **right** side. So if we saw any answer choices that were concluding, "Therefore, the soup was _not_ delicious", we immediately know that answer is wrong.

When we're given two rules, we're only going to be using one or the other to justify our correct answer. Start each answer choice by scanning for the conclusion, so that you can see which rule it's trying to use. That way you can remind yourself of what _that_ rule is all about (ignoring the other one temporarily), and just focus on whether the answer choice clearly establishes the trigger of the rule it's trying to use.

Violating a rule means that you trigger the left side but go against the right side. If we said that "Jonathan had an apple pie that had cinnamon and whip cream in it, but it wasn't that tasty", that would violate the principle above.

All of these examples are the much-more common style of Principle-Strengthen in which the _answers_ are principles. _Billy lied to our customers. Thus he shouldn't be on our staff anymore._

(A) A person can be on the staff only if they are consistently truthful with customers

Given this argument, we might see a weighing-tradeoffs answer
_Billy is extremely dependable, although she lied to our customers. Clearly, she shouldn't be on our staff._

(A) Dependability is a less important trait in a staff member than is honesty.

## Quantifiers Set 2Process

If so, then we can combine those claims to derive at least **Some** overlap between the two other traits.

_If we know_ Some X's are **B**
_and we know_                     All **B** 's are ~A
_we can derive_   Some  X's            are  ~A

_If we know_ Most X's are **B**
_and we know_                   All **B** 's are ~A
_we can derive_   Most  X's            are  ~A

_but if we know_ Most **B** 's are X
_and we know_       All **B** 's are ~A
_we can only derive_   Some X's are ~A

The key idea is that in order to make an inference, the **trigger** of the conditional needs to show up in another claim. If the **outcome** is the term that shows up elsewhere, we can't infer anything (but that's where a lot LSAT authors' flawed inferences come from).

In the third example, the overlapping term is the trigger in both claims, so we can't "chain" the ideas together. But we still know for sure that there are some X's who have the property of being B, and thus they also must have the property of being ~A.

Sometimes, we can infer something from **two Most claims** .

GIVEN:      Most A's are B          Most A's are B
+ Most A's are C      + Most B's are ~A

INFER:    Some B's are C      There are more B's than A's

Then there's a famous **_incorrect_** __ inference from two Most claims:

GIVEN:    Most A's are B
+ Most B's are C

INFER:      nothing  (but LSAT authors think, "Most A's are C")

## Quantifiers: Trait Overlap inferencesProcess

- Type: Process | Time: 35 mins | Weeks: Week 19

### Left Column

Questions involving multiple quantified words are usually testing our ability to see when two traits do / don't have to overlap, and to what degree.

## COMMON QUANTIFIERS
**100%** _- all, any, each, every,_ (or universal nouns)
**more than 50%** _- most, usually, typically, generally, majority_
**less than 50%** _- few, rarely, infrequently_
**at least a handful** _- many, commonly, often_
**at least one** _- some, sometimes, can, not all, not always, need not_

If we see a conditional-strength quantifier like "No A's are B", we think
A → ~B
B → ~A

If there are any additional facts about A or B, then we can derive a Trait Overlap inference.

ADDITIONAL FACT      WE CAN INFER
Some X's are B              Some X's are ~A
Some B's are X              Some X's are ~A
Most B's are X                Some X's are ~A
Most X's are B                Most X's are ~A
All X's are B                    All X's are ~A
All B's are X                    Some X's are ~A

_If we know_ Some X's are **B**
_and we know_                     All **B** 's are ~A
_we can derive_   Some  X's            are  ~A

_If we know_ Most X's are **B**
_and we know_                   All **B** 's are ~A
_we can derive_   Most  X's            are  ~A

_but if we know_ Most **B** 's are X
_and we know_       All **B** 's are ~A
_we can only derive_   Some X's are ~A

In the first two examples, we can hook the conditional onto the end of the first statement, and that allows us to "chain" the trigger of the first claim to the outcome of the conditional.

In the third example, the overlapping term is the trigger in both claims, so we can't "chain" the ideas together. But we still know for sure that there are some X's who have the property of being B, and thus they also must have the property of being ~A.

Some quantified claims mean the same thing even when you reverse the terms. "Some dogs are brown = some brown things are dogs".  Other quantified terms definitely don't mean the same thing if you reverse the terms. "Most Senators are men ≠ Most men are Senators".

REVERSIBLE
Some X's are Y = Some Y's are X
Many X's are Y = Many Y's are X
No X's are Y = No Y's are X

NOT REVERSIBLE                WE CAN ONLY SAY
Most X's are Y ≠ Most Y's are X          Some Y's are X
All X's are Y ≠ All Y's are X              Some Y's are X

Sometimes, we can infer something from **two Most claims** .

GIVEN:      Most A's are B          Most A's are B
+ Most A's are C      + Most B's are ~A

INFER:    Some B's are C      There are more B's than A's

Then there's a famous **_incorrect_** __ inference from two Most claims:

GIVEN:    Most A's are B
+ Most B's are C

INFER:      nothing  (but LSAT authors think, "Most A's are C")

### Right Column

## Try these 8 questions, untimed.

If the question is testing us on what can be inferred, scan primarily for **conditional** strength quantifiers (_all / each / every / any / no / none_ , or anything else that's conditional).

If we find a conditional, then we look to see whether the trigger of that conditional (or the trigger of the contrapositive) shows up in any other claim.

If so, then we can combine those claims to derive **Some** overlap between the two other traits.

For example, if we're told "All cats are sneaky"
we have two triggers:
_cat → sneaky_
_not sneaky → not cat_

If we see "cat" or "not sneaky" in any other claim, then we can make a trait overlap inference.

If they tell us "Some cats are smelly", then we know that "smelly and sneaky overlap, at least sometimes".

If they tell us that "Some of Alan's pets are not sneaky", then we know that "Some of Alan's pets are not cats".

If we're being tested on what's Flawed about an argument, then the author is probably going to be assuming that two traits overlap, even though it's possible they never show up together.

## Relative vs. AbsolutePatterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 25 mins | Weeks: Week 18

### Left Column

## **Relative / Superlative vs. Absolute**

We can say that Bill Gates is poorer than Jeff Bezos, even though we would never say Bill Gates is poor (even after the divorce). We can say that a grasshopper is taller than a ladybug, even though we wouldn't say that a grasshopper is tall.

Adjectives like "poor" and "tall" are in absolute form. Something is poor or not-poor, tall or not-tall. Where does that threshold occur where you go from not-poor to poor, or from not-tall to tall? There's isn't any way to quantify most absolute adjectives, but we still know when they're being abused (such as calling Bill Gates poor or calling a grasshopper tall).

Because a relative adjective like "poorer" can be apt even though an absolute adjective like "poor" isn't (or vice versa), LSAT loves test the distinction between these two forms. Some plan might be our **best** option, but still not be a **good** option. One restaurant might be farther away than another, but still not be far away. Always be cautious when you see that the stimulus/passage has used a word in one form but an answer choice is using it in the other form.

### Right Column

## **Try these 7 problems untimed.**

Watch out for **Relative vs. Absolute** distinctions
in the argument as well as in answer choices.
There's also a mathematical sense of Relative (fractions / percentages / ratios) vs. Absolute (amount / number). When authors see that a certain percentage is rising, they assume that means that the real number/amount is also rising.

But it's possible for a percentage to rise, even while the real number stays the same or shrinks.

Suppose that last year a certain chess club had 4 boys and 4 girls on it. We can say 50% of the members were boys. Now what if _this_ year, we find out that 80% of the members are boys. Does that mean more boys are on the chess club?

Not necessarily, if 3 girls left, and the chess club is now 4 boys and 1 girl, then 80% of the club's members are boys, even though no new boys were added. Percents and fractions are a Relative relationship between numerator and denominator:

_Boy members_
_------------------    =  % of boys_
_Total members_
The percent of boys can be changed by adding boy members to the numerator or by subtracting non-boy members from the denominator. When authors assume that going from 50% boys to 80% boys means more boys, they're assuming that the denominator is at least as big as before. They're assuming that this percentage increase was _not_ caused by non-boy members leaving.

## Sampling FlawsPatterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 35 mins | Weeks: Week 2

### Left Column

## **Sampling**
is one of the ten Famous Flaws.

There's nothing inherently wrong with using a sample to extrapolate to a general conclusion, but a sample can seem very untrustworthy if it's **too small** , **unrepresentative** , **biased** , or **self-selecting** .

People often confuse Sampling with Part to Whole. Here's the difference:

_Part vs. Whole_ flaws involve assuming a similarity between individual parts and a collective entity (that entity will always be a singular noun).

_Sampling_ involves an assumed similarity
between individuals and other individuals. (that will always be a plural noun)

**Whole to Part**
The Millville High School cheer team was banned from the City Dance-Off competition. Tony is a member of the Millville High School cheer team. Thus, Tony is banned from the City Dance-Off competition.

_this fails to consider that Tony could transfer to a different school and still be able to compete in this competition; they don't have his name written down somewhere, like HE'S blacklisted._

**Part to Whole**
Tony, Tanya, and Tyreke are members of the Millville High School cheer team. None of them have enough money to pay the entry fee for the City Dance-Off competition. Thus, the Millville High School cheer team does not have enough money to pay the entry fee for the City Dance-Off competition.

_this fails to consider that even though these three single people may have enough money to pay the fee, the squad collectively might have enough money._

**Sampling**
Tony, Tanya, and Tyreke are members of the Millville High School cheer team. None of them have enough money to pay the entry fee for the City Dance-Off competition. Thus, each of the members of the Millville High School cheer team does not have enough money to pay the entry fee for the City Dance-Off competition.

_this fails to consider that even though these three cheerleaders don't have enough money there may be some members who are different. the income level of the three T people isn't representative of the income of other members._

### Right Column

## Try these 9 questions, **untimed.**

Thinking about how the sample data in the Evidence could fail to prove the Conclusion.

Is the sample too small?
Is it atypical or unrepresentative for some reason?
Is it biased or self-selecting?

### Left Column

Typically, LSAT arguments that deal with **science** have a messy, real-world feel to them. We're less likely to think we're supposed to be looking at the internal logic of the argument and more likely to think that we're going to be weighing the impact of brand new considerations in the answers.

These are the most common question stem keywords for Locate Detail questions:
_according to the passage_
_the passage indicates_
_the author states / mentions / says / offers_

These tend to be the easiest questions available in RC, because they're asking for stuff we can actually find in the passage. They usually have Searchable Keywords in the question stem as well, so we can know where to look.

When we get an EXCEPT version of these, though, it will usually be more time consuming because we have to hunt down four things in the passage.

**Locate Detail** questions with good keywords (and thus a _Defined Target_ ) would potentially be the most realistic problems for us to get right if we only had 5 mins to deal with our last RC passage of the section. ## Theme / Example + What's ImpliedProcess

- Type: Process | Time: 5 mins | Weeks: Week 4

### Left Column

This set contains a _Theme / Example_ passage from June 2007, as well as three accompanying **What's Implied / Most Supported** question tasks, like **Inference** , **Opinion** , and **Attitude** . These tend to be harder, because the correct answer is a gisty re-wording or a technical implication of one or more sentences from the passage.

Most Supported means that question stem is expressed in "squishy" terms, indicating that our answer might not be fully derivable from the passage, but it's more derivable than any other answer:
- most supported
- most reasonably inferred
- most likely to agree
- suggests

### Right Column

## Attempt the passages and questions **untimed** .

You've read them before, so you might be able to just dive in where needed, but since Most Supported questions often pull support from unexpected sentences or feel like a holistic implication of someone's view, it's safer to have just read the whole passage.

- When looking for an answer, be very cautious about overly Strong / Specific / Comparative wording in the answer choices.

- Gravitate towards stuff that sounds more weakly worded, where the burden of proof is one example, or where you feel like you don't need support beyond the passage to verify an answer is true.

- Don't settle on an answer until you've matched it up with some Support Sentence(s) in the passage that you think you could use, if you had to defend your answer in court.

## Weaken + ComparisonProcess

- Type: Process | Time: 25 mins | Weeks: Week 7

### Left Column

## **Weaken** **** questions intersect with comparisons
## in a handful of common ways:

- If the author is concluding some sort of _Plan / Recommendation_ , then we end up comparing our current world to the world in which we follow the author's advice. Since she is telling us this advice will work out _better,_ either overall or in some specific sense, our job in being skeptical is to think about _what else_ could be different in the world where we follow the author's advice, that could go against the result the author promised.

- If the author is comparing two things and seeming to favor one over the other, then we need to consider a full comparison of all the advantages and disadvantages of each thing (_Net Gain / Loss_ ). If the author concludes that X is better than Y, then we can weaken with bad stuff about X or good stuff about Y.

- If the author is assuming that "what was true in the past will be true in the present" or assuming that "what is currently true will be true in the future", then we can weaken those arguments by looking for something important that's different between the past and the present or between the present trends and the future.

- If the author is trying to explain a difference between two groups / situations, then the test will often have a correct answer that presents some **other difference** between the two things that could be an Alternate Explanation.

### Right Column

## Try these 6 problems, **untimed**

Focus on whether the problem seems to be dealing with
- a plan / recommendation
- preferring one option over another
- assuming similarities between different timeframes
- offering a causal explanation for a difference between two groups or situations

## Weaken + Explain Curious FactPatterns

- Type: Patterns | Time: 25 mins | Weeks: Week 20

### Left Column

These **Weaken** questions all involve an author presenting a **Curious Fact** and then concluding or assuming a certain causal explanation of that fact.

- The **evidence** presents some _Curious Fact_ (an asymmetric comparison like a correlation, a statistical change, or a difference between two groups or situations)

- The author's **conclusion** will state or assume one possible _Causal Explanation_ for this curious fact.

- The correct answer will either deal with _Alternate Explanations_ for the curious fact or with the _plausibility_ of the Author's**** Explanation.

### Right Column

## Try these 5 questions**untimed** ,
while rehearsing these steps:

What is the _Curious Fact_ ?****
- what language tips us off? is it an asymmetric comparison?
- ask__ yourself the Curious Fact:  _why/how is it that [curious fact]?_

What is the _Author's Explanation / Hypothesis?_
- Is there a verb or expression in the conclusion indicating that the author is making a causal claim?
- Make sure that you spell out every step in the author's causal story. It's very common for an author to fail to mention each step in the causal chain that gets them from their causal explanation to the actual curious fact.

Does the correct answer deal with an _Alternate Explanation_ ?
- Is it dealing with the idea of _Reverse Causality_ ?
- Is it dealing with the idea of a _Third Factor_ ?

Or does it address the **plausibility** of the Author's Explanation?
- Is it addressing plausibility via covariation? (strengthening by No Cause / No Effect, or More Cause / More Effect data points or weakening by Cause without Effect, or Effect without Cause data points)

- Or is it addressing plausibility some other way?  (placing the suspect at the scene of the crime, or explaining the motive/mechanism by which the crime would have taken place)

## What's Stated (Locate Detail + Five Questions)Process

- Type: Process | Time: 10 mins | Weeks: Week 11

### Left Column

This set contains three__ passages from test 55 and 56, as well as six **What's Stated** questions.

Most such questions are **Locate Detail** :
_according to the passage, the passage indicates, the author states / says / mentions_

There's one we call **Five Questions** , because each answer choice is a question, and whichever one we can find an answer to in the passage is the correct choice.

When we're being tested on what was / wasn't stated in the passage, our ability to get correct answers comes down mainly to doing our due diligence. We shouldn't pick an answer until we can point to where in the passage that answer is stated.

Naturally, we won't see the same **wording** necessarily stated. We'll get the same **meaning** . But we should still feel like our Support Line from the passage means the same thing as the answer we're picking.

### Right Column

## Attempt the passages and questions **untimed** .

You can read the passage first, if you want to practice your big picture reading and get a feel for the lay of the land in each passage.

Or you can skip reading the passage, and just find the line(s) being tested based on the keywords in the question stem. That's good practice for "emergency situations", when you only have 4-5 minutes for your last passage in an RC section, and you're determined to get at least a couple correct answers.

Practice the skill of finding keywords in the question stem, or in an answer choice, and doing a quick visual scan or Ctrl / Command + F search in order to locate where those words appear in the passage.
