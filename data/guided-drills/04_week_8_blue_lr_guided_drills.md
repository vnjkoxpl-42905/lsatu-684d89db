## 1. Explain Curious Fact (Medium) Set 4

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Explain Curious Fact (Medium) Set 4
category: Process
estimated_time: 50 mins
question_count: 5
question_type: Explain Curious Fact
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Explain Curious Fact
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

In the world of Strengthen, Weaken, Flaw, and Evaluate, there are tons of arguments where the author is trying to **Explain a Curious Fact**.

- The **evidence** presents some **Curious Fact** (an asymmetric comparison like a correlation, a statistical change, or a difference between two groups or situations)

- The author's **conclusion** will state or assume one possible **Causal Explanation** for this curious fact (it will sometimes deny an Alternate Explanation as well).

- The correct answer will deal either with an **Alternate Explanation** or with the **plausibility of the Author's Explanation**.

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 questions untimed,

while rehearsing these steps:

What is the **Curious Fact?**

- what language tips us off? is there an _Asymmetric Comparison?_
- ask yourself the Curious Fact: "Why is it that .. [curious fact]?"

What is the **Author's Explanation / Hypothesis?**

- Is there a verb or expression in the conclusion indicating that the author is making a causal claim?
- Make sure that you spell out every step in the author's causal story. It's very common for an author to fail to mention each step in the causal chain that gets them from their causal explanation to the actual curious fact.

Does the correct answer deal with an **Alternate Explanation?**

- Is it dealing with the idea of _Reverse Causality?_
- Is it dealing with the idea of a _Third Factor?_

Or does it address the **plausibility** of the Author's Explanation?

- Is it addressing plausibility via covariation? (strengthening by _No Cause / No Effect_, or _More Cause / More Effect_ data points or weakening by _Cause without Effect_, or _Effect without Cause_ data points)
- Or is it addressing plausibility some other way? (placing the suspect at the scene of the crime, or explaining the motive/mechanism by which the crime would have taken place)

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Assumes Similarity (set 1)
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 50 mins
question_count: 7
reasoning_type: Comparison
question_feature: Assumes Similarity
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column



## Every comparison hides an assumed sameness

In the world of **Comparisons**, authors lean on a few recurring assumptions:
- What was true in the **past** is still true **today**.
- What is true **today** will be true in the **future**.
- What was true for **this** group, person, or thing is true for **that** one.

When an author reasons across time or across cases, the argument only holds if the two sides are similar enough to carry the conclusion. That assumed similarity is the pressure point.

- **Weaken** by pointing out a salient difference between the two sides.
- **Strengthen** by adding a similarity or affirming the assumed one.

#### Right Column



## Try these 7, untimed

Go for accuracy and pattern recognition, not speed.

For each argument:
- Name the comparison. What two things, groups, or moments is the author treating as alike?
- Find where that sameness is assumed rather than proven.
- Prephrase the gap: "this only works if the two sides are similar in the way that matters."

Then judge the answers:
- A weakener surfaces a **difference** that breaks the link.
- A strengthener shores up the **similarity**.
- Wrong answers often raise a difference that does not matter, or simply restate the comparison without testing it.

## 3. Anti-Causal - Set 3

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Anti-Causal - Set 3
category: Process
estimated_time: 1 hr
question_count: 6
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Anti-Causal
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

In many **Anti-Causal** arguments, the author doesn't believe that "X causes / explains Y", because the author's premise supposedly makes that causal relationship seem implausible or wrong.

_for example_ -- Timmy says that his love of sushi was caused by his upbringing. However, this must be false, since he did not grow up in a Japanese household.

In objecting to these arguments, we're essentially trying to restore the idea that, "yes, X _does_ cause Y" (Timmy's upbringing _is_ what explains his love of sushi) while reconciling that with the author's concern.

Even though your premise is true,  
it still _is_ the case that X caused Y,  
because ... ?

Even though Timmy didn't grow up in a Japanese household,  
it still _is_ the case that his upbringing caused his love of sushi,  
because ... ?

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 problems untimed.

Focus on finding the anti-causal features.

The authors will be thinking, "How can this Cause/Effect relationship be true if fact X seems to go against it?" (fact X will normally feel like something showing Cause w/o Effect or Effect w/o Cause)

So our job in thinking about these problems is to figure out we can reconcile fact X with the Cause/Effect relationship.

Set up your thinking this way to respond to their evidence:

- A _does_ cause B (or A _did_ cause B)
- X is just true because ... ?

Correct weakening ideas on Anti-Causal arguments will usually feel like they're "**making an excuse**" for the premise, helping us to see it in a new light that allows us to still believe in the Cause/Effect relationship being disputed.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

- Focus on finding **Conditional Indicator** words and using them as a way to organize how you write the If / Then rule.
- Write or think through the contrapositive as well.
- Beware the temptation to make illegal Backwards or Opposite moves.

How does Conditional Logic show up, by question type?

- **Principle** (the left side of the Principle matches the Evidence, the right side matches the Conclusion)

- **Parallel** (diagramming these arguments with "algebraic recipes" allows us to be more picky and decisive when evaluating answers)

- **Assumption** (the conclusion gives us an A --> Z logic path we want to prove, and the evidence + the correct answer choice assemble together to create that A --> Z path / or the evidence gives us a conditional rule that potentially could output the conclusion, but we still need an answer choice to tell us something in order to clearly trigger that rule.)

- **Must Be True / Most Supported** (multiple conditionals often chain together, and provided facts often match the contrapositive-trigger of a conditional and allow us to infer the contrapositive-outcome)

- **Flaw / Parallel Flaw** (Flaw + Conditional Logic usually means the **Necessary vs. Sufficient** flaw, which is using a rule in an illegal Backwards or Opposite fashion. Sometimes it involves messing up Or / And stuff)

#### Right Column

# Try these 10 problems in 17 minutes

(1.5x = 23 mins, 2x = 34 mins)  
featuring conditional logic, avg diff = level 4

Focus on answering them all within the time limit, and then when you review the set (untimed) consider doing more diagramming to improve your mastery of conditional logic.

# CONDITIONAL KEYWORDS

- Put on **Left** (sufficient conditions)  
  _if, when, any, each, all, every, the only, whenever, whoever, X's_

- Put it on the **Right** (necessary conditions)  
  **only if**, only, must, requires, ensures, will, always, implies, presupposes, depends on, guarantees,

- Put negated version of either idea on left (**If-Not**)  
  **unless**, without, until, except

- **Negative to Positive** conversions  
  No A's are B = All A's are ~B  
  Not all A's are B = Some A's are ~B  
  Few A's are B = Most A's are ~B
