### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Back again for more fun with **Explain a Curious Fact**, in which the evidence will provide some _Curious Fact_ (almost always an asymmetric comparison).

The author is implicitly asking herself, "Wussup with this Curious Fact?", and her conclusion either states or implies some causal explanation.

We evaluate these arguments by thinking about:

- **Alternate Explanations** for this curious fact
- the **plausibility** of the Author's Explanation

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 problems, untimed.

**(1) Locate the _Curious Fact_ in the evidence, and ask the curious question.**

EXAMPLE: "People who run marathons are more likely than those who don't to be in excellent health. Clearly the mental discipline it takes to run a marathon also allows people to make better dietary and lifestyle choices."

The first sentence is a correlation, a Curious Comparison (ppl who are X are more likely than those who aren't to be Y). The author is _asking_ herself: **Hmm - why is is that** there's this correlation between marathon running and excellent health?

**(2) Spell out the author's explanation for the curious fact, because the author is often conveying a lot of implicit ideas within their conclusion:**

_People who run marathons have good mental discipline. This mental discipline helps them not over-eat, not over-indulge in alcohol or smoking. By not doing those things, they end up in excellent health._

**(3) Think about _Alternate Explanations_ for the Curious Fact.**

Consider _Reverse Causality_ (maybe being in excellent health lets you run a marathon) or _Third Factor_ (maybe being wealthy both enables you to have time to practice/run a marathon and also enables you to get better nutrition, exercise, and medical care)

**(4) Think about what could help us assess the _Plausibility_ of the Author's Explanation.**

Covariation answers (No Cause, No Effect / Effect without Cause / Cause without Effect), or Presence / Motive / Method ideas (Suspect was at the scene of the crime / suspect had incentive to do it / here's how the suspect could have done it).

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column



## When to reach for the diagram

This set mixes question types, but they share one trigger: **conditional logic** dense enough that diagramming pays off. The types that most often reward a diagram:
- **Must Be True** and **Most Supported**
- **Sufficient Assumption**
- **Parallel** and **Parallel Flaw**
- **Principle-Strengthen** and **Principle-Conform**

The move is always the same. Translate each conditional into an arrow, chain the arrows that connect, and read off what must follow. The contrapositive is half your toolkit, so write it down.

On **Flaw** questions with conditional logic, expect the author to abuse a rule with an illegal **reversal** or **negation**: the Necessary vs. Sufficient flaw.

#### Right Column



## Try these 7, on paper

From tests 65, 71, and June 2007. Keep a pen moving.

For each stimulus:
- Spot the conditional **trigger words** (if, only if, unless, no, all, none) and diagram them.
- Write the **contrapositive** of any rule you diagram.
- Look for conditionals that **chain** together, then read the chain end to end.

Judging answers:
- On inference questions, the answer must be forced by the chain, not merely consistent with it.
- On **Sufficient Assumption**, find the missing link that completes the chain from evidence to conclusion.
- On **Flaw / Parallel Flaw**, name the illegal move (reversal or negation) and match it exactly.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**LENGTH** -- 15 Q's, 15 minutes (1.5x = 22, 2x = 30)  
**TYPES** -- Paradox, Flaw, Role, Main Conclusion, Necessary Assumption, Sufficient Assumption, Strengthen, Weaken, Method, and Must Be True.  
**LEVEL** -- 1

Wait a sec -- these are all level 1? I thought this activity was listed as level 5?

Yes, because the goal is something a level 5 student needs to work on: be able to quickly take care of easier questions, so that you'll have more time and brainpower in reserves for the harder, more flexible thinking questions in the section.

Given how many subtle reading traps there are on LSAT, it's sometimes difficult to ever let yourself believe that an answer you find appealing is correct. "It's never this easy", we might think. But, sure it is! Sometimes it really is that easy. We need to improve our ability to _let the easy be easy._

And we need to develop enough self-trust while we're doing easier problems that we can pick up our pace when possible.

#### Right Column

Try to get through these 15 questions in 15 mins. Keep yourself going at a brisk pace. When you have a strong idea of what you're looking for (like on Main Conclusion, Role, Sufficient Assumption, Flaw), try to be proactive about scanning for and finding your answer, rather than reading all five choices start to finish.

However, on tasks where we compare the impact of new information (like Paradox, Strengthen, Weaken), we may still need to use more time to consider all five answers.

If your thinking feels unfinished on any of them, flag them as you go (so that you can maintain your brisk pace), and then take a second look at them after the timer goes off before you check your answer / the explanation.
