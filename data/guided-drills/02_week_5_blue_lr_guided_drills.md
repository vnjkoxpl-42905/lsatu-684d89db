  - Must Be True

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Causal Difference-Maker** is a fuzzy term  
we use for a variety of things:

## One Difference Explains Another Difference

_Tim and Jill were both struggling with games. Jill, unlike Tim, decided to try meditating shortly after practicing each logic game. Jill no longer struggles with games, though Tim still does._

This seems to be suggesting that meditation was a Causal Difference-Maker when it comes to Jill getting better at games. We certainly can't prove that, but if you've been told about Difference X (Jill does meditation, Tim doesn't) then it's supportable to say it might be the reason for Difference Y (Jill no longer struggles with games).

## The Most Important Consideration

A lot of arguments and 2-speaker conversations are about how we should go about making a decision, or how we should define a certain thing, or how we should evaluate something, or what matters more between two things. Whatever is identified as the "main thing" we should base our decisions / evaluation / definition on is what we might call the Causal Difference-Maker.

## Flip the Causal Difference-Maker

When we're told that X happened, because of Y, then a popular style of correct answer has the form of, "if Y hadn't happened, then X wouldn't have". We sometimes call this Flipping the Causal Difference-Maker. "If the causal difference-maker hadn't occurred, then the effect wouldn't have been observed".

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions untimed

- Look for causal verbs, like "_promotes, inhibits, increases, lowers, contributes to, influences_". Look for ", -ing" constructions that convey causation, such as "Sarah yelled at the top of her lungs, shaking the vase on the shelf".

- Look for causal connector language that tells you that the previous sentence has a causal relationship to the present sentence, like "because of this, due to this, this allows, this makes possible, accordingly, that is why".

- Look for one Difference that could potentially explain another Difference.

- Look for someone to say _what really matters_, what the key concern is when making a judgment, a decision, a classification.

How does Conditional Logic show up, by question type?

- **Principle** (the left side of the Principle matches the Evidence, the right side matches the Conclusion)

- **Parallel** (diagramming these arguments with "algebraic recipes" allows us to be more picky and decisive when evaluating answers)

- **Assumption** (the conclusion gives us an A --> Z logic path we want to prove, and the evidence + the correct answer choice assemble together to create that A --> Z path / or the evidence gives us a conditional rule that is being used in drawing the conclusion, but we still need an answer choice to tell us something in order to clearly trigger that rule.)

- **Must Be True / Most Supported** (multiple conditionals often chain together, and provided facts often match the contrapositive-trigger of a conditional and allow us to infer the contrapositive-outcome)

- **Flaw / Parallel Flaw** (Flaw + Conditional Logic usually means the **Necessary vs. Sufficient** flaw, which is using a rule in an illegal Backwards or Opposite fashion. Sometimes it involves messing up Or / And stuff)

### Metadata

```yaml

  - Weaken
  - Flaw
  - Strengthen
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Anti-Causal
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Anti-Causal** arguments assume or conclude that X ...

- ... doesn't seem to make a difference
- ... doesn't seem to be helping
- ... isn't likely to be causing Y

## DOESN'T MAKE A DIFFERENCE

Ann and Jerry both studied LSAT for 3 months, but only Ann added a mindfulness regimen to her studying. Since they both ended up with similar LSAT scores, apparently the mindfulness regimen didn't do anything for Ann.

## DOESN'T SEEM TO BE HELPING

The companies that are guilty of the most egregious environmental violations are also the companies that are most scrutinized by environmental regulators. This just goes to show the futility of trying to stop companies from damaging the environment with regulatory oversight.

When President Knowles took office, the unemployment rate was 9%. Shortly after her stimulus bill passed, the unemployment rate hit 10%. Clearly, her stimulus bill did nothing to help employment.

## ISN'T LIKELY TO BE CAUSING Y

Someone has been buying ads in the school paper to promote the campaign of Lucas, for Class President. It's rumored that the person buying the ads is Alicia. However, this rumor is clearly false, since Alicia's best friend Trixie is Lucas's main opponent in this class election.

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 anti-causal questions, untimed.

Keep an eye out for some of these common anti-causal objections:

## Would have been even worse without

If the environmental regulators _weren't_ on top of these offending companies, the companies' behavior would be even worse. If it weren't for that stimulus, the unemployment rate would be 12%.

## More frequent source of problem gets more attention

The fact that these bad-acting companies have the most oversight doesn't mean that oversight does nothing to help; we will always direct more of our Solution Efforts to the Biggest Source of a problem. That will always leave someone able to disingenuously argue, "See? The solution efforts don't work. In the places where we have most solution efforts, we have biggest incidence of problem."

## Not fair to compare / different starting points / different obstacles

The mindfulness _did_ help Ann, because she went from a 140 to a 160, whereas Jerry just went from a 150 to 160, or because she tends to have test anxiety that lowers her score on test day whereas he doesn't.

## Overlooked consideration

Even though Alicia's best friend Trixie is running against Lucas, Alicia doesn't want Trixie to win and thus be less available for chillaxing, so Alicia has motive to want Trixie to lose.

Note: when a conclusion is saying "X wasn't caused by Y, it was caused by Z", there are two equally valid ways to affect that conclusion:

- information helping us to assess whether it _wasn't_ Y
- information helping us to assess whether it _was_ Z

# The three main Reasoning Types

**Conditional Logic** - Correct answers address the internal logic:

- assumed links between ideas
- if/then rules
- a missing fact needed to trigger a rule
- valid and invalid conditional inferences

**Comparisons** - Correct answers address assumed similarities or differences between two things:

- part vs. whole
- sample vs. general population
- then vs. now
- this situation vs. that situation
- this group vs. that group

Or they address the net gain / loss of a comparison:

- doing this thing vs. not doing this thing
- this thing vs. that thing

**Causation** - Correct answers relate to one of these two angles:

- alternate explanations for the curious background fact
- the plausibility of the author's explanation for it.

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions, untimed.

If it feels like you're dealing with Rules, or **if / then logic**, then focus on understanding the If / Then rule. What idea goes on the **Left** of the conditional arrow? What idea goes on the **Right?** When we're using **Conditional Logic** to "prove an argument", the **Left** side should be triggered by facts we know from the Evidence (or Answer Choice), and the **Right** side of the rule should match the Conclusion's language.

If it feels like you're dealing with a **Comparison**, think about weakening with potential differences or strengthening by affirming some similarity. If you're dealing with a Net Gain / Loss type of Comparison, think about weakening with potential downsides or strengthening with "equal or better" ideas.

If the author is interpreting the **Causal** significance of some Curious Fact (a weird asymmetry exists, or a study that has some interesting data), take time to understand the Author's Explanation, think about what kind evidence would help make that Explanation seem more/less plausible, and be sure to consider Alternate Explanations for that same Curious Fact.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Causal Difference-Maker** is a very malleable term we can use in a range of difference contexts. It's a useful mental construct because when the stimulus presents something we could describe this way, the correct answer is almost always testing our understanding of the causal difference-maker. Here are some of the ways it shows up.

- when you're hearing about the linchpin, the crucial element, the clutch factor that's involved in making some decision or producing some effect, you're talking about a reaaaal **causal difference-maker**

_you're trying to decide whether to stay at the library past midnight to study more by yourself or to call it a night and walk back with your study buddies to the parking lot. your most important criterion is feeling safe, so that's the causal difference-maker that makes you say, "wait up, guys. I'm leaving, too."_

- when you're given a causal relationship and you have a way to apply it to some comparison

_Having a taller frame makes your center of gravity higher. Ann is taller than Martin_ -- you'll end up thinking to yourself, "this cause they told me about will **make a difference** between the two things in this comparison. Ann's center of gravity will be higher."

- when you're trying to solve a causal mystery, you're searching for the explanation or the **causal difference-maker**

_Ever since Randy came back from summer camp, he's had an itchy rash._ (Maybe it's poison ivy? Some reaction to the water from the lake? Bed bugs from the bunks? Whatever it is, the camp probably caused his rash)

- when the test identifies factors that made a key difference, they often test our understanding of that with answers that feel like, "If that **causal difference-maker hadn't occurred,** then things would be different"

_I saw Bernadette doing a call exercise I had never seen, so I started copying it. Because of this, I was able to dunk a basketball by the end of the summer. (had I not seen her call exercise, I probably wouldn't have dunked by the end of summer)_

#### Right Column

# Try these 10 questions untimed

Look for causal verbs, like "promotes, inhibits, increases, lowers, contributes to, influences". Look for ", -ing" constructions that convey causation, such as "Sarah yelled at the top of her lungs, shaking the vase on the shelf".

Look for causal connector language that tells you that the previous sentence has a causal relationship to the present sentence, like "because of this, due to this, this allows, this makes possible, accordingly, that is why".

Look for one Difference that could potentially explain another Difference.

Apply cause/effect relationships to two halves of a comparison (one that has more of the causal factor, one that has less).

Look for someone to say _what really matters_, what the key concern is when making a judgment, a decision, a classification.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column



This 10 question set is comprised of three questions from the Match Family, which test our ability to understand what role certain claims are playing: Role, Method, and Main Conclusion.

# Role and Main Conclusion

Most questions "hide" the conclusion in one of two ways:

- it's the **very first claim**, or
- it's attached to some **pivot / rebuttal**

**Opinion** indicators and **Pivot** indicators are very helpful for finding the parts of the argument.

When we're assessing whether something is a conclusion (be it main conclusion or intermediate conclusion), we should ask ourselves,

"**Is this Supported?** Why should we believe this?"  
If no support it provided, then it isn't a conclusion.

# Method

These use a lot of abstract verbiage to describe parts of arguments. We want to not only learn how to read this type of language but also to start to think more in terms of this language. When you're using abstract language, you're speaking about general forms / archetypes, and the magic of getting better at standardized tests is getting better at recognizing the general forms / archetypes that show up a lot on that test.

We should get used to the most typical types of reasoning moves:

- Analogies / Distinctions
- Suggesting / Ruling out Alternatives
- Overlooked Consideration / Dubious Assumption
- Flawed Implications of a Claim or Plan

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column



## Find the one claim they actually clash on

Agree / Disagree questions (also called Point at Issue) give you two speakers. The correct answer is the single statement where one speaker would **affirm** it and the other would **deny** it.

Most wrong answers fail one of two tests:
- Only one speaker addresses it, so we cannot know the other's view.
- Both speakers would actually take the same side.

You are not hunting for conclusion versus support here. You are mapping what each person commits to, then finding the one point of genuine collision.

#### Right Column



## Try these 5 in 8 minutes

(1.5x = 12, 2x = 16). Read each person's paragraph for what they claim, not for argument structure.

Then, for each claim the first speaker made, ask:
- Did the second speaker **agree**, **disagree**, or give **no comment**?
- If no one stated it outright, would the first speaker's reasoning force a view the second speaker rejects?

The right answer is the claim where one says yes and the other says no. Run each choice through a simple check: could both speakers fill in a clear "I agree" or "I disagree"? If either has to shrug, that answer is out.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

A correct answer on **Evaluate** asks a relevant question. How do we know if it's relevant? We know because if we answered the question one way, we would Strengthen the argument. If we answered it the opposite way, we would Weaken the argument.

The more important one, though, is the potential to weaken. So, if you want you can just listen for the weakening potential. Think to yourself, "Which of these questions could I answer in a way that would most Weaken the argument?"

# Wrong Answers

**No Impact** - they pose a question that we could answer any way we'd like and it wouldn't affect the argument.

**Too Specific / Strong** - they're requesting an exact figure or measurement or posing an extreme question that wouldn't Weaken if the answer were 'no'.

#### Right Column

# Try these 7 questions, untimed.

_Given_ this evidence

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Causal Difference-Maker** is a fuzzy term  
we use primarily for Most Supported

The most common type of reasoning tested on Most Supported questions is Causality, so whenever we read the stimulus on a Most Supported question, we listen closely for any explicit or implicit cause/effect relationships.

These tend to be expressed in three ways:

- **Causal verbs** (_leads to, results in, contributes to, increases, limits, etc._) When they use causal verbs, they often use more than one, setting up a causal chain.

- **Causal connectors** between adjacent claims (_Because of this, this allows, this makes possible, accordingly, thereby, etc._) This is a sneaky way of conveying causality. We might hear something like, "Ants shed their hind legs and grow new ones every two years. This cycle allows the ant to avoid suffering from arthritis." This sort of causal connection reveals that an ant's hind legs influence whether or not they suffer from arthritis.

- Presenting two differences, with the suggestion that **one difference explains the other**. (for example: _Tim and Jill were both struggling with reading comp. Jill, unlike Tim, decided to try meditating shortly after practicing each RC passage. Jill no longer struggles with RC, though Tim still does._) This seems to be suggesting that meditation was a Causal Difference-Maker when it comes to Jill getting better at RC. We certainly can't _prove_ that, but if you've been told about Difference X (Jill does meditation, Tim doesn't) then it's supportable to say it might be the reason for Difference Y (Jill no longer struggles with RC).

#### Right Column
