```yaml
question_type:
  - Paradox
  - Strengthen
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

A common misconception about **EXCEPT** questions is that the correct answer will be the **opposite** of what the other four do. Like if we were doing a Strengthen-EXCEPT question, a lot of times students are looking for "the one that Weakens".

But even though that will be true sometimes, the task is only asking us to pick something that "doesn't Strengthen", so the correct answer will either Weaken or (more commonly) will be Neutral with no impact.

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 Except questions, untimed

They're a mix of Paradox, Strengthen, and Most Supported, which are the question types where EXCEPT most commonly occurs as a Feature.

These question types are all closely related, because they force us to consider novel ideas and judge what impact, if any, those ideas have. On an EXCEPT variation of these tasks, it's common to get down to two answer choices at which point the one that seems relevant often isn't (it's the correct answer). And the one that seems weirdly different still does its job, but not in the same way the other three eliminated answers did their job.

## 2. Anti-Causal - Set 1

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Anti-Causal - Set 1
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 40 mins
question_count: 5
difficulty_level: not visible
question_type:
  - Weaken
  - Flaw
  - Strengthen
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Anti-Causal
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Anti-Causal** arguments attempt to argue that "X makes no difference / X doesn't help."

The evidence is that things seem to be the same, or worse, without X, or that X wouldn't seem to have anything to do with what really matters.

# EXAMPLES

Some people add a mindfulness regimen to their LSAT studying. But since mindfulness is primarily used to reduce anxiety and Ann suffered from no anxiety, the mindfulness regimen probably wouldn't do anything for Ann.

The companies that are guilty of the most egregious environmental violations are also the companies that are most scrutinized by environmental regulators. This just goes to show the futility of trying to stop companies from damaging the environment with regulatory oversight.

## OBJECTION - would have been even worse without

if the environmental regulators _weren't_ on top of these offending companies, the companies' behavior would be even worse. The fact that these companies have the most oversight doesn't mean that oversight does nothing to help; we will always direct more of our Solution Efforts to the Biggest Source of a problem. That will always leave someone able to disingenuously argue, "See? The solution efforts don't work. In the places where we have most solution efforts, we have biggest incidence of problem."

## OBJECTION - overlooked consideration

even though it looks like mindfulness wouldn't help Ann because she doesn't suffer from anxiety, a mindfulness regimen may still help her, since regular mindfulness breathing changes the gray matter in our brains in ways that boost retention while learning ... _this is true, by the way_

#### Right Column

# Try these five questions, untimed.

Think about what wording in the Conclusion makes it feel like the author is _denying_ a causal connection between two things.

Think about possible objections just as

- would have been even worse without
- biggest source of problem usually gets most solution-effort
- different initial reference points (not fair to compare)
- overlooked consideration that shows how there _could_ be a causal relationship

## 3. Explain Curious Fact (Easier) Set 1

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Explain Curious Fact (Easier) Set 1
category: Process
estimated_time: 46 mins
question_count: 6
difficulty_level: not visible
question_type: Explain Curious Fact
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Explain Curious Fact
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

These arguments involve conclusions that express or assume  
a **causal explanation for a curious fact.**

There are a handful of different question types here, but they all have a common theme:

- The **evidence** presents some **Curious Fact** (an asymmetric comparison like a correlation, a statistical change, or a difference between two groups or situations)

- The author's **conclusion** will state or assume one possible **Causal Explanation** for this curious fact.

- The correct answer will either deal with **Alternate Explanations** for the curious fact or with the **plausibility of the Author's Explanation**.

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions untimed,

while rehearsing these steps:

## What is the Curious Fact?

- what language tips us off? is there an _Asymmetric Comparison?_
- ask yourself the Curious Fact: "Why is it that ... [curious fact]?"

## What is the Author's Explanation / Hypothesis?

- Is there a verb or expression in the conclusion indicating that the author is making a causal claim?
- Make sure that you spell out every step in the author's causal story. It's very common for an author to fail to mention each step in the causal chain that gets them from their causal explanation to the actual curious fact.

## Does the correct answer deal with an Alternate Explanation?

- Is it dealing with the idea of _Reverse Causality?_
- Is it dealing with the idea of a _Third Factor?_

Or does it address the **plausibility** of the Author's Explanation?

- Is it addressing plausibility via covariation? (strengthening by No Cause / No Effect, or More Cause / More Effect data points or weakening by Cause without Effect, or Effect without Cause data points)
- Or is it addressing plausibility some other way? (placing the suspect at the scene of the crime, or explaining the motive/mechanism by which the crime would have taken place)

## 4. Analogies (set 1)

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Analogies (set 1)
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 40 mins
question_count: 6
difficulty_level: not visible
question_type:
  - Must Be True
  - Most Supported
question_feature: Analogies
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Analogies** are two _specific_ cases that share some _general_ trait or relationship.

# TRY THIS

"Kittens are to cats, what puppies are to ______."

You know the answer is _dogs_ because puppies and dogs have the same general relationship that kittens and cats have (youth vs. adult). They are analogous on at least one level.

Because we, as law students, will be tasked with reading judges' opinions and digesting their full meaning, LSAC wants to test us on how well we pick up on implied ideas. We most commonly talk about implied ideas with the word Assumption (unstated, but implied ideas in an argument). But we can also talk about implied ideas in the sense of Inference (Must Be True / Most Supported), because inferences are _implications / consequences / conclusions_ that can be drawn from the information.

And we can talk about implied ideas when it comes to **Completing Analogies**. The structural signpost of an analogy instructs the reader to **carry through** a trait or relationship that was described in the 1st case over to the 2nd case.

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions untimed,

thinking through the analogies.

- What are the two separate cases?
- What is the common trait?
- What language cues exist to telegraph that the author is making a Comparison / Analogy?
- Do the two cases seem "fair to compare" or do you think there could be an important difference between the two cases that calls into question the implied similarity?

# EXAMPLE

An NFL quarterback is the leader of his team, responsible for organizing and inspiring his teammates, all within a very high pressured environment. Similarly, the President is the leader of the Executive Branch and all its staff, and so she too ______.

The test would be expecting us to **carry through** the traits we heard about quarterbacks.

_so she too ..._  
(A) must provide oversight to her staff and motivate them, while managing very stressful situations.

```yaml
  - Flaw

reasoning_type: Comparison
question_feature: Break the Comparison
visible_status: Completed
buttons_visible:
  - Info
  - See Results
  - Takeaways
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

In the world of **Comparisons**, there are several recurring assumptions:

- What's true in this timeframe is true in that timeframe
- What is true today will be true in the future.
- What was true for this thing / this group / this person, will also be true for that thing / that group / that person.

When authors lean on Comparisons like this, they are **assuming similarities**. We can Weaken these arguments by **pointing out salient differences** or we can Strengthen these arguments by providing more similarities, affirming the assumed similarity, or ruling out some crucial dissimilarity.

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 questions, untimed.

Focus on the comparisons being made. The author will be assuming that two timeframes, or two groups, or two situations, or two subjects are fair to compare.

Our job, on **Flaw** and **Weaken** questions, is to think of some important difference that makes them unfair to compare, that "breaks the comparison".

Our job, on **Necessary Assumption** and **Strengthen**, is to provide similarities or rule out these potentially important differences.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

When we do **Main Conclusion** questions, we expect LSAT to avoid putting the conclusion in its most obvious place (the final claim). We also expect them to avoid using obvious conclusion indicators (like therefore, thus, hence, so).

Instead, we expect the conclusion to usually either be the **first claim** or be some **rebuttal**, typically introduced with Pivot words like but, however, yet.

Rebuttal conclusions usually have some sort of pronoun or referential language -- e.g. "But _this plan_ would be likely to fail", "However _her conclusion_ is misguided". We need to replace those pronouns / referential ideas in a conclusion with the actual ideas they may back to.

## 8. Describing Arguments (set 1)

### Metadata

```yaml

source_tests:
  - PT 72
difficulty_level: 1-5
question_type:
  - Role
  - Main Conclusion
question_feature: Describing Arguments
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column



# Role and Main Conclusion

These questions frequently "hide" the conclusion by making it the **very first claim**, or by making it some **rebuttal claim**.

**Opinion** indicators (should, probably, ought to) and **Pivot** indicators (but, yet, however) are very helpful for understanding which claims we should assign to which roles in the argument.

**Arguments** need a claim we call the **Conclusion**, and some **Support** for that claim, which we call the evidence or the premise(s).

Some of those supporting premises can themselves also be considered conclusions, if they are supported by at least one claim. Another name for one of these _supported premises_ is an Intermediate Conclusion, or a Subsidiary Conclusion.

Any time you're asking yourself, "Is this claim a Conclusion?", you should ask yourself "_why should I believe this claim?_" If it's really a Conclusion, then you'll be able to point to at least one claim that sounds like a reason why you should believe that claim.

If a claim **has** support, we can call it a Conclusion.  
If it **provides** support, we can call it a Premise.  
If it **has** support and also **provides** support, an Intermediate Conclusion.

#### Right Column



Length -- **5 questions, 8 mins**  
Types -- Role and Main Conclusion  
Tests -- 72  
Levels -- 1, 2

- Try to answer all 5 questions in 9 minutes.
- Flag a problem every time you feel less confident in your answer or feel like you struggled to get there.
- Click on the answer you're selecting, but don't hit Confirm.
- When the timer goes off, flip back through the questions and take a second look, hit confirm, and check out the Explanation (if there is one).

## 9. Assumption Family (Easier) Set 4

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Assumption Family (Easier) Set 4
category: Process
estimated_time: 56 mins
question_count: 6
difficulty_level: not visible
question_type:
  - Sufficient Assumption
  - Necessary Assumption
  - Principle
  - Flaw
  - Strengthen
  - Weaken
  - Evaluate
question_feature: Idea Math vs Courtroom Debate
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

In the **Assumption Family**, we're usually doing one of two things:

**Internal Logic** reading arguments for the sake of noticing missing facts or missing links in the internal logic of the argument / looking for conditional rules in the answer choices, or evaluating whether a conditional rule presented in the evidence is being clearly triggered. When we're Weakening the idea math of an argument, we're showing how the Evidence is compatible with the Anti-Conclusion.

**Courtroom Debate** reading arguments and then thinking, "**GIVEN THAT this evidence is true, HOW COULD WE STILL ARGUE the conclusion is wrong?**" What new/external considerations haven't yet been considered? What possible objections could we make? Could there be some alternate explanation for a curious fact? Could there be some crucial difference between two things being treated similarly? Is there something sketchy about the author's evidence or her reasoning?

How do we know which one we're doing? We don't, really.

**Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption**, and **Principle** skew way more towards Internal Logic.

**Flaw, Strengthen, Weaken**, and **Evaluate** are more likely to test our ability to think of potential objections like we're engaged in Courtroom Debate.

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions, untimed.

Shift your thinking from **Idea Math** to **Courtroom Debate**, based on the question type.

When you're trying **Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption**, or **Principle** questions, think to yourself that when you combine the correct answer with the existing premises you can derive, or come much closer to deriving, the conclusion.

If the conclusion is trying to prove that "A is Z", then we need two basic things:

- a rule that allows us to derive Z, like "If X, then Z".
- info telling us that A matches that trigger, like "A is X".

When you're trying **Flaw, Strengthen, Weaken**, or **Evaluate**, try to respond to the argument as though you're Opposing Counsel. Even though you accept that the Prosecution's evidence is true, how could you make it seem irrelevant, less trustworthy, or less important than some other consideration.

Think about the argument in one (or more) of these ways.

- GIVEN [this evidence],  
  HOW COULD I ARGUE [the anti-conclusion] ?

- EVEN THOUGH [evidence] is true,  
  [anti-conclusion] could still be true, BECAUSE ...

- GIVEN THAT [I'm arguing the anti-conclusion],  
  HOW CAN I RESPOND TO [the author's evidence / logic]

The LR question types that most often would prompt us to consider diagramming the Conditional Logic are

- Most Supported

levels 2 and 3

## 13. Weaken + Comparison

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Weaken + Comparison
category: Process
estimated_time: 50 mins
question_count: 6
difficulty_level: not visible
question_type: Weaken
reasoning_type: Comparison
question_feature: Weaken + Comparison
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Weaken** questions intersect with comparisons in a handful of common ways:

- If the author is concluding some sort of _Plan / Recommendation_, then we end up comparing our current world to the world in which we follow the author's advice. Since she is telling us this advice will work out _better_, either overall or in some specific sense, our job in being skeptical is to think about _what else_ could be different in the world where we follow the author's advice, that could go against the result the author promised.

- If the author is comparing two things and seeming to favor one over the other, then we need to consider a full comparison of all the advantages and disadvantages of each thing (_Net Gain / Loss_). If the author concludes that X is better than Y, then we can weaken with bad stuff about X or good stuff about Y.

- If the author is assuming that "what was true in the past will be true in the present" or assuming that "what is currently true will be true in the future", then we can weaken those arguments by looking for something important that's different between the past and the present or between the present trends and the future.

- If the author is trying to explain a difference between two groups / situations, then the test will often have a correct answer that presents some **other difference** between the two things that could be an Alternate Explanation.

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 problems, untimed

Focus on whether the problem seems to be dealing with

- a plan / recommendation
- preferring one option over another
- assuming similarities between different timeframes
- offering a causal explanation for a difference between two groups or situations

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Analogies** are two separate cases that share some trait or relationship.

# Fill in the blank

_"Baseball is to the World Series,  
what (American) football is to ______."_

The answer is _Superbowl_ because World Series has the same relationship to baseball (season-ending championship) as Superbowl has to football. They're at least partly analogous.

Arguments by analogy tend to think, "Because X and Y are similar when it comes to Thing A, they're probably similar when it comes to Thing B".

_Thelma and Rodney both like rap from the 90s. I just made this Spotify playlist of 90s rap. Thelma really likes it. Thus, Rodney will really like it too._

The author has established one similarity "both like 90s rap" and is assuming that Thelma and Rodney will also be similar in terms of a second trait, "liking my Spotify playlist".

Necessary Assumption / Strengthen / Principle / Most Supported questions will want us to **Complete the Analogy**.

Flaw and Weaken will want us to challenge this analogy by pointing to a potentially **crucial difference** (like maybe Thelma only likes east coast rap, Rodney's only likes west coast, and my playlist totally leaves out west coast rap like Dre, Snoop, Pac, NWA, etc.)

thinking through the **analogies**.

- What are the two separate cases?
- What is the common principle?
- What language cues exist to telegraph that the author is making a Comparison / Analogy?

When people start dating each other, it often turns one person off if the other person seems too enthusiastic. Similarly, when students are shopping around for an LSAT tutor, ______.

How would we **carry through** a trait or relationship from the dating situation to the LSAT tutor situation? Something like,

_students are likely to be more attracted to tutors who don't seem desperate for their business._
