### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Focus on finding **Conditional Indicator** words and using them as a way to organize how you write the If / Then rule.

Write or think through the contrapositive as well.

Beware illegal Reversals or Negations.

_Conditional Logic, by type_

- **Principle** (the left side of the Principle matches the Evidence, the right side matches the Conclusion)

- **Parallel** (diagramming these arguments with "algebraic recipes" allows us to be more picky and decisive when evaluating answers)

- **Assumption** (the conclusion gives us an A --> Z logic path we want to prove, and the evidence + the correct answer choice assemble together to create that A --> Z path / or the evidence gives us a conditional rule that is being used in drawing the conclusion, but we still need an answer choice to tell us something in order to clearly trigger that rule.)

- **Must Be True / Most Supported** (multiple conditionals often chain together, and provided facts often match the contrapositive-trigger of a conditional and allow us to infer the contrapositive-outcome)

- **Flaw / Parallel Flaw** (Flaw + Conditional Logic usually means the **Necessary vs. Sufficient** flaw, which is using a rule in an illegal Backwards or Opposite fashion. Sometimes it involves messing up Or / And stuff)

#### Right Column

# Try these 7 questions in 12 minutes

(1.5x = 18, 2x = 24)

Focus on getting an answer to all of them within the time limit, but then as you review the set, indulge in more untimed diagramming, if useful.

# CONDITIONAL KEYWORDS

- Put on **Left** (sufficient conditions)  
  _if, when, any, each, all, every, the only, whenever, whoever, X's_

- Put it on the **Right** (necessary conditions)  
  **only if**, only, must, requires, ensures, will, always, implies, presupposes, depends on, guarantees,

- Put negated version of either idea on left (**If-Not**)  
  **unless**, without, until, except

- **Negative to Positive** conversions  
  No A's are B = All A's are ~B  
  Not all A's are B = Some A's are ~B  
  Few A's are B = Most A's are ~B

```yaml
start_button_label: Continue
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

If the question is testing us on what can be inferred, scan primarily for **conditional** strength quantifiers (_all / each / every / any / no / none_, or anything else that's conditional).

If we find a conditional, then we look to see whether the trigger of that conditional (or the trigger of the contrapositive) shows up in any other claim.

If so, then we can combine those claims to derive at least **Some** overlap between the two other traits.

_If we know_ Some X's are B  
_and we know_ All B's are ~A  
_we can derive_ Some X's are ~A

_If we know_ Most X's are B  
_and we know_ All B's are ~A  
_we can derive_ Most X's are ~A

_but if we know_ Most B's are X  
_and we know_ All B's are ~A  
_we can only derive_ Some X's are ~A

The key idea is that in order to make an inference, the **trigger** of the conditional needs to show up in another claim. If the **outcome** is the term that shows up elsewhere, we can't infer anything (but that's where a lot LSAT authors' flawed inferences come from).

In the third example, the overlapping term is the trigger in both claims, so we can't "chain" the ideas together. But we still know for sure that there are some X's who have the property of being B, and thus they also must have the property of being ~A.

Sometimes, we can infer something from two **Most** claims.

GIVEN:  
Most A's are B  
+ Most A's are C

Most A's are B  
+ Most B's are ~A

INFER:  
Some B's are C  
There are more B's than A's

Then there's a famous **incorrect** inference from two Most claims:

GIVEN:  
Most A's are B  
+ Most B's are C

INFER:  
nothing (but LSAT authors think, "Most A's are C")

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 questions, untimed.

If you see two or more **most** claims, assess whether you can infer anything by combining them (the two most claims should be about the same group).

Otherwise, your number one priority is always question is testing us on what can be inferred, scan primarily for **conditional** strength quantifiers (_all / each / every / any / no / none_, or anything else that's conditional).

If we find a conditional, then we look to see whether the trigger of that conditional (or the trigger of the contrapositive) shows up in any other claim.

## 22. Explain Curious Fact (Harder) Set 1

### Metadata

```yaml
question_type: Explain Curious Fact

question_feature: Explain Curious Fact
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Back again for more fun with **Explain a Curious Fact**, in which the evidence will provide some _Curious Fact_ (almost always a comparison).

The author is implicitly asking herself, "Wussup with this Curious Fact?", and her conclusion either states or implies some causal explanation.

We evaluate these arguments by thinking about:

- **Alternate Explanations** for this curious fact
- the **plausibility** of the Author's Explanation

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 problems, untimed

**(1) Locate the _Curious Fact_ in the evidence, and ask the curious question.**

EXAMPLE: "People who run marathons are more likely than those who don't to be in excellent health. Clearly the mental discipline it takes to run a marathon also allows people to make better dietary and lifestyle choices."

The first sentence is a correlation, a Curious Comparison (ppl who are X are more likely than those who aren't to be Y). The author is _asking_ herself: **Hmm - why is is that** there's this correlation between marathon running and excellent health?

**(2) Spell out the author's explanation for the curious fact, because the author is often conveying a lot of implicit ideas within their conclusion:**

_People who run marathons have good mental discipline. This mental discipline helps them not over-eat, not over-indulge in alcohol or smoking. By not doing those things, they end up in excellent health._

**(3) Think about _Alternate Explanations_ for the Curious Fact.**

Consider _Reverse Causality_ (maybe being in excellent health lets you run a marathon) or _Third Factor_ (maybe being wealthy both enables you to have time to practice/run a marathon and also enables you to get better nutrition, exercise, and medical care)

**(4) Think about what could help us assess the _Plausibility_ of the Author's Explanation.**

Covariation answers (No Cause, No Effect / Effect without Cause / Cause without Effect), or Presence / Motive / Method ideas (Suspect was at the scene of the crime / suspect had incentive to do it / here's how the suspect could have done it).

```yaml
question_feature: EXCEPT
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

A common misconception about **EXCEPT** questions is that the correct answer will be the **opposite** of what the other four do. Like if we were doing a Strengthen-EXCEPT question, a lot of times students are looking for "the one that Weakens".

But even though that will be true sometimes, the task is only asking us to pick something that "**doesn't** Strengthen", so the correct answer will either Weaken or (more commonly) will be Neutral with no impact.

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 Except questions, untimed

EXCEPT most commonly occurs on Strengthen, Weaken, and Paradox, but this set has some oddballs like Flaw-EXCEPT, Must Be True-EXCEPT, and Could Be True-EXCEPT.

That last one is really just a **Must Be False** question, not an EXCEPT variation on a normal question type, but we like throwing it in here to juxtapose how doing Must Be False is **not** like doing an EXCEPT question.

On genuine EXCEPT questions, we really don't worry about why the correct answer is correct (it might be irrelevant / out of scope, or it might do the reverse of what the question stem asks for). Instead, we worry about eliminating the four answers that _do_ perform the function indicated by the question stem.

But on a **Must Be False** question, we don't worry about the four wrong answers. They're all wrong because we can't contradict them. Whether they make an illegal inference or just say something we can't support, who cares. We're looking to the one correct answer that we _can_ contradict.
