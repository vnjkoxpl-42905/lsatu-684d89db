### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Within the world of Inference (**Most Supported / Must Be True**), we are trying to combine 2 or more ideas together in order to derive some synthesis of ideas.

We often can't predict what the correct answer will say, but we often can, if we pay attention to certain "clue words":

- conditional indicators (if, only if, unless, requires, etc.)
- causal relationships (because of this, due to this, this allows, this makes possible, as a result, this leads to, etc.)
- quantifiers (most, some, all, no)
- other mathematical ideas (percents, fractions, numbers, ratios)
- pivots (but, yet, however)

The pattern we call _Reconcile the Pivot_ is when the last claim begins with a Pivot word. That usually creates a tension with some idea(s) that came before it, and the correct answer almost always resolves or expresses that tension.

#### Right Column

# Try these 9 problems, untimed

- When you hit the pivot word (but / yet / however), think about how that new claim creates some tension with what came before or has some relevance to it.

- Try to mentally articulate what the net effect is of combining that pivot idea with what came before. Expect the answer to deal with whatever it is you're thinking in that moment.

- Since these are mainly Most Supported questions, remember that _Strong Language_ in the answer choices is a big red flag. We want the most provable answer, so weaker claims (with a lower burden of proof) are more attractive.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

# Causal Chains

popular on **Most Supported, Must Be True,** and **Necessary Assumption**

A causal chain is three or more things causally linked:  
_Reading gives Jorge a headache, which makes him take a nap._

The test wants to make sure we see the implicit connection that  
"Reading leads to taking a nap".

We might note causal chains on our paper like  
**Reading → Headache → Take Nap**

This isn't the same as conditional logic, so we need to understand that these are "causal" arrows we're showing ourselves. With both causal chains and conditional chains, it's illegal to go backwards.

You wouldn't say that "if **Nap**, then **Headache**, then **Reading**"

But here's the big difference: with causal chains it's plausible to say "if the first thing doesn't happen, the last thing won't happen".

On a Most Supported task, we can feel pretty safe about ideas like  
(A) If Jorge did less reading during the day, he would take fewer naps.

# VOCAB

given a causal chain, like **X → Y → Z**  
we can say that  
X **directly** causes Y.  
X **indirectly** causes Z.

#### Right Column

# Try these 9 questions untimed.

Work on building these causal chains,  
before you look at answers.

Causal chains within **Necessary Assumption** can be hard to understand because you're missing parts of the chain (the author is assuming them).

EXAMPLE  
_B causes C. So, I guess A causes D._

That's very confusing, because there are no repeating ideas!

EXAMPLE  
_Waking up earlier makes people be more productive at work in the morning. So, I guess getting a new dog might lead to a promotion._

Be patient trying to find the right causal sequence. Put **any** cause / effect relationship you can find on the page, with space on your page to both the left and the right of that idea.

_earlier wake → more prod morn_

Now, go seek out something that has to do with causing someone to "wake up earlier", or find something that could be caused by "having a more productive morning".

In the author's mind, we have this causal sequence

_get dog → wake up earlier → more productive morn → promotion_

**ASSUMED** / **PREMISE** / **ASSUMED**

The middle idea was stated. The other two are assumed. If you go from beginning of the chain to the end, you get the author's conclusion, "get dog → promotion".

Remember, on **Most Supported**, if you know that A leads to B leads to C, it's pretty fair to say "if you cut back on A, you'll get less C".

But watch out for switches between _factual claims_ and _opinionated claims!_

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Causal Difference-Maker** is a fuzzy term we mainly on Most Supported questions. A ton of correct answers on Most Supported reward us for understanding some cause/effect relationship we were provided with in the stimulus. Here are the most common variations of how it crops up.

## One Difference Explains Another Difference

_Tim and Jill were both struggling with games. Jill, unlike Tim, decided to try meditating shortly after practicing each logic game. Jill no longer struggles with games, though Tim still does._

This seems to be suggesting that meditation was a Causal Difference-Maker when it comes to Jill getting better at games. We certainly can't prove that, but if you've been told about Difference X (Jill does meditation, Tim doesn't) then it's supportable to say it might be the reason for Difference Y (Jill no longer struggles with games).

## Causal Connector Language

An individual claim can present a cause / effect connection by using a causal verb:

- exercising **burns** calories
- smoking **reduces** your life span
- practicing LSAT **makes** you better at the test

But you can also create cause / effect connections by connecting two claims with causal language:

- Jen is the only blonde in her family. **Because of this**, she gets picked on more by stand-up comedians. (_hair color can affect likelihood of being singled out by a performer_)
- Carl wakes up at 430am every morning. **This allows** him to get to work in less time than if we were to wake later. (_the time you leave for work can affect how long it takes you to get to work_)

## Apply the Causality

Sometimes we provided with a cause/effect connection and then expected to apply that connection to a specific situation:

Performing an awkward physical task, such as brushing your teeth wrong-handed, triggers a state of neural plasticity, which makes learning easier. Since Jamal is trying to learn LSAT as efficiently as possible, he should ______.

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions untimed

- Look for causal verbs, like "_promotes, inhibits, increases, lowers, contributes to, influences_". Look for ", -ing" constructions that convey causation, such as "Sarah yelled at the top of her lungs, shaking the vase on the shelf".

- Look for causal connector language that tells you that the previous sentence has a causal relationship to the present sentence, like "because of this, due to this, this allows, this makes possible, accordingly, that is why".

- Look for one Difference that could potentially explain another Difference.

- Look for someone to say _what really matters_, what the key concern is when making a judgment, a decision, a classification.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

A lot of missing links and inferences rely on **common terms**.

Consider this argument:  
_Rex's painting uses bold colors. Artists are usually hiding the fact that their painting expresses no deep ideas if they use bold colors. Thus, Rex is probably ashamed of his painting._

Several ideas were mentioned twice:

- Rex's painting
- bold colors

There was also a frequency modifier mentioned twice:

- usually / probably

A couple ideas were mentioned only once:

- hiding the fact that the painting expresses no deep ideas
- ashamed

The missing link in this argument is,  
"If an artist is hiding that their painting expresses no deep ideas, then they will be ashamed".

When we're searching for Missing Links, a good rule of thumb (though of course there are always exceptions) is **Mentioned Twice? That'll Suffice.**

What that stupid slogan means is that the missing link is between buzzwords or phrases that only showed up once. The stuff that's said twice isn't going to be in our correct answer.

#### Right Column

# Try these 7 problems, untimed.

- Consider using the notepad window to cover up the answers, until you've typed a prediction.

- You can also try using the highlighter and underline tools to mark off things that are mentioned twice. If you see "Rex's painting" mentioned twice in the paragraph, grab a highlighter and color both of those the same color. Once you've color-coded all the stuff mentioned twice, you know that the missing link exists between what remains.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

# The three main Reasoning Structures

**Conditional Logic** - Correct answers address the author's internal logic

- assumed links between ideas
- if/then rules
- a missing fact needed to trigger a rule
- valid and invalid conditional inferences

**Comparisons** - Correct answers address assumed similarities or differences between two things:

- part vs. whole
- sample vs. general population
- then vs. now
- this situation vs. that situation
- this group vs. that group

Or they address the net gain / loss of a comparison:

- doing this thing vs. not doing this thing
- this thing vs. that thing
- weighing the positives vs. the negatives

**Causation** - Correct answers address one of two pressure points:

- alternate explanations for some Curious Comparison
- the plausibility of the author's explanation for it

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions, untimed.

When we're using **Conditional Logic** to "prove an argument", the **Left** side should be triggered by facts we know from the Evidence (or Answer Choice), and the **Right** side of the rule should match the Conclusion's language. If we want to weaken an argument's internal logic, then we're showing how the Left side is compatible with the **opposite** of the Right side (the Premise is compatible with the Anti-Conclusion).

If it feels like you're dealing with a **Comparison**, think about weakening with potential differences or strengthening by affirming some similarity. If you're dealing with a Net Gain / Loss type of Comparison, think about weakening with potential downsides or strengthening with "equal or better" ideas.

If the author is interpreting the **Causal** significance of some Curious Comparison (a weird asymmetry exists, or a study that has some interesting data), take time to understand the Author's Explanation, but then consider Alternate Explanations for the same curious fact (is the author confusing cause and effect? is the author failing to recognize that two things are linked to some third factor?)

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Try these five questions that test us on the presence (or absence) of _Intermediate Conclusions_.

**Role** and **Main Conclusion** questions usually "hide" the conclusion by making it the **very first claim**, or by making it some **rebuttal claim**.

They will also sometimes complicate our assessment of the argument's structure by throwing in multiples conclusions.

Arguments need a claim we call the Conclusion, and some Support for that claim, which we call the evidence or the premise(s).

Some of those supporting premises can _themselves_ also be considered conclusions, if _they_ are supported by at least one claim. The more common name for a _supported premise_ is an Intermediate Conclusion, or a Subsidiary Conclusion.

Any time you're asking yourself, "Is this claim a Conclusion?", you should ask yourself "_why should I believe this claim? did the author provide a supporting reason?_" If it's really a Conclusion, then you'll be able to point to at least one claim that sounds like a reason offered for why we should believe that claim.

If a claim **has** support, we can call it a Conclusion.  
If it **provides** support, we can call it a Premise.  
If it **has** support and also **provides** support, an Intermediate Conclusion.

In the Assumption / Objection family, when an argument has this three part structure,  
_Premise → Intermediate Conclusion → Main Conclusion_  
then the correct answer might deal with the connection between Premise and Intermediate Conclusion, or it might deal with the connection between the Intermediate and the Main Conclusion. Hence, we sort of have twice as much to worry about. We need to think about what assumptions are being made with each move, or what objections we might make.

#### Right Column

# Try these problems, untimed

think through the following:

How can we tell whether there's an Intermediate Conclusion?

Are there Support / Conclusion / Opinion indicators that help us show us when there is or isn't an Intermediate Conclusion?

Practice testing whether a given claim X is or isn't a conclusion by asking yourself, "**Why should I believe X?**" If the author offers a reason, then we can call it a conclusion. If she doesn't, then it's just an unsupported claim.
