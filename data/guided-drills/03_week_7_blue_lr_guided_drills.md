## 2. Complete the Analogy (set 3)

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Complete the Analogy (set 3)
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 1 hr
question_count: 8
question_type:
  - Fill in the Blank
  - Method
  - Assumption Family
question_feature: Complete the Analogy
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Analogies** are two separate cases that share some trait or relationship.

f.e. - "_a peak is to a mountain, what a scalp is to a _____._"

One answer would be a _human_, peak is the top of a mountain and scalp is the top of a human. These two separate cases share some relationship, so they are analogous in at least one respect.

Analogies show up a lot on **Method** of reasoning questions, which ask us to characterize the type of evidence or type of move the author used.

When we see a **fill-in-the-blank** question (aka Logical Completion), the paragraph is leading us to some final idea, and they frequently use a **Complete the Analogy** type structure, as I did at the top of this page.

When we see analogies show up in the **Assumption Family**, they tend to go like this: "Because X and Y are similar when it comes to Thing A, they're probably also similar when it comes to Thing B".

_Five years ago, my family went to Lake Crocus and I didn't have any fun. Thus, I will not have any fun when my family goes to visit Lake Crocus this summer._

The author has established one similarity "both vacations are at the same location" and is assuming a second similarity, "both vacations will be not-fun".

Flaw and Weaken will want us to challenge this analogy by pointing to a potentially **meaningful difference**. Necessary Assumption will _rule out_ salient differences, and Strengthen will add more similarity, make them seem more Fair to Compare.

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 questions untimed,

thinking through the **analogies**.

- What are the two separate cases?
- What is the common principle?
- What language cues exist to telegraph that the author is making a Comparison / Analogy?
- Do the two cases seem "fair to compare" or do you think there could be an important difference between the two cases that calls into question the implied similarity.

# EXAMPLE

When people go to casinos, they gamble with their money, spending as much as they can stand to lose, hoping for the excitement of a jackpot. Similarly, when a fisherman goes out on Lake Crocus for the better part of the day, hoping for the excitement of a big catch, ______.

How would we **carry through** a trait or relationship from the dating situation to the LSAT tutor situation? Something like,  
_these fisherman are gambling with their time, spending as much of it as they can stand to lose_

## 3. Assumes Similarity (Mini-Set)

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Assumes Similarity (Mini-Set)
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 40 mins
question_count: 5
reasoning_type: Comparison
question_feature: Assumes Similarity
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column



## Every comparison hides an assumed sameness

In the world of **Comparisons**, authors lean on a few recurring assumptions:
- What was true in the **past** is still true **today**.
- What is true **today** will be true in the **future**.
- What was true for **this** group, person, or thing is true for **that** one.

Whenever an argument reasons across time or across cases, it is assuming the two sides are similar enough to carry the conclusion.

That gives you both attack and support:
- **Weaken** by pointing out a salient difference between the two sides.
- **Strengthen** by adding a similarity or affirming the assumed one.

#### Right Column



## Try these 5, untimed

Go for accuracy and pattern recognition, not speed.

For each argument:
- Name the comparison. What two things, groups, or moments is the author treating as alike?
- Find where that sameness is assumed rather than proven.
- Prephrase the gap: "this only works if the two sides are similar in the way that matters."

Then judge the answers:
- A weakener surfaces a **difference** that breaks the link.
- A strengthener shores up the **similarity**.
- Wrong answers often raise a difference that does not matter to the conclusion, or restate the comparison without testing it.

## 6. Causal Chains (Mini Set)

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Causal Chains (Mini Set)
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 36 mins
question_count: 4
question_type:
  - Most Supported
  - Must Be True
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Causal Chains
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

# Causal Chains

appear most in **Most Supported** and **Must Be True**.

A causal chain is three or more things causally linked: _Reading gives Jorge a headache. Headaches make Jorge lie down._

The test wants to make sure we see the implicit connection that "Reading leads to laying down".

_Reading → Headache → Lay Down_

In many cases, causal chains overlap with conditional logic. If we see conditional logic wording, like **"if, when, only, only if, unless, requires, always, will"**, then we can choose to think of it conditionally or causally, same difference.

But on **Most Supported** questions, there will be looser language that still indicates a causal flow: _Doing X **promotes** Y, which **increases** the likelihood of Z being true. And Z is a **factor** in the formation of A._

The test wants to make sure we see the implicit connection that "Doing X can increase your chance of A's forming".

#### Right Column

# Try these 4 questions untimed

Work on building these causal chains,  
before you look at answers

Be patient trying to find the right causal sequence. Write **any** cause / effect relationship you can find on the page, with space on your page to both the left and the right of that idea, e.g.

_reads too much → gets a headache_

Now, go seek out something that has to do with causing someone to "read a lot", or find something that could be caused by "having a headache".

On **Most Supported** questions, if you have a causal chain, A → B → C, is it okay to say "if no A, then no C"?

Yes, basically! It feels _naughty_ because that would be an illegal negation in the world of conditional logic, but in the world of Causality, you'll find LSAT being cool with the common sense plausibility of "if cause didn't happen, effect wouldn't have happened".

If I say, "**Because** I took Franklin St to the party and there ended up being an accident there, I was late to the party, which **led** to my missing the opening of gifts." Then it's plausible to say, "if had **not taken** Franklin St., I probably would **not have** missed the opening of gifts".

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

A correct answer on **Evaluate** asks a relevant question. How do we know if it's relevant? We know because if we answered the question one way, we would Strengthen the argument. If we answered it the opposite way, we would Weaken the argument.

The more important one, though, is the potential to weaken. So, if you want you can just listen for the weakening potential. Think to yourself, "Which of these questions could I answer in a way that would most Weaken the argument?"

# Wrong Answers

**No Impact** - they pose a question that we could answer any way we'd like and it wouldn't affect the argument.

**Too Specific / Strong** - they're requesting an exact figure or measurement or posing an extreme question that wouldn't Weaken if the answer were 'no'.

#### Right Column

# Try these 7 questions, untimed.

Each answer choice poses a question that could be answered in at least 2 different ways. We want one of those answers to _weaken_ the argument. So don't pick an answer choice unless you hear it asking a question that could be answered in a way that would weaken the argument.

In general, expect Evaluate arguments to deal with dubious _comparisons_ and authors who are trying to offer _causal_ explanations for some curious fact.

Try to use your Weaken mindset to evaluate the argument. Think:

_Given_ this evidence  
_How can I argue_ the anti-conclusion?

## 9. Intermediate Conclusions Set 2

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Intermediate Conclusions Set 2
category: Process
estimated_time: 40 mins
question_count: 5
question_type:
  - Role
  - Main Conclusion
question_feature: Intermediate Conclusions
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Try these five questions that test us on the presence (or absence) of _Intermediate Conclusions_.

**Role** and **Main Conclusion** questions usually "hide" the conclusion by making it the **very first claim**, or by making it some **rebuttal claim**.

They will also sometimes complicate our assessment of the argument's structure by throwing in multiples conclusions.

Arguments need a claim we call the Conclusion, and some Support for that claim, which we call the evidence or the premise(s).

Some of those supporting premises can _themselves_ also be considered conclusions, if _they_ are supported by at least one claim. The more common name for a _supported premise_ is an Intermediate Conclusion, or a Subsidiary Conclusion.

Any time you're asking yourself, "Is this claim a Conclusion?", you should ask yourself "_why should I believe this claim? did the author provide a supporting reason?_" If it's really a Conclusion, then you'll be able to point to at least one claim that sounds like a reason offered for why we should believe that claim.

If a claim **has** support, we can call it a Conclusion.  
If it **provides** support, we can call it a Premise.  
If it **has** support and also **provides** support, an Intermediate Conclusion.

#### Right Column

# Try these problems, untimed

think through the following:

- How can we tell whether there's an Intermediate Conclusion?

- Are there Support / Conclusion / Opinion indicators that help show us when there is or isn't an Intermediate Conclusion?

- Practice testing whether a given claim X is or isn't a conclusion by asking yourself, "_Why should I believe X?_" If the author offers a reason, then we can call it a conclusion. If she doesn't, then it's just an unsupported claim.

## 11. Causal Chains - Set 3

### Metadata

```yaml
title: Causal Chains - Set 3
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 1 hr
question_count: 8
question_type:
  - Assumption
  - Inference
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Causal Chains
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

# Causal Chains

show up mostly in **Assumption** and **Inference** questions

given this:  
_Reading for a long time gives Jorge a headache.  
Headaches make Jorge lie down._

we might note it like this:  
**Read long time → Headache → Lie Down**

As with conditional logic, we're not allowed to say, "I just saw Jorge laying down. He must have been reading a bunch earlier today". That's inferring that one possible cause of Jorge's laying down is the only one we need to consider.

But we can say stuff like, "This afternoon, Jorge needs to be alert and active. So he shouldn't read a long time after lunch." This is sort of like a contrapositive: _If you don't want the Effect, don't do that Cause_

An argument can use that structure to create flaws or gaps:  
_Reading for a long time gives Jorge a headache. Headaches make Jorge lie down. Thus if Jorge needs to stay alert and active all day, he should not read anything._

Whoa, whoa, there author! I understand you're saying, "if he wants to avoid the Effect of laying down, he better not do the Cause (i.e. reading for a long time)". But you said, "he better not read _anything_".

_Reading for a long time gives Jorge a headache. Headaches make Jorge lie down. Thus if Jorge needs to stay alert and active all day, he should not Pam's essay._

Whoa, whoa, there author! I understand you're saying, "if he wants to avoid the Effect of laying down, he better not do the Cause (i.e. reading for a long time)". But who said that reading Pam's essay means that he'd be reading for a long time? You must be assuming that, "It would take a long time to read Pam's essay".

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 questions untimed.

Work on understanding the causal chain,  
before you look at answers

On Assumption questions, causal chain arguments might create a **missing link**.

EXAMPLE: _Stress causes Anton to sweat. So, Anton probably gets sweaty when he plays chess._

This argument is assuming a missing link between playing chess and being stressed.

**CONCLUSION**  
play chess ---------------> sweat

**SUPPORT**  
play chess → stress → sweat  
**ASSUMED** / **PREMISE**

Causal chain arguments might create a **missing fact**.

EXAMPLE: _Playing chess gives Anton stress, and he tends to get sweaty when he's stressed. Thus, he will probably be sweating tonight._

This argument is assuming that Anton will be playing chess tonight. It set up a causal chain and seems to be expecting the effect we get at the end of the chain, but it never triggered the causal chain.

When you're trying to organize a causal chain on your paper, be patient trying to find the right causal sequence. Put **any** cause / effect relationship you can find on the page, with space on your page to both the left and the right of that idea. Then, go seek out something that has to do with causing the left side, or something that could be caused by the right side.

Remember, on **Most Supported**, if you know that A leads to B, which leads to C, it's pretty fair to say "if you cut back on A, you'll get less C".
