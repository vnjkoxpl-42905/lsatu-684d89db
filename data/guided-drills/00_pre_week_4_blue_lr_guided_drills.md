```yaml
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Within the world of Inference (**Most Supported / Must Be True**), we are trying to combine 2 or more ideas together in order to derive some synthesis of ideas.

We often can't predict what the correct answer will say, but we often can, if we pay attention to certain "clue words":

- conditional indicators (if, only if, unless, requires, etc.)
- causal relationships (because of this, due to this, this allows, this makes possible, as a result, this leads to, etc.)
- quantifiers (most, some, all, no)
- other mathematical ideas (percents, fractions, numbers, ratios)
- pivots (but, yet, however)

The pattern we call _Reconcile the Pivot_ is when the last claim begins with a Pivot word. That provides a strong clue that we're supposed to reconcile that final fact with what came before it.

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 problems in 8 mins

1.5x: 12 mins / 2x: 16 mins

- When you hit the pivot word (but / yet / however), think about how that new claim creates some tension with what came before or how it's relevant to the preceding ideas.

- Try to mentally articulate what the net effect is of combining that pivot idea with what came before. Expect the answer to deal with whatever it is you're thinking in that moment.

- Since these are Most Supported questions, remember that _Strong Language_ in the answer choices is a big red flag. We want the most provable answer, so weaker claims (with a lower burden of proof) are more attractive.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

# Parallel and Paradox

go together like ketchup and soup

Which is to say, .... _not at all!_

We're purposefully mixing oil and water in this question set to remind our brain of how it feels on a real Logical Reasoning section when you have to totally take off one brain and put on another one, as you switch between some question types.

Certainly, there's not much switching cost involved when you switch within a family.

Reading a stimulus for Strengthen, Weaken, Evaluate, Flaw, and Necessary Assumption will feel pretty similar. Reading an Agree / Disagree and a Most Supported feel somewhat the same.

But Parallel and Paradox are pretty darn far apart. Why?

- What are we reading in each case. Is the stimulus an argument or not? Is it likely to have conditional logic or not?

- How are we judging answers: based on whether they can be inferred? based on whether they would have the desired impact, if true? based on whether we can match them up with the stimulus?

#### Right Column

# Try these 6 questions in 9 minutes

(1.5x = 14 mins, 2x = 18 mins)  
Parallel tends to take longer than Paradox, so pace accordingly

## PARADOX

- We're not reading an argument.
- The question stem will tell us what surprising idea we need to explain, or the final sentence of the stimulus will use a pivot word like "but, yet, however" to show us the surprising idea
- Figure out what background fact, if any, makes this idea a surprise. Frame your thinking like this:

_Given that .. background fact(s),_  
_How can we explain ... surprising claim?_

## PARALLEL

- We are reading an argument.
- Our job is to see the structure or reasoning pattern behind the argument.
- If it's possible to describe the reasoning pattern conversationally (e.g. "The author is arguing that if the extra effort has diminishing returns, it ain't worth it") go for it.
- But if we see _Conditionals, Quantifiers, or Repeating Ideas_, then it's usually better to create a little algebraic recipe for the argument.
- Once you have a recipe for the argument, articulate the _first_ ingredient you plan to look for. Are you looking to see if there are two "most" claims? Are you checking to see whether the Conclusion has the same _probably_ strength as the original? Are you seeing whether there are two conditional premises that chain together?
- Be suspicious of answers that have similar topics or other cheap superficial similarities.
