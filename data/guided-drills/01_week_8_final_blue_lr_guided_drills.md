### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column



## The flaw is usually one of the famous ten

Medium Flaw questions reward pattern recognition. The author makes a reasoning move that breaks in a predictable way, and the LSAT reuses the same handful of breaks again and again. We call these the **Famous Flaws**.

The ten that show up most:
- **Necessary vs. Sufficient** (a rule used backwards or negated)
- **Causation** (correlation treated as cause, reversed cause, third factor)
- **Sampling** (an unrepresentative group)
- **Equivocation** (one word, two meanings)
- **Percent vs. Amount** (a rate confused for a count)
- **Ad Hominem** (attacking the source, not the claim)
- **Circular** (the conclusion smuggled into the evidence)
- **Whole vs. Part**
- **Appeal to Authority / Popularity**
- **False Choice** (only two options when more exist)

At the medium level the trap answers also describe famous flaws. A wrong answer names a real flaw that this argument simply never made.

#### Right Column



## Try these 5, then time them

Set a timer for **8 minutes** (1.5x = 12, 2x = 16). Get an answer to all five, and flag any that felt like a low-confidence guess.

For each argument:
- Find the conclusion, then ask what the author had to assume to get there.
- Name the broken move in your own words before you read the answers.
- Match your prephrase to the famous-flaw list.

When you read the answers:
- A choice can describe a real flaw and still be wrong because the argument never made it. Ask, did this argument actually do this?
- Some correct answers state the flaw in plain, specific language, not the textbook phrasing. Do not wait for the classic wording.

After the timer, take a slow untimed second pass before you confirm. Note any flaw you keep missing.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Focus on finding **Conditional Indicator** words and using them as a way to organize how you write the If / Then rule.

Write or think through the contrapositive as well.

Beware the temptation to make illegal Reversals or Negations.

_Conditional Logic, by question type_

- **Principle** (the left side of the Principle matches the Evidence, the right side matches the Conclusion)

- **Parallel** (diagramming these arguments with "algebraic recipes" allows us to be more picky and decisive when evaluating answers)

- **Assumption** (the conclusion gives us an A --> Z logic path we want to prove, and the evidence + the correct answer choice assemble together to create that A --> Z path / or the evidence gives us a conditional rule that is being used in drawing the conclusion, but we still need an answer choice to tell us something in order to clearly trigger that rule.)

- **Must Be True / Most Supported** (multiple conditionals often chain together, and provided facts often match the contrapositive-trigger of a conditional and allow us to infer the contrapositive-outcome)

- **Flaw / Parallel Flaw** (Flaw + Conditional Logic usually means the **Necessary vs. Sufficient** flaw, which is using a rule in an illegal Backwards or Opposite fashion. Sometimes it involves messing up Or / And stuff)

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 problems featuring conditional logic.

Try them all within 14 minutes (or 21, or 28), then circle back and confirm your thinking (untimed) before confirming your answer and reading the explanation, if available.

# CONDITIONAL KEYWORDS

- Put on **Left** (sufficient conditions)  
  _if, when, any, each, all, every, the only, whenever, whoever, X's_

- Put it on the **Right** (necessary conditions)  
  **only if**, only, must, requires, ensures, will, always, implies, presupposes, depends on, guarantees,

- Put negated version of either idea on left (**If-Not**)  
  **unless**, without, until, except

- **Negative to Positive** conversions  
  No A's are B = All A's are ~B  
  Not all A's are B = Some A's are ~B  
  Few A's are B = Most A's are ~B

The correct answer contradicts something we're told or something that's implied by the paragraph. If we negated every answer choice and turned the question stem into "Which of the following, **must be true**", we'd get the same correct answer.

**Stimulus:** Marsha is taller than her classmate Simon.  
**Correct answer:** Marsha is the shortest person in her class

**Correct answer:** Simon is the most feared person in his class

#### Right Column

# Try this set of problems untimed

When we're doing **Must Be False**, there are two primary things to read for:

Is there a _Conditional_?  
a huge pattern with Must Be False questions is that the paragraph includes a **conditional**, and the correct answer just contradicts that conditional.

Can I make an inference by _Combining Ideas?_  
pretend you're reading a Must Be True (or Most Supported) question. Look for _Conditional, Causal, Mathematical_ wording. See if two or more ideas can be synthesized to derive something. If so, the correct answer will usually contradict that.

## Same broken move, new clothes

This set blends two tasks that lean on the same skill: spotting the **Famous Flaw** in an argument, then finding another argument that breaks the *same* way.

On **Parallel Flaw**, the correct answer matches the structure of the error, not the topic. A stimulus about vitamins and a correct answer about city budgets can be a perfect match if both commit the same Necessary vs. Sufficient slip.

The flaws you will see most here:
- **Necessary vs. Sufficient** (a conditional rule used backwards or negated)
- **Causation** (correlation read as cause)
- **Whole vs. Part** and **Part vs. Whole**
- **Percent vs. Amount**
- **Equivocation**

Name the flaw in abstract terms and the matching gets much easier.

#### Right Column



## Work the set untimed first

For each argument:
- Find the conclusion and the support, then state the broken move as a rule: "the author treats a necessary condition as if it were sufficient."
- On **Parallel Flaw**, carry that abstract sentence to the answers and find the one that commits the identical move. Ignore the subject matter.
- On straight **Flaw**, pick the choice that describes that same move, and watch for traps that name a different famous flaw the argument never made.

Check answers and explanations as you go. When two answers feel close on a Parallel question, the tie-breaker is usually whether the conclusion type matches: a prediction parallels a prediction, a recommendation parallels a recommendation.

## 5. Relative vs. Absolute

### Metadata

```yaml

question_type: Mixed LR question types

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

# Relative / Superlative vs. Absolute

We can say that Bill Gates is poorer than Jeff Bezos, even though we would never say Bill Gates is poor (even after the divorce). We can say that a grasshopper is taller than a ladybug, even though we wouldn't say that a grasshopper is tall.

Adjectives like "poor" and "tall" are in absolute form. Something is poor or not-poor, tall or not-tall. Where does that threshold occur where you go from not-poor to poor, or from not-tall to tall? There's isn't any way to quantify most absolute adjectives, but we still know when they're being abused (such as calling Bill Gates poor or calling a grasshopper tall).

Because a relative adjective like "poorer" can be apt even though an absolute adjective like "poor" isn't (or vice versa), LSAT loves to test the distinction between these two forms. Some plan might be our best option, but still not be a good option. One restaurant might be farther away than another, but still not be far away. Always be cautious when you see that the stimulus/passage has used a word in one form but an answer choice is using it in the other form.

#### Right Column

# Try these 7 problems untimed.

Watch out for **Relative vs. Absolute** distinctions in the argument as well as in answer choices.

There's also a mathematical sense of Relative (fractions / percentages / ratios) vs. Absolute (amount / number). When authors see that a certain percentage is rising, they assume that means that the real number/amount is also rising.

But it's possible for a percentage to rise, even while the real number stays the same or shrinks.

Suppose that last year a certain chess club had 4 boys and 4 girls on it. We can say 50% of the members were boys. Now what _if this year_, we find out that 80% of the members are boys. Does that mean more boys are on the chess club?

Not necessarily, if 3 girls left, and the chess club is now 4 boys and 1 girl, then 80% of the club's members are boys, even though no new boys were added. Percents and fractions are a Relative relationship between numerator and denominator:

_Boy members_  
------------ = % of boys  
_Total members_

The percent of boys can be changed by adding boy members to the numerator or by subtracting non-boy members from the denominator. When authors assume that going from 50% boys to 80% boys means more boys, they're assuming that the denominator is at least as big as before. They're assuming that this percentage increase was _not_ caused by non-boy members leaving.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

When a **Principle-Conform** stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.

If the rule(s) is conditional, then we need to be attentive to conditional logic words, conjunctions such as "and / or", and sometimes even little qualifying adjectives, as we diagram how the rule works.

_a brutally hard example_  
A resignation offered under duress should be accepted only if the person resigning still endorses it one week later or they spoke to HR about it before submitting the resignation.

**only if** = put it on the right

offered under duress  
and  
should be accepted  
→  
still endorsed 1 week later  
or  
spoke to HR before

The way these principles get used, you want the normative, "Judgey" term to be on the right of the arrow (in this case, whether it _should / shouldn't_ be accepted sounds like the judgment this rule would render). So an effective rephrase would be something like this:

resignation offered under duress  
and  
didn't speak to HR before  
and  
not endorsed 1 week later  
→  
should not be accepted

This is not the only form a correct version could take. We could consider leaving off the context-setting idea that we're talking about resignations offered under duress and just focus on whether we should accept it based on whether the person spoke to HR and whether they endorsed it 1 week later.

Often, simplifying down to the core ideas is beneficial. But if we end up with two answers that both seem to work (they're both seeming to let us conclude that we shouldn't accept a resignation), then we need to re-read the principle because we might be missing some of the context-setting qualifiers like "under duress" that we selectively ignored while diagramming.

Perhaps only one of those two remaining answers actually establishes that this resignation was under duress.

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 problems, untimed.

- 5 of them want us to apply 1 or 2 conditional rules.
- 2 of them want us to conform to a fuzzier, non-conditional rule or pattern.
- 1 of them wants us to actually find a situation that **violates** the rule.

Given a principle: _has cinnamon in it → delicious_  
We can only use that rule to conclude that something is delicious. Rules can only prove what's on their **right** side. So if we saw any answer choices that were concluding, "Therefore, the soup was _not_ delicious", we immediately know that answer is wrong.

When we're given two rules, we're only going to be using one or the other to justify our correct answer. Start each answer choice by scanning for the conclusion, so that you can see which rule it's trying to use. That way you can remind yourself of what _that_ rule is all about (ignoring the other one temporarily), and just focus on whether the answer choice clearly establishes the trigger of the rule it's trying to use.

Violating a rule means that you trigger the left side but go against the right side. If we said that "Jonathan had an apple pie that had cinnamon and whip cream in it, but it wasn't that tasty", that would violate the principle above.

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

These 5 problems are all **Principle-Conform** with principles in the answers, taken from tests 50-54. The question stems look something like this.

- Which of the following principles most conforms to the reasoning in the argument?
- Which of the following most accurately expresses the principle underlying the reasoning above?

We often think of this question as the "Necessary Assumption" version of Principle. They're similar in that we would be wary of any answer whose wording seems go beyond what the author said (either in Strength or Scope).

They're different in the sense that on Necessary Assumption the correct answer is only linking together Premise and Conclusion language about 40% of the time, whereas with Principle-Conform, it will be linking Premise and Conclusion language about 90% of the time.

It's essentially the same mindset as a Principle-Strengthen question. We are still pre-phrasing "IF evidence, THEN conclusion" and then expecting our correct answer to _Link_ language from the Evidence to the Conclusion's language. All that's different is that because the question stem says _conform_ to the author's reasoning, we can't pick an answer that's overly strong. (That usually doesn't matter, but occasionally it does)

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 problems, untimed

Once you find the Conclusion and identify the main Supporting Idea(s), you will generally want to articulate an, "If [premise], then [conclusion]" linking idea.

If, otherwise, you felt like the argument was grappling with competing interests, then articulate how the author is weighing tradeoffs: "she cares more about X than about Y".

Try to get rid of a lot answers quickly because they don't have any good matching language for the conclusion, or because the matching language for the conclusion is being put on the **left** of the conditional arrow.

Correct answers will often, if not usually, be written in contrapositive form, so be willing to try that move. If you read "should be on staff only if consistently truthful", then you have to make your brain say, "Okay, this is saying _if you're not consistently truthful, should not on staff_".

## 10. Quantifiers: Trait Overlap inferences

### Metadata

  - Must Be True
  - Flaw
reasoning_type: Quantifiers
question_feature: Trait Overlap Inferences
start_button_label: Continue
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Questions involving multiple quantified words are usually testing our ability to see when two traits do / don't have to overlap, and to what degree.

# COMMON QUANTIFIERS

**100%** - _all, any, each, every_, (or universal nouns)  
**more than 50%** - _most, usually, typically, generally, majority_  
**less than 50%** - _few, rarely, infrequently_  
**at least a handful** - _many, commonly, often_  
**at least one** - _some, sometimes, can, not all, not always, need not_

If we see a conditional-strength quantifier like "No A's are B", we think

A → ~B  
B → ~A

If there are any additional facts about A or B, then we can derive a Trait Overlap inference.

| ADDITIONAL FACT | WE CAN INFER |
|---|---|
| Some X's are B | Some X's are ~A |
| Some B's are X | Some X's are ~A |
| Most B's are X | Some X's are ~A |
| Most X's are B | Most X's are ~A |
| All X's are B | All X's are ~A |
| All B's are X | Some X's are ~A |

_If we know_ Some X's are B  
_and we know_ All B's are ~A  
_we can derive_ Some X's are ~A

_If we know_ Most X's are B  
_and we know_ All B's are ~A  
_we can derive_ Most X's are ~A

_but if we know_ Most B's are X  
_and we know_ All B's are ~A  
_we can only derive_ Some X's are ~A

In the first two examples, we can hook the conditional onto the end of the first statement, and that allows us to "chain" the trigger of the first claim to the outcome of the conditional.

In the third example, the overlapping term is the trigger in both claims, so we can't "chain" the ideas together. But we still know for sure that there are some X's who have the property of being B, and thus they also must have the property of being ~A.

Some quantified claims mean the same thing even when you reverse the terms. "Some dogs are brown = some brown things are dogs". Other quantified terms definitely don't mean the same thing if you reverse the terms. "Most Senators are men ≠ Most men are Senators".

# REVERSIBLE

Some X's are Y = Some Y's are X  
Many X's are Y = Many Y's are X  
No X's are Y = No Y's are X

# NOT REVERSIBLE

Most X's are Y ≠ Most Y's are X  
All X's are Y ≠ All Y's are X

# WE CAN ONLY SAY

Some Y's are X  
Some Y's are X

Sometimes, we can infer something from two **Most** claims.

GIVEN:  
Most A's are B  
+ Most A's are C

Most A's are B  
+ Most B's are ~A

INFER:  
Some B's are C  
There are more B's than A's

Then there's a famous **incorrect** inference from two Most claims:

GIVEN:  
Most A's are B  
+ Most B's are C

INFER:  
nothing (but LSAT authors think, "Most A's are C")

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 questions, untimed.

If the question is testing us on what can be inferred, scan primarily for **conditional** strength quantifiers (_all / each / every / any / no / none_, or anything else that's conditional).

If we find a conditional, then we look to see whether the trigger of that conditional (or the trigger of the contrapositive) shows up in any other claim.

If so, then we can combine those claims to derive **Some** overlap between the two other traits.

For example, if we're told "All cats are sneaky" we have two triggers:

cat → sneaky  
not sneaky → not cat

If we see "cat" or "not sneaky" in any other claim, then we can make a trait overlap inference.

If they tell us "Some cats are smelly", then we know that "smelly and sneaky overlap, at least sometimes".

If they tell us that "Some of Alan's pets are not sneaky", then we know that "Some of Alan's pets are not cats".

If we're being tested on what's Flawed about an argument, then the author is probably going to be assuming that two traits overlap, even though it's possible they never show up together.
