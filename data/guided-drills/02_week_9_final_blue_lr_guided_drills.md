### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

With **abstract** answers on **Flaw** questions, most of them allude to the same 10-15 famous flaws. The other ones feel more like answer choices on **Method** questions.

We need to pay attention to language indicators in each answer choice signifying which half of the answer is meant to describe the Conclusion and which half is meant to describe the Evidence.

Once we've determined that, we can start asking ourselves questions like, "Can I match up this (abstract answer choice) wording with the Conclusion?" If not, eliminate.

If so, then we would try to match up the other half, making sure that we're consistent in terms of matching the same nouns/traits to the same abstract terms in each answer choice.

# Try these ten level 4-5 problems, untimed

When you encounter a wrong answer, challenge yourself to come up with a sample argument of your own that _would_ match that answer choice.

If you're stumped by an answer, thinking, "What _would_ this look like if it were actually occurring?", email a teacher, or ask through Intercom, or bring it up in your next class.

## 13. Science + Sufficient Assumption

### Metadata

```yaml
global_number: 13
week: 9
title: Science + Sufficient Assumption
category: Process
estimated_time: 1 hr
question_count: 6
question_type: Sufficient Assumption
question_feature: Science-heavy Stimulus
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Typically, LSAT arguments that deal with **science** have a messy, real-world feel to them. We're less likely to think we're supposed to be looking at the internal logic of the argument and more likely to think that we're going to be weighing the impact of brand new considerations in the answers.

However, **Sufficient Assumption** is only about the internal logic. So when you combine Sufficient Assumption with Science, you might feel your brain engaging with the story, when our attention needs to stay with the logical features.

If there is a **New Concept in the Conclusion**, we know that idea has to be in the correct answer (and usually will be referred to in verbatim language). If the argument seems pretty internally solid, try to think of a possible objection: "given [this evidence], how could it still be possible that [conclusion is false]?"

If we can think of a possible objection, then we know the correct answer will have to rule out that objection.

#### Right Column

# Try all 6 problems untimed.

Make use of the Notepad popup. Click on the icon in the lower left and move / resize the Notes window so that it covers up your answer choices.

Try to make a specific prediction about what the answer should say / do. Sometimes you might predict an "If Premise, Then Conclusion" link. Sometimes you might think, "it has to rule out _this Objection_". If you can't figure out the whole missing link, at least focus on the wording from the Conclusion that you need to prove.

Once you see the correct answer, revisit your prediction. Do you think you could have anticipated the correct answer if you had read the paragraph differently? Or is this correct answer more one you just have to read and interpret on the fly?

Remember that on Sufficient Assumption, the correct answer, when added to our evidence, allows us to mathematically derive the conclusion.


### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

A common misconception about **EXCEPT** questions is that the correct answer will be the **opposite** of what the other four do. Like if we were doing a Strengthen-EXCEPT question, a lot of times students are looking for "the one that Weakens".

But even though that will be true sometimes, the task is only asking us to pick something that "**doesn't** Strengthen", so the correct answer will either Weaken or (more commonly) will be Neutral with no impact.

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 Except questions, untimed

They're a mix of Strengthen, Weaken, or Paradox, which are the question types where EXCEPT most commonly occurs as a Feature.

These question types are all closely related, because they force us to consider novel ideas and judge what impact, if any, those ideas have. On an EXCEPT variation of these tasks, it's common to get down to two answer choices at which point the one that _seems_ relevant often isn't (it's the correct answer). And the one that _seems_ weirdly different still does its job, but not in the same way the other three eliminated answers did their job.

## 15. Weaken + Explain Curious Fact

### Metadata

```yaml
global_number: 15
week: 9
title: Weaken + Explain Curious Fact
category: Patterns
estimated_time: 50 mins
question_count: 5
question_type: Weaken
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Explain Curious Fact
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

These **Weaken** questions all involve an author presenting a **Curious Fact** and then concluding or assuming a certain causal explanation of that fact.

- The **evidence** presents some _Curious Fact_ (an asymmetric comparison like a correlation, a statistical change, or a difference between two groups or situations)

- The author's **conclusion** will state or assume one possible _Causal Explanation_ for this curious fact.

- The correct answer will either deal with _Alternate Explanations_ for the curious fact or with the _plausibility_ of the Author's Explanation.

#### Right Column

# Try these 5 questions untimed,

while rehearsing these steps:

What is the _Curious Fact?_

- what language tips us off? is it an asymmetric comparison?
- ask yourself the Curious Fact: _why/how is it that [curious fact]?_

What is the _Author's Explanation / Hypothesis?_

- Is there a verb or expression in the conclusion indicating that the author is making a causal claim?

- Make sure that you spell out every step in the author's causal story. It's very common for an author to fail to mention each step in the causal chain that gets them from their causal explanation to the actual curious fact.

Does the correct answer deal with an _Alternate Explanation?_

- Is it dealing with the idea of _Reverse Causality?_

- Is it dealing with the idea of a _Third Factor?_

Or does it address the **plausibility** of the Author's Explanation?

- Is it addressing plausibility via covariation? (strengthening by No Cause / No Effect, or More Cause / More Effect data points or weakening by Cause without Effect, or Effect without Cause data points)

- Or is it addressing plausibility some other way? (placing the suspect at the scene of the crime, or explaining the motive/mechanism by which the crime would have taken place)

```yaml
  2x: 32 mins

reasoning_type: Conditional Logic
question_feature: Conditional Logic / Conditional Keywords
start_button_label: Continue
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

Focus on finding **Conditional Indicator** words and using them as a way to organize how you write or think about the If-Then rule.

Write or think through the contrapositive as well.

Beware illegal Reversals and Negations.

_Conditional Logic, by question type_

- **Principle** (the left side of the Principle matches the Evidence, the right side matches the Conclusion)

- **Parallel** (diagramming these arguments with "algebraic recipes" allows us to be more picky and decisive when evaluating answers)

- **Assumption** (the conclusion gives us an A --> Z logic path we want to prove, and the evidence + the correct answer choice assemble together to create that A --> Z path / or the evidence gives us a conditional rule that is being used in drawing the conclusion, but we still need an answer choice to tell us something in order to clearly trigger that rule.)

- **Must Be True / Most Supported** (multiple conditionals often chain together, and provided facts often match the contrapositive-trigger of a conditional and allow us to infer the contrapositive-outcome)

- **Flaw / Parallel Flaw** (Flaw + Conditional Logic usually means the **Necessary vs. Sufficient** flaw, which is using a rule in an illegal Backwards or Opposite fashion. Sometimes it involves messing up Or / And stuff)

#### Right Column

# Try these 8 questions in 16 minutes

1.5x = 24 mins, 2x = 32 mins

Focus on getting an answer to all of them within the time limit. Then, when you review the set, practice diagramming them by writing more stuff out.

# CONDITIONAL KEYWORDS

- Put on **Left** (sufficient conditions)  
  _if, when, any, each, all, every, the only, whenever, whoever, X's_

- Put it on the **Right** (necessary conditions)  
  **only if**, only, must, requires, ensures, will, always, implies, presupposes, depends on, guarantees,

- Put negated version of either idea on left (**If-Not**)  
  **unless**, without, until, except

- **Negative to Positive** conversions  
  No A's are B = All A's are ~B  
  Not all A's are B = Some A's are ~B  
  Few A's are B = Most A's are ~B

```yaml
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

This is a set of six **Must Be False** questions, taken from tests 45-49.

The correct answer contradicts something we're told or something that's implied by the paragraph. If we negated every answer choice and turned the question stem into "Which of the following, **must be true**", we'd get the same correct answer.

So if it fits your common sense better, you could think of a Must Be False question stem as saying, "Which answer's negation Must Be True (or is Most Supported, if it's a squishier sense of must be false)?"

What does it mean to contradict _something we're told?_

**Stimulus:** Marsha is taller than her classmate Simon.  
**Correct answer:** Marsha is the shortest person in her class

**Stimulus:** Lemonade tastes bitter unless sugar is added.  
**Correct answer:** Marsha's new sugar-free lemonade has the natural zest of lemons without any bitterness to the taste.

What does it mean to contradict _something that's implied?_

**Stimulus:** Marsha is taller than her classmate Simon. Taller students are always more feared than shorter students.  
**Correct answer:** Simon is the most feared person in his class

**Stimulus:** There were more students at Valley High in 2022 than there were in 2021. By law, the school must maintain the same student to teacher ratio every year.  
**Correct answer:** No teachers have been hired or fired from Valley High in the past two years.

#### Right Column

# Try this set of problems untimed

When we're doing **Must Be False**, there are two primary things to read for:

Is there a _Conditional_?  
a huge pattern with Must Be False questions is that the paragraph includes a **conditional**, and the correct answer just contradicts that conditional.

Can I make an inference by _Combining Ideas?_  
pretend you're reading a Must Be True (or Most Supported) question. Look for _Conditional, Causal, Mathematical_ wording. See if two or more ideas can be synthesized to derive something. If so, the correct answer will usually contradict that.

**Theme:** science-heavy stimulus

This question set contains 10 tough questions, all level 3-5, Strengthen / Weaken, with Science-heavy paragraphs. These are the questions that can become quicksand time-suckers. We try to buy time on easier questions so that we have adequate time to think about these.

Ultimately, the gross vernacular of science questions is just a distraction. We're still playing the same old reasoning games, so pay more attention to reasoning patterns and right/wrong answer tendencies for that question type, and pay less attention to getting a thorough understanding of whatever scientific concept is being discussed.

# Choose a study goal

_Untimed:_ Focus on perfect accuracy  
_Timed:_ 15 mins keep-it-moving

For **Weaken** questions, focus on the _Anti-Conclusion_. After you read the argument, frame your goal by saying:

_Given_ this evidence  
_How can I still argue_ the anti-conclusion?

For **Strengthen** questions, remember that when you're dealing with scientific hypotheses, you can strengthen by either _Ruling Out_ an Alternate Hypothesis, or you can _Boost the Plausibility_ of the Author's Hypothesis.

For **Principle-Strengthen**, focus on finding an answer that works like "If _evidence_, then _conclusion_". A lot of answers are easy to eliminate because they aren't rules that are set up to have the conclusion on the right side of the arrow.

```yaml
estimated_time: 30 mins
question_count: 3
reasoning_type: Causation
question_feature: Anti-Causal
start_button_label: Start
```

### Pre-Start Instruction Card

#### Left Column

**Anti-Causal** arguments attempt to argue that "X makes no difference", "X didn't help", or "it's not resulting from X, but Y". Their evidence is usually that things seem to be the same, or worse, without X.

# EXAMPLES

Ann and Jerry both studied LSAT for 3 months, but only Ann added a mindfulness regimen to her studying. Since they both ended up with similar LSAT scores, apparently the mindfulness regimen didn't do anything for Ann.

When President Knowles took office, the unemployment rate was 9%. Shortly after her stimulus bill passed, the unemployment rate hit 10%. Clearly, her stimulus bill did nothing to help employment.

Anti-Causal arguments are vulnerable to several common objections:

- **different initial reference points**  
  the mindfulness _did_ help Ann, because she went from a 140 to a 160, whereas Jerry just went from a 150 to 160.

- **would have been even worse without**  
  the stimulus bill _did_ help, because the economy was in such a free-fall that if it hadn't been for the stimulus bill, the unemployment would have been at like 12%

- **overlooked consideration**  
  even though it looks like the stimulus bill did nothing because unemployment continued to rise in its immediate aftermath, that's because it takes several months before stimulus can manifest any changes in employment figures.

#### Right Column

Try these three questions that have an Anti-Causal vibe to them.

The conclusion will sound like it's saying,  
"So, X doesn't cause Y"

If we're objecting to these arguments, we need to find a way to accept the evidence but still find a way to argue that X _could_ cause Y.
